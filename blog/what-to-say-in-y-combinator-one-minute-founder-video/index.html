What should I say in the one-minute Y Combinator founder video so reviewers keep watching?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Introduce every founder in the first few seconds, say in one plain sentence what you are building and for whom, then spend the rest of the minute on why you, why now and what is already happening. Y Combinator's own instructions are short: the video "should be 1 minute long and should contain nothing except the founders talking", all founders should appear, and you should not recite a script. Treat it as a conversation, not an advert.

Key takeaways

  • YC's application video page asks for one minute of "nothing except the founders talking". It is "not the place to submit a demo or promotional video".
  • If there are several founders, all of them should be in it. If you can't meet in person, YC suggests you "screen record a video call instead".
  • "Do not recite a written script: Use bullet points instead." Talk "as you would to a friend".
  • The video exists to show how the founders communicate, so clarity and energy matter more than lighting or music.
  • The opening line should be your product in one sentence, not a story about how you met.
  • Rehearse the order of speakers, not the words.

What YC actually asks for

YC says it uses the video to see "how the founders communicate". That is the whole brief: can these people explain clearly, and do they work well together?

Why reviewers drift, even in 60 seconds

A minute feels short, but the same rule applies as on any video: the first seconds decide whether the viewer pays full attention. The common ways founders lose it:

  • A slow start. "Hi, so, um, we're really excited to..." spends five seconds saying nothing.
  • Backstory first. Ten seconds on how you met at university before anyone knows what the company does.
  • Jargon. "An AI-native orchestration layer for vertical workflows" makes the viewer work to understand you.
  • Reading. Eyes moving across a screen, flat delivery, and no interaction between founders.
  • One founder talking for 50 seconds while the others sit silently, which says something about the team you didn't mean to say.

Research on lecture videos points the same way. A study of 6.9 million edX video sessions (Guo, Kim and Rubin, 2014) found that viewers engaged more with speakers who talked faster and with more enthusiasm, and the authors recommend extemporaneous speaking rather than reading. That is the same instinct as YC's "bullet points, not a script".

A 60-second structure that holds attention

SecondsWhoWhat to say
0 to 8Founder ANames of everyone on screen, then the product in one sentence: "We're building X for Y."
8 to 20Founder BThe problem, in a concrete example a stranger understands
20 to 35Founder A or CWhy you: the experience, skill or insight that makes your team the right one
35 to 50Founder BProgress so far, stated plainly and accurately (users, revenue, pilots, or what you've built)
50 to 58AnyoneWhy now, or what you'll do next, then stop

Rules for each block:

  • Say the product first. If a reviewer only remembers one sentence, make it the one that says what you do.
  • Use real numbers only. If you have 40 weekly users, say 40. Rounding up or implying traction you don't have is the fastest way to lose trust, and the written application will show the truth anyway.
  • Hand over naturally. A glance or a short "Sam built the first version" makes the handover feel like a team, not a relay race.
  • End on a full stop. Don't fade out with "so, yeah, that's us". Stop after your last strong sentence.

Recording without a script

  1. Write three to five bullets per founder on a card or sticky note below the lens.
  2. Do a timed run-through. If you're over 60 seconds, cut a bullet, don't talk faster.
  3. Record five or six takes back to back. Later takes are usually looser and more natural.
  4. Watch the takes with the sound off for the first five seconds. Do you look engaged, or like you're waiting for permission to start?
  5. Pick the clearest take, not the most polished one.

Basic quality still matters because bad audio is distracting: sit close to the microphone, face a window or lamp, and record somewhere quiet. You don't need a studio.

Example: two founders, one take

Example: two founders of a scheduling tool for dental practices sit side by side at a desk.

SecondsLine (paraphrased bullets, not read)
0 to 7"I'm Priya, this is Tom. We're building booking software that fills last-minute cancellations at dental practices."
7 to 19Tom: a practice he worked at lost several appointments a week to late cancellations, and staff phoned patients one by one to fill them.
19 to 33Priya: she ran front desk systems for a dental group; Tom wrote the practice's scheduling integrations.

Names, practices and numbers are illustrative.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Adding music, titles, product screenshots or a logo sting.
  • Recording separately and stitching clips together when you could sit together or use a video call.
  • Reading from a teleprompter or a second screen.
  • Spending more than a sentence on the founding story.
  • Claiming results you can't support.
