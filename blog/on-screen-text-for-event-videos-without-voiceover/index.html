How do you write on-screen text for an event video without voiceover?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Write on-screen text like headlines: three to seven words, one idea per screen, held long enough to read twice, and placed where platform buttons won't cover it. Use text for four jobs only: the hook, the promise, short proof points and the call to action. Let the footage carry the rest. This lets an event video work without voiceover and with the sound off, which suits feeds where many people watch silently.

Key takeaways

  • Canon's recap guide suggests explanatory text instead of voiceover, which it calls "clichéd".
  • Google's ABCDs advise reinforcing the message "with audio and text" and avoiding "competing elements".
  • TikTok recommends adding captions or text overlays for context and keeping content inside the UI safe zone.
  • On-screen text is not the same as captions: captions transcribe speech; text carries your message.
  • Fewer, bigger words beat more, smaller words every time on a phone.

Why text instead of voiceover

Canon's five steps for recap videos recommends adding explanatory text rather than voiceover, which it calls "clichéd". Text also works for viewers watching without sound and lets the room's real sound and music carry the mood.

The four jobs of on-screen text

JobWhenExampleLength
HookFirst 1-3 seconds"The talk everyone's still quoting"3-6 words
Promise3-8 seconds"3 ideas from Leeds Growth Day"4-7 words
ProofThrough the body"600 founders. 2 days. 40 speakers."Short facts, all true
Call to actionLast 3-5 seconds"Tickets for 2027: link below"3-6 words

The Retention Editing Playbook's cold open rule, start on the most interesting moment, applies to text too: the first line should be the most compelling sentence you have, not the event's full name and date.

Writing rules

  • One idea per screen. If you need "and", you probably need two screens.
  • Concrete over clever. "40 speakers" beats "Unforgettable voices".
  • Active and present tense. "Founders swap notes" not "Notes were swapped".
  • True numbers only. Every figure on screen is a claim; make sure you can back it up.
  • Name, don't describe. "Keynote: Dr Sara Ahmed" is clearer than "An amazing keynote speaker".
  • Match the footage. The text should label or add to what's on screen, not describe something else.

Google's ABCDs of effective video ads advise supporting the story "with audio and supers" (on-screen text) and to "Avoid competing elements." Don't put text over a speaker's face or over busy movement.

Design so it can be read

  • Size: big enough to read on a phone at arm's length.
  • Contrast: light text on dark footage or a subtle shadow or bar; Google's ABCDs also say to keep visuals "bright and high-contrast".
  • Position: inside the safe zone. TikTok's creative best practices say to keep content visible within the UI safe zone, away from buttons and captions at the bottom and right.
  • Timing: hold each line long enough to read twice. Short lines can be quick; longer ones need more time.
  • Consistency: one font family, one or two sizes, the same position for the same kind of line.

On-screen text vs captions

Captions transcribe what people say. The W3C's captions guidance defines them as "a text version of the speech and non-speech audio information needed to understand the content". If your video has interviews or speaker clips, caption them as well as adding your headline text, and keep the two visually distinct so they don't compete.

Example: a regional business awards night.

  • 0:03: "Northern Business Awards 2026"
  • 0:08 (packed room): "450 guests. 14 awards."
  • 0:15 (finalists on stage): "Smallest winner: a 3-person bakery"
  • 0:22 (captioned interview clip): captions of the bakery owner's 6-second line.
  • 0:30 (dancing): "And then the night really started."
  • 0:40: "Entries for 2027 open 1 March"
  • 0:43: "Link in bio"

Eight lines of text, all true, none longer than seven words. Music and real crowd sound carry the mood.

Quick checklist before export

  • Read every line aloud. Is any line longer than seven words?
  • Check every number and name on screen is accurate.
  • Watch on a phone at arm's length with the sound off.
  • Make sure no line sits under platform buttons or captions.
  • Check text never covers a face or the main action.
  • Hold each line long enough to read twice.
  • Keep the same font, size and position for the same kind of line.
  • Make sure the call to action is the last thing on screen and easy to act on.

Common mistakes

  • Opening with the event's full name and date instead of a hook.
  • Writing full sentences that need more time to read than the shot lasts.
  • Placing text at the very bottom where captions and buttons cover it.
  • Using thin or decorative fonts that blur on a phone.
  • Adding text to every shot, so nothing stands out.
  • Mixing too many colours and styles.
  • Letting text and captions overlap.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches hooks and cold opens you can use for text-led video?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches cold opens, intro killers and pacing. Canon's free recap guide is a fair alternative.

How many words should be on screen at once?

Usually three to seven. If it needs more, split it across two screens.

How long should text stay on screen?

Long enough to read it twice at a normal pace.

Should I use the event's brand font?

Yes, if it's readable at small sizes. Otherwise use a clean, bold font and keep the brand in colour and logo.

Is on-screen text the same as subtitles?

No. Subtitles or captions transcribe speech; on-screen text carries your own message.

Can text replace voiceover completely?

For most recaps and promos, yes. For complex explanations, a short voice line can help.
