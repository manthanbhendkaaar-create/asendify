Who owns the copyright when you hire a video editor?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 14 November 2026 · Last updated 14 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

In the UK, if you hire a freelance video editor, the editor usually owns the copyright in their creative work unless you agree otherwise in writing, and a transfer (assignment) of copyright only works if it is in writing and signed by the editor. If the editor is your employee working in the course of their job, you as employer normally own it. The simplest fix is a short contract signed before work begins that says who owns what.

Key takeaways

  • Freelancer (contract for services): usually keeps copyright unless a written contract says otherwise.
  • Employee (contract of employment): the employer is usually the first owner.
  • Copyright assignment "is not effective unless it is in writing signed by or on behalf of the assignor", under section 90(3) of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988.
  • If you do not take ownership, you can still get a licence: a written permission to use the work in agreed ways.
  • Your raw footage stays yours; the question is the edit, graphics, music choices and project files the editor creates.

This page is general information about UK law, not legal advice. For valuable or long-term deals, ask a solicitor to check your contract.

What UK guidance says

The government's guidance on ownership of copyright works explains that for commissioned work, the creator is usually the first owner, not the person who paid, "unless you otherwise agree it in writing". Someone working under a "contract for services", like most freelancers, usually keeps copyright unless a contract says otherwise. An employee's work made in the course of employment belongs to the employer, subject to any agreement to the contrary.

The Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, section 90(3), sets the formal rule: "An assignment of copyright is not effective unless it is in writing signed by or on behalf of the assignor." A Slack message saying "it's all yours" is not enough to transfer ownership.

The government's quick guide to protecting creative work adds that copyright is automatic in the UK with no registration, that creators can license work while keeping ownership, and that it is "best to agree ownership and any licensing arrangements in writing before work begins". It also notes moral rights, including the right to be identified as author where asserted in writing and to object to derogatory treatment.

For a YouTube channel you might one day sell or license, assignment is usually the cleanest choice, because buyers will want to know the channel owns its videos.

A simple contract clause checklist

  1. Ownership: the editor assigns to you all copyright in the deliverables on payment, signed by the editor.
  2. Moral rights: the editor agrees not to assert moral rights, or agrees how credit will be given.
  3. Third-party material: the editor lists any stock music, footage or fonts used and confirms the licences cover YouTube use.
  4. Portfolio use: whether the editor can show the work in their portfolio.
  5. Files: whether project files and assets are delivered.
  6. Payment: when ownership transfers (often on full payment).

FAQ

Do I own a video if I paid someone to edit it?

Not automatically in the UK. Paying for a freelance edit does not transfer copyright unless there is a written, signed assignment. You usually still have at least an implied right to use it as intended, but that is less certain than owning it.

Is an email enough to transfer copyright?

The law requires an assignment to be in writing signed by or on behalf of the person giving up the rights. Use a signed document to be safe.

Can my editor post my video in their portfolio?

Only if your agreement allows it, or if they still own copyright and nothing in your agreement stops it. Decide this upfront.

What about agencies?

The same rules apply. An agency's staff editors' work usually belongs to the agency as employer, so your contract with the agency should assign or license the work to you.

Does copyright need to be registered in the UK?

No. Copyright is automatic in the UK when the work is created.
