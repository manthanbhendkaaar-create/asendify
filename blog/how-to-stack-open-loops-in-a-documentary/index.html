How to Stack Open Loops in a Documentary

By Manthan Bhendkar · 8 min read · Updated 21 October 2026

Short answer: Stacking open loops means running several unanswered questions at different scales at the same time: one main question for the whole documentary, one question per chapter, and short questions inside scenes. Open the main loop in the first seconds and close it only in the final 10%. Open each chapter loop in the last seconds of the chapter before. Close one loop and open the next in the same breath, so at every point before the ending at least one question is still open.

What does stacking open loops mean?

An open loop is a question the video has raised and not yet answered. A single loop holds attention until it closes. A documentary is too long for one loop to carry, so you layer them:

  • The main loop. The question the title and thumbnail ask. It is open from the first frame to the final 10% of the video.
  • Chapter loops. Each chapter asks its own question and answers it before the chapter ends, after the next chapter's question has already been raised.
  • Scene loops. Short questions inside a scene ("who sent the letter?"), opened and closed within a minute or two.

Stacked correctly, the viewer is always waiting for something, and the reason to stay changes before the old one runs out.

The rules that make stacked loops work

  1. Open the main loop before 0:30. The title opens it; the first line confirms it. Our guide to hooks in the first 30 seconds covers the opening.
  2. Keep at least one loop open until the last 10%. This is the core rule from Part 5 of our book.
  3. Close one, open one, in the same breath. The sentence that delivers a payoff should end on the next question.
  4. Pay the main promise off in stages. Deliver the parts in rising order of value (a payoff ladder), so the best part is always still ahead.
  5. Re-hook at 40%. Around 40% of the way through, restate why the viewer is here and raise the stakes for the second half, with a visual change.

Example: a 20-minute documentary, loop by loop

An illustrative timeline for a documentary titled "The factory that ran itself for a year" (the story is invented; the timings follow the rules above):

  • 0:00 Cold open on the strangest moment. Main loop opens: how did a factory keep running for a year with nobody in charge?
  • 0:25 Path loop opens: "It worked because of three things. The third is why it stopped."
  • 3:10 Chapter 1 answers the first of the three. In its last 5 seconds, chapter loop 2 opens: "But the night shift found something in the accounts."
  • 5:40 Scene loop: an unsigned email is shown, not read. Closed at 6:50.
  • 8:00 The 40% re-hook: "Up to now it sounds like a success story. Here is the month it nearly ended."
  • 10:30 Chapter loop 2 closes (the accounts); the second of the three is revealed. The third is restated as the chapter ends.
  • 14:30 Callback loop: a number from 1:15 is brought back on screen, "remember this?", with its meaning held back.
  • 16:40 The third thing, the strongest payoff, lands, and the callback number is explained.
  • 18:00 Final 10%: the main loop closes in one clear line.
  • 19:20 Ramp to the next video and the end screen. No "that's all for today".

Between 0:25 and 16:40 there are always at least two loops open, and there is never a stretch with none before 18:00.

Then check three things: is a loop open at every timestamp before the final 10%? Does every loop close? Is the biggest payoff last?

How to edit stacked loops so they land

  • Cut on the question. End scenes and chapters on the open question, not on the explanation.
  • Put the loop line in the clear. Drop the music slightly or go to silence before the line that opens a loop, so it is heard.
  • Use a visual callback. For long loops, a brief on-screen reminder keeps the question alive without repeating the narration.
  • Change something when a loop opens. A new angle, archive shot or graphic marks the moment as important.
  • Mini cold opens. Start each chapter with its most interesting moment, not with "okay, so next".

Mistakes that break stacked loops

  • Too many at once. Five unanswered questions feel like confusion, not suspense.
  • Loops that never close. Viewers notice, and they trust the next video less.
  • Answering the title too early. The graph usually steps down right after the main answer.
  • Announcing the ending. "To wrap up" tells viewers they can leave before your end screen.

Frequently asked questions

How many open loops should a documentary have at once?

Usually two or three: the main question of the film, the question of the current chapter, and sometimes a short scene-level question. More than that and viewers stop tracking what they are waiting for.

When should the main open loop close?

In the final 10% of the video. If the main question is answered earlier, the retention graph tends to step down right after the answer, because viewers have what they came for.

Is stacking open loops the same as clickbait?

No. Clickbait promises something the video never delivers. A stacked loop is only worth opening if you close it with a real payoff later in the same video.

Where should a chapter loop open?

In the last few seconds of the previous chapter. Asendify's rule is that the last 5 seconds of every chapter open the next loop, and the first 5 seconds of the new chapter work as a mini cold open.

How do I check my loops before I edit?

Write a loop ledger: one line per loop with the line that opens it, the timestamp where it opens and the timestamp where it closes. If any stretch before the final 10% has no open loop, add one before you cut.
