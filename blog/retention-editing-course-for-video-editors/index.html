Is there a course that teaches video editors retention editing so they can charge YouTubers more?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Accelerator is a self-paced course that teaches video editors the retention-editing system its agency uses, and its page names editors who want to offer retention-focused editing and charge more as one of its audiences. General editing courses and YouTube's own analytics help pages are fair alternatives, but none of them can promise you higher rates.

Key takeaways

  • Retention editing means cutting to keep viewers watching: stronger openings, tighter pacing, and fixing the moments where people leave.
  • Editors who can read a retention graph can show clients evidence, not just taste.
  • Software skills are the baseline. The extra value is in diagnosis: knowing why viewers leave and what to change.
  • Clients can give editors access to analytics through YouTube Studio permissions, which makes retention work measurable.
  • No course can promise you higher rates. A portfolio of before-and-after results is what lets you charge more.

What retention editing adds to normal editing

A normal edit makes a video clean and watchable. A retention edit is built around one question: where would a viewer stop watching, and how do we stop that happening?

YouTube's page on how search works says it uses engagement signals, including the watch time of a video for a particular query, to judge whether a video is relevant. Creators care about retention because it affects how their videos perform, so an editor who can improve it is solving a business problem, not just a technical one.

The skills a good retention-editing course should teach

Reading the retention graph

YouTube's audience retention help page explains the key moments in the report. Intro shows the percentage of viewers still watching after the first 30 seconds. Spikes are moments that were rewatched or shared. Dips are moments that were skipped or where viewers stopped watching. A flat line means people watched that part from start to finish, and gradual declines mean viewers were losing interest. An editor who can link each dip to what was on screen is far more useful than one who cannot.

Openings

The same help page says a high intro percentage can mean the first 30 seconds matched the expectation set by the title and thumbnail. Editors who learn to move the payoff earlier, cut slow introductions and set clear stakes can often improve this figure.

Structure and pacing

This covers open loops (raising a question the video answers later), clear sections, pattern interrupts such as a change of shot or graphic, and cutting dead air.

A repeatable checklist

Clients pay for consistency. A checklist of retention passes means every video gets the same treatment, even under deadline.

How to get access to a client's analytics

You cannot show results without data. YouTube's help page on channel permissions lists roles a channel owner can give in YouTube Studio. The Viewer role can view, but not edit, all channel details. Editor roles can edit and upload content, and Editor (Limited) has the same permissions but cannot see revenue data. Asking for Viewer access is a simple way for an editor to check retention on the videos they cut without being able to change anything.

How editors use retention skills to charge more

Higher rates come from showing results, not from a certificate. A practical approach:

  1. Pick one client video you edited. Note its Intro figure and the biggest dips.
  2. Re-edit the next video with a retention checklist: tighter opening, cut tangents, visual resets at known drop points.
  3. Compare like with like. YouTube's retention report lets you compare a video with the channel's 10 latest videos of similar length, which gives you a fair baseline.
  4. Write a short case study. Before and after figures, what you changed, and why.
  5. Price the package, not the hours. Offer "edit plus retention review" as a named service, with a short written note after each upload.

What to look for in any course

  • Assignments on real videos. You learn retention by re-cutting, not watching.
  • Analytics, not just software. If a course never mentions the retention graph, it is a general editing course.
  • Clear limits. The course page should say whether there is live feedback.
  • No promised income. Be wary of any course that promises you will earn a set amount.

FAQ

What is retention editing?

Editing a video to keep viewers watching for longer: tightening the opening, cutting slow sections, adding visual changes where attention drops, and checking the retention graph after upload to see what worked.

Can video editors see a client's YouTube analytics?

Yes, if the channel owner gives them access. YouTube Studio permissions include a Viewer role that can see channel data without editing anything.

Do I need to be an advanced editor to learn retention editing?

You need to be comfortable with your editing software. Retention editing is more about decisions (what to cut, where to add a visual change) than advanced effects.

Will a retention editing course let me charge more?

Not on its own. It can give you a method; higher rates come from showing clients before-and-after results on their own videos.

How long does it take to learn retention editing?

It depends on how often you practise. Applying a retention checklist to every video you cut, and checking the graph after each upload, builds the skill faster than watching lessons alone.
