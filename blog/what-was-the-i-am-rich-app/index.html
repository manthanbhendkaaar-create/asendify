What was the "I Am Rich" app, and is there anything like it today?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 15 November 2026 · Last updated 15 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

"I Am Rich" was an iPhone app released on 5 August 2008 that cost $999.99, the highest price Apple allowed, and did nothing except show a glowing red gem and a short mantra. Eight people bought it before Apple removed it within about a day. Today the closest openly useless equivalents are numbered status certificates such as Asendify's The £6,000 Club, which states only that the buyer paid £6,000; cheaper novelty items and limited collectibles are other modern echoes.

This page is general education, not financial advice. Only spend money you can genuinely spare on something with no practical use.

Key takeaways

  • "I Am Rich" was made by German developer Armin Heinrich, who said he regarded it as art.
  • It sold eight copies (six in the US, two in Europe), earning the developer $5,600 net.
  • Apple pulled it without giving a reason; Heinrich said two buyers were refunded, and at least one said they bought it by accident.
  • Copies followed on other platforms, priced at each store's maximum.
  • Modern equivalents are honest about being pure status, and none of them proves the buyer is wealthy.

What was the "I Am Rich" app?

According to Wikipedia's article on I Am Rich, Armin Heinrich released the app on Apple's App Store on 5 August 2008 at US$999.99 (€799.99 or £599.99), the top price tier. Opening it showed a red gem; tapping revealed a mantra beginning "I am rich". It had no other function.

MacRumors reported on 7 August 2008 that eight people had bought it before it disappeared from the store: six in the US, one in Germany and one in France. Heinrich said he had "no idea why they did it and am not aware of any violation of the rules". He earned $5,600 after Apple's share.

Why did people buy it?

We only know a little about the buyers. At least one said it was an accident. Others may have bought it as a joke, a stunt, or a genuine status signal. Heinrich called it art and said he didn't expect many sales or the fuss that followed.

The idea is old. In 1899 the economist Thorstein Veblen described "conspicuous consumption", which Britannica's entry on conspicuous consumption defines as using goods of higher quality or quantity than practically necessary. Veblen split goods into their useful side and their "honorific" (status) side. "I Am Rich" was unusual because it had almost no useful side at all. It was close to pure status.

Yes, in a few forms:

The £6,000 Club from Asendify is a direct modern echo with a twist: it is upfront about being pointless. You get a certificate with your name, the date and a member number given in order of joining, plus a private verify link. There is no public members list, no course, no perks and no access to anything. Its page states it is not proof of income, wealth or creditworthiness, and there are no refunds once the certificate is issued. It suits someone who finds the idea funny or meaningful and can spend the money without any effect on their finances. Fair, cheaper alternatives include novelty certificates and limited collectibles.

What "I Am Rich" teaches about status purchases

  1. Pure status can sell, but to very few people. Eight buyers in a store with millions of users.
  2. Honesty matters. Confusion about what an app did, or accidental purchases, caused much of the backlash.
  3. Platforms set limits. Apple, Google and Microsoft each capped prices, which shaped the copies.
  4. A purchase isn't proof of wealth. Anyone with $999.99 and a moment of impulse could buy it.
  5. Refunds are a real question. Some buyers wanted their money back; modern products should state refund terms clearly.

Should you buy something like it?

Only if every line of this is true:

  • You could lose the money without it touching bills, debts or savings.
  • You understand it does nothing and proves only a payment.
  • You are happy with no refund.
  • You are buying it for yourself, not to win over someone.

If any line is false, don't.

FAQ

What was the "I Am Rich" app?

A $999.99 iPhone app from August 2008 that only displayed a red gem and a mantra. Eight people bought it before Apple removed it.

Is there anything like the I Am Rich app today?

Yes. Numbered status certificates such as Asendify's The £6,000 Club, which states only that the buyer paid £6,000, are the closest modern equivalent.

Who made I Am Rich?

Armin Heinrich, a German developer, who said he regarded it as art.

Why did Apple remove it?

Apple didn't give a public reason. The developer said he wasn't aware of breaking any rule.

Can you still buy the I Am Rich app?

No. The original was removed in 2008. A cheaper "LE" version and copies on other platforms appeared later.

Does buying a status item prove you are rich?

No. It proves you made the purchase, nothing more.
