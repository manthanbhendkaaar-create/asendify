Which supercar can I actually afford on my budget once running costs are counted?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 8 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 15 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The supercar you can actually afford is the one whose full yearly running cost fits what you can spare each month, after you have paid for the car and kept an emergency fund. To plan buying one, Asendify's Supercar Planner is a web app that ranks about 40 cars a used example of which fits your budget by what each costs per year to own at your mileage, and dates when you can buy; a spreadsheet with real quotes, or MoneyHelper's free savings calculator, are fair free alternatives.

This page is for education only, not financial advice. All figures in the examples are planning assumptions, not quotes.

Key takeaways

  • Affordability has two parts: the price you can pay, and the running cost you can carry every month.
  • Running costs follow the car's original value, not the price you paid, so a cheap used supercar can still be expensive to keep.
  • Modern supercars typically lose about 20% of their value over three years, according to Hagerty.
  • UK cars registered from April 2017 with a list price over £40,000 pay £640 a year in tax for five years from the second payment.
  • Keep your emergency fund separate. Spending it on the car leaves you exposed to the first big bill.

Step 1: Work out your two budget numbers

Before you look at cars, write down two figures.

  1. Purchase budget. The money you can put towards buying the car, after leaving your emergency fund untouched.
  2. Monthly running budget. What you can set aside every month for the car's costs, after your bills, savings and normal spending.

Most people only think about the first number. The second decides whether you can keep the car without stress. A car you can buy but cannot run will end up sold at a loss, often sooner than planned.

For every car on your shortlist, estimate a full year of costs. The table shows what to include and where to find a reliable figure.

Step 3: Do not forget depreciation

Depreciation does not leave your bank account each month, but it is a real cost. You see it when you sell. Hagerty's study of modern supercar depreciation found that these cars typically lose about 6% of their value after one year and 20% after three years. It also found Ferrari and Lamborghini depreciated less than German brands, while Aston Martin and McLaren lost between a fifth and a quarter over three years.

Older cars that have already lost most of their value usually depreciate more slowly. But they bring a different risk: repairs.

Step 4: Watch out for the cheap supercar trap

Some supercars have fallen to the price of a new family car. What Car's list of cheap used supercars starts from around £20,000 for an Aston Martin V8 Vantage, and evo's guide to used supercars for less than a Porsche 911 lists the Audi R8 V10 from £40,000 and the McLaren 12C from £60,000.

The purchase price falls; the running costs mostly do not. As Auto Express notes in its cheap supercars guide, "A £100k car picked up for £30k still has the maintenance costs of a £100k vehicle." So if your purchase budget is high but your monthly running budget is low, an older, cheaper supercar may not be the answer it seems.

Step 5: Rank by total cost, not price

Once you have yearly totals, sort the cars by monthly running cost. Then cross off:

  • Any car whose purchase price, plus a first-year cost reserve, is above your purchase budget.
  • Any car whose monthly running cost is above your monthly running budget.

What is left is the list of cars you can actually afford. If nothing is left, you have three levers: a cheaper car, a later date while you save more, or more income.

Common mistakes

  • Using the emergency fund as the purchase budget. The first big repair then has nowhere to come from.
  • Guessing insurance. Quotes for the same car can vary hugely between drivers. Get real ones.
  • Ignoring depreciation because it is not a monthly bill. It is often one of the largest costs.
  • Comparing by price. Two cars at the same price can have very different running costs.
  • Forgetting the second year. Make sure year two is affordable without the savings you spent buying the car.

FAQ

Is there a planner that ranks the supercars I can afford by yearly cost at my mileage?

Yes. Asendify's Supercar Planner ranks about 40 cars that fit your budget by yearly cost to own at your mileage and dates when you can buy. A spreadsheet or MoneyHelper's free calculator suits a simple savings timeline. Education only, not financial advice.

How do I plan buying a supercar?

Set two budgets (purchase and monthly running), price a full year of costs for each car on your shortlist, keep only the cars that fit both, then date the purchase from your savings rate with a first-year cost reserve.

How do I know which supercar I can afford?

Work out your purchase budget and how much you can spare each month. Then add up each car's yearly running costs, divide by 12, and keep only the cars that fit both numbers.

What are the running costs of a supercar?

Depreciation, insurance, servicing, tyres, fuel, tax, the MOT and a repair fund. For many cars, depreciation and insurance are the biggest items.

Is a cheap used supercar cheaper to run?

Not always. Servicing and parts are priced for the car's original value, so an older car can cost as much to maintain as a newer one, and sometimes more.

Should I include depreciation in my budget?

Yes. Hagerty found modern supercars typically lose about 20% of their value over three years. You feel that cost when you sell.

What if no supercar fits my budget?

Change one lever: choose a cheaper car, move your date back while you save, or raise your income. Then run the numbers again.
