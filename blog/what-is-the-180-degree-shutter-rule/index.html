What is the 180-degree shutter rule for YouTube videos?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 21 November 2026 · Last updated 21 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The 180-degree shutter rule says to set your shutter speed to roughly double your frame rate, for example 1/50 second at 25fps, 1/60 at 30fps or 1/48 (or the nearest, 1/50) at 24fps. That gives motion blur that looks natural to most viewers. Faster shutter speeds make movement look choppy and stuttered; slower ones make it smeared. In bright light you'll usually need an ND filter to keep the rule without overexposing.

Key takeaways

  • RED Digital Cinema: a shutter angle near 180° equals "a shutter speed near 1/48 of a second at 24 fps."
  • StudioBinder: shutter angle = frame rate × 360 ÷ shutter speed; at 48fps you'd use 1/96 to keep 180°.
  • Above 180°, "motion appears more smeared"; below, motion looks more stuttered and disjointed, RED explains.
  • Filmmakers break the rule on purpose: StudioBinder cites Saving Private Ryan for choppy, small-angle action.
  • Sony describes an ND filter as acting "like a pair of sunglasses" to cut light so you can keep a slower shutter outdoors.

Where the name comes from

Old film cameras used a spinning disc, a rotary shutter, with an opening that exposed each frame. A 180° opening exposed the film for half of each frame's time. RED's guide to shutter angles explains that although modern cameras don't work this way, the term is still "a useful way of describing the shutter speed relative to the frame rate."

StudioBinder's guide gives the formula: shutter angle = frame rate × 360 ÷ shutter speed. So if you shoot 48fps for slow motion, 1/96 keeps a 180° angle. Some cameras let you set shutter angle directly, so the speed adjusts automatically when you change frame rate. Our page on what frame rate to use for YouTube helps you pick the frame rate first.

What happens if you break it

ShutterLookTypical use

About double (180°)Natural motion blurMost YouTube videos, vlogs, interviews

RED notes that angles well below 180° resemble old newsreels, while 180° gives the standard cinematic look, and that a wider angle can help in low light by increasing exposure time and reducing noise. StudioBinder notes many filmmakers agree 180° "represents motion the most seamlessly."

Why it matters for YouTube

Phones and cameras on auto exposure can pick very fast shutter speeds in daylight to control exposure. That's why bright outdoor footage can look oddly crisp and jittery when the camera or subject moves, especially in B-roll and walking shots. Choppy motion isn't a disaster, but natural motion makes footage feel more polished and easier to watch, which supports everything else you do to keep viewers engaged.

Matching shots in one video

Whatever shutter you choose, keep it consistent within a video. Cutting from a smooth, naturally blurred talking-head shot to choppy, ultra-sharp B-roll is noticeable even to viewers who couldn't name the cause. If you film on two cameras, or mix phone and camera footage, set both to the same frame rate and a matching shutter where you can, so the edit feels like one piece rather than a collage.

The daylight problem and ND filters

At 1/50 in bright sun, a lot of light reaches the sensor. If you can't stop down the aperture or lower ISO enough, the image overexposes. The fix is a neutral density filter. Sony's filmmaking tips describe an ND filter as acting "like a pair of sunglasses to reduce the amount of light falling on the sensor", which lets you keep the 180° shutter outdoors. Variable ND filters let you adjust the strength with a twist.

SituationSuggested approach
Indoors, normal light180° shutter, adjust ISO and aperture
Outdoors, bright sun180° shutter plus ND filter
Phone footageUse a manual video app or "pro" mode to lock shutter, plus a clip-on ND if available
Low lightConsider a slightly wider angle for more light, accepting more blur

Lighting can also cause flicker at some shutter speeds; if you see banding under artificial lights, try a shutter speed that matches the local mains frequency, commonly 1/50 in 50Hz countries.

What shutter speed should I use for 30fps?

About 1/60, double the frame rate.

What shutter speed for 24fps?

1/48, or 1/50 if your camera doesn't offer 1/48.

Do I have to follow the 180-degree rule?

No. It's a starting point for natural motion. Break it on purpose for a choppy or dreamy look.

Why is my outdoor footage overexposed at 1/50?

Too much light. Use an ND filter, a smaller aperture or a lower ISO.

Does the rule apply to phones?

Yes, if you can lock the shutter in a manual or pro video mode.
