What is Dan Harmon's story circle, and does it work for YouTube videos?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Dan Harmon's story circle is an eight-step story structure: You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change. A character in a comfort zone wants something, enters an unfamiliar situation, adapts, gets what they wanted, pays a price, returns and is changed. Harmon, the writer behind Community and Rick and Morty, simplified it from Joseph Campbell's Hero's Journey for episodic TV. It works well for YouTube because it gives every section of a video a job and builds towards a payoff, which keeps viewers watching.

Key takeaways

  • StudioBinder lists the eight steps as You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change, and describes the circle as a simplified version of Campbell's Hero's Journey.
  • Final Draft explains Harmon built it as an easy template for episodic TV, adapting the Hero's Journey as later modernised by Chris Vogler.
  • The structure creates an open question (will they get what they need?) early and answers it late, which suits retention.
  • The "Take" step, the price paid, is the twist that stops the middle of a video sagging.
  • Check whether it works by watching your retention graph for flat lines through the middle.

The eight steps

StepStory meaningYouTube example (a creator trying a 30-day challenge)
1. YouA character in a familiar situation"I've never run more than 5k."
2. NeedThey want something"I want to run a half marathon in 30 days."
3. GoThey enter an unfamiliar situationDay 1 of a training plan
4. SearchThey adapt, struggle and learnWeek 1 and 2: blisters, slow times, small wins
5. FindThey get what they wantedRace day: they finish
6. TakeThey pay a priceA knee injury, or missing family time
7. ReturnThey go back to normal lifeRecovery week
8. ChangeThey are different nowWhat they learned, and what they would do differently

Final Draft notes that at step 5 the win often "feels incomplete or questionable", and step 6 costs the character "something significant". That tension is what makes the second half of a story worth watching.

Why it suits YouTube retention

It opens a question early

Step 2 (Need) sets up the question the whole video answers. Viewers stay to find out. This is the same mechanism as an open loop.

It gives the middle a direction

Many videos lose people in the middle. In the circle, the middle (Search) is a series of attempts and setbacks heading somewhere. Each setback is a natural re-hook.

It has a twist built in

Getting what you wanted, then paying for it (Find, Take), adds a turn after the apparent climax. That stops viewers leaving the moment they get the answer.

It ends on change, not "thanks for watching"

Step 8 is the payoff: what changed. It gives the ending a reason to exist.

How to use it without making videos formulaic

  • Start at step 5 or 6. Open with the result or the price ("I finished the half marathon. It cost me my knee."), then go back to step 1. This puts the hook first.
  • Compress steps 1 to 3. On YouTube, the set-up should be fast. One or two sentences for "You" and "Need".
  • Spend most time in Search. That is where the content is: tests, attempts, lessons.
  • Use it for non-story videos too. A product review can follow it: the problem (You, Need), testing the product (Go, Search), it works (Find), but there is a catch (Take), back to daily use (Return), the verdict (Change).

How to check if it worked

YouTube Studio's retention graph shows where viewers leave. YouTube's key moments help page says a flat line means viewers are watching that part from start to finish, while dips mean they are abandoning or skipping. If your Search section shows a long slow decline, it needs more setbacks or progress. If viewers leave right after Find, your Take is too weak or too late. Example: planning a 12-minute review with the circle

A creator is reviewing a budget 3D printer.

TimeStepContent
0:00Find/Take as hookShow a perfect print, then a failed one: "It made this. Then it did this."
0:15You, Need"I wanted a printer under £200 that just works."
0:40GoUnboxing and setup, cut to 60 seconds
1:40-7:00SearchFive test prints, each with a problem and a fix
7:00FindThe best print it made
8:00TakeThe failure that keeps coming back
9:30ReturnA week of normal use
11:00ChangeVerdict: who should buy it, who should not

Each Search test ends with a small question ("will the fix hold on a bigger print?"), so the middle keeps moving. The creator then compares the middle of the retention graph with their typical retention.

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops, re-hooks and pattern interrupts, with a Bonus Pack including a 30-day plan. Free guides to the story circle are a good companion.

Is the story circle the same as the Hero's Journey?

It is a simplified version. The Hero's Journey has more stages; Harmon cut it to eight for TV episodes.

Does the story circle work for Shorts?

A compressed version can: need, attempt, result, twist. Most Shorts only have time for three or four beats.

Do I have to follow the steps in order?

No. Many YouTube videos open with the result and then tell the story, which keeps the hook first.

Does it work for tutorials?

Partly. Tutorials suit clear steps more than story, but a problem, a failed attempt and a fix can still follow the circle.
