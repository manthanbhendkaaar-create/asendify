How do I write revision notes for an editor using timestamps from the retention graph?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 25 November 2026 · Last updated 25 November 2026

Write each note as a timestamp, what the graph shows there, the likely cause and one change you want. Put all notes in one list tied to the timeline, rank them by size of drop, and ask for a re-check on the graph after publishing. Notes tied to a moment are easier to act on than general comments.

Key takeaways

  • The Frame.io workflow guide says "with timecode as the anchor point" a note tells the editor exactly where it applies, and suggests a review service that locks all feedback to the timeline.
  • It advises: "Once you get the feedback needed, document it, then execute."
  • YouTube Help says dips show where viewers abandon or skip and spikes where they watch or rewatch, which gives you the evidence for each note.
  • Describe the problem and the viewer behaviour, then suggest a fix; leave the editor room to find a better one.

Why graph-based notes work better

Notes such as "make it punchier" or "the middle drags" leave the editor guessing which minute you mean. A retention graph gives you a map. Each dip or spike has a time and a shape. If you tie each note to a timestamp and say what the graph shows there, the editor can go straight to the moment, watch it and decide what to do.

The Frame.io workflow guide stresses the same idea in a general way: timecode is the anchor point for feedback, and a review tool that locks all comments to the timeline avoids scattered emails. Its tip for a final step is to document the feedback, then execute.

The note format

Use four parts for each note.

PartExampleWhy
Timestamp00:42 to 00:55Anchors the note
Graph evidenceDrop from 71% to 58% across this stretchShows why you care
Likely causeLong background before the first stepGives context, not a verdict
Requested changeStart step one by 00:45; move background to a text cardOne clear action

Keep it to one change per note. If you want two things, write two notes. Add a priority: high for a large drop, medium for a smaller one and low for polish.

What the graph can and cannot tell you

YouTube Help says dips mean viewers are abandoning or skipping at that point, spikes mean viewers are watching, rewatching or sharing, and gradual declines mean viewers are losing interest over time. It also says some taper is normal. The graph shows what happened, not why. So label the cause as a guess, such as "possible cause", and let the editor use judgement.

Do not use the graph to prove the editor wrong. Use it to point at moments. A drop can have causes outside the edit, such as packaging or the traffic source. Say so in the notes if you suspect it.

Steps to write the notes

  1. Open the graph and the cut side by side. Note each dip and spike with time stamps.
  2. Rank by size. Start with the largest drop in the first 30 seconds, since YouTube's Intro key moment looks at that point.
  3. Write one note per issue with the four parts above.
  4. Give the intent. For example: "I want viewers to see the result within 10 seconds."
  5. Mark what to keep. Note spikes and flat stretches that are working, so the editor does not cut them.
  6. Put everything in one place. One document or one review tool; avoid sending notes across email, chat and calls.
  7. Send one consolidated list per round. Do not trickle notes in.
  8. Talk through unclear notes. The Frame.io guide recommends a short live conversation when written notes are unclear or disputed.
  9. After the revision is published, re-read the graph. Compare the same moments and report back.

How many rounds

Two rounds are a common starting point for a cut: one for structure and one for polish. That is a convention, not a rule; agree it in the brief or contract. Structure notes come first, since a cut that changes order makes small notes obsolete. Do not mix big and small notes in the same round if the big ones will change the timeline.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not send a graph screenshot with no timestamps. Do not give notes that contradict each other across rounds. Do not promise the editor a retention number. And do not forget to thank the editor for what works; a spike is evidence of skill as well as a lesson.

A brief set at the start helps here too: Storyblocks advises being specific about deadlines from the first draft to final delivery, which includes the dates for each round of notes.
