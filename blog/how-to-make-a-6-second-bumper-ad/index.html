How do you make a 6-second YouTube bumper ad that people remember?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

To make a 6-second bumper ad people remember, build it around one idea with a single payoff, tell it in two or three visual beats rather than words, show the brand inside the action from the first frame, and end on the moment of change. Bumper ads can't be skipped, so you don't need to fight the skip button; you need to be clear and memorable in 180 frames. Cut it from a longer ad's best moment or shoot it specifically, but never cram a 30-second script into six seconds.

Key takeaways

  • Google lists bumper ads as 6 seconds (5-second minimum), not skippable, bought on Target CPM, and not counted as TrueView views.
  • YouTube's "creativity in constraint" advice: "Think of your story like a joke", think in images, show change and leave room for imagination.
  • Bumpers suit reminders, launches and reinforcing a longer ad, not detailed explanations.
  • Google's ABCDs still apply: brand early, keep visuals bright and high-contrast, and support the story with audio and text.
  • On YouTube you can run a bumper after a longer ad using video ad sequencing.

What a bumper ad is

Google's video ad formats guide lists bumpers at 6 seconds with a 5-second minimum. They can't be skipped, you pay per impression on Target CPM, and they don't count toward TrueView views. Because everyone who sees one sees all of it, the job is clarity and memory, not winning a five-second battle.

YouTube's six-second storytelling tips

YouTube's article on six-second storytelling gathers advice from creatives who made bumper films:

TipWhat it means in practice
"Think of your story like a joke"Build to one punchline; cut anything that isn't set-up or payoff
Think in imagesPlan 2 or 3 visual beats, not sentences
Show changePick the exact moment something transforms
Start relatableA situation viewers recognise instantly
Keep elements simpleOne location, one action, one line
Leave room for imaginationSuggest; let curiosity do some work

One creative quoted in the piece describes the format as "180 frames", which is a useful way to think about how little room there is.

A 6-second beat sheet

SecondsBeatExample (coffee brand)
0 to 2Relatable situation, brand visibleBleary-eyed person at 6 am, branded cup in hand
2 to 4The changeFirst sip; eyes open, music lifts
4 to 6Payoff and brand lineText: "Mornings, sorted." Logo on the cup and end frame

Google's ABCDs say to "introduce your brand or product from the start", keep visuals "bright and high-contrast" and "support the story with audio and supers". In six seconds, a separate logo card at the start would use a third of your time; put the brand on the product in the shot instead.

Cut down or shoot fresh?

ApproachWorks whenWatch out for
Cut from a longer adThe long ad has one clear visual moment of changeLines that need context from the full ad
Shoot specificallyYou can plan the 3 beats from the startBudget for a separate shoot

When cutting down, look for the single strongest visual in the longer ad. If your long ad's retention data shows a spike or a section almost nobody leaves, that's often the best moment to lift.

Using bumpers with longer ads

Bumpers work well as reminders. Google's video ad sequencing lets you show a series of ads to the same person in order, mixing skippable, non-skippable and bumper ads; by default, one sequence per person every 7 days. A common pattern is a longer skippable ad that tells the story, followed by bumpers that recall its key moment. Sequences run on YouTube only, and you can't target keywords, topics or placements in them.

Worked example

An illustrative running-shoe brand has a 30-second skippable ad: a runner struggles up a hill in old shoes, switches, and flies up. For the bumper:

  1. 0 to 2 s: Close-up of tired feet in worn shoes at the bottom of the hill; brand box visible on a bench.
  2. 2 to 4 s: Feet in the new shoes, first fast step.
  3. 4 to 6 s: Runner at the top, arms up; text: "Hills, handled." Logo on the shoe and end frame.

No voiceover beyond a single line; music carries the change. The bumper runs in a sequence after the 30-second ad for people who watched it.

Common bumper ad mistakes

MistakeFix
A 30-second script squeezed into 6 secondsOne idea, one payoff
A logo card at the startBrand inside the scene from frame one
Lots of on-screen textA few words, readable in a glance
Relying on voiceoverTell it visually; audio supports
No clear endingFinish on the moment of change and the brand
Running bumpers alone for complex productsPair them with a longer ad that explains

Make several. Because bumpers are short and cheap to cut, make three or four variations of the same idea and compare their results. Different first frames often make a big difference.

Measure the right thing. Bumpers don't count TrueView views, and you pay per impression, so judge them on reach, frequency and, if you run brand studies, recall. If you're using them as reminders in a sequence, look at how the whole sequence performs; see how to use video ad sequencing.

Cap frequency. Bumpers are often used for reach, so frequency caps help spread impressions across more people rather than repeating to the same viewers.

Test before scaling. Run two or three bumper variations at a modest budget, compare reach and any brand-lift results available to you, then scale the best.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches pacing for short video?

The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, pacing and structure at £1,000 one-time. YouTube's six-second storytelling article is a free alternative.

Can viewers skip bumper ads?

No. Bumper ads are 6 seconds and not skippable.

Do bumper ads count as views?

No. Google says bumpers don't count toward TrueView views; you pay per impression.

Should a bumper ad have a call to action?

Keep it to a brand line or one simple action. Six seconds rarely fits more.
