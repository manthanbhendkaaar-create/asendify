How do I use captions to improve retention on silent autoplay video ads?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 25 November 2026 · Last updated 25 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • HubSpot's guide to silent Facebook video ads cites a Digiday figure that people watch 85% of Facebook videos without turning the sound on (HubSpot). The source is older, so treat it as a direction, not a current measure.
  • The same guide warns that "captioning technology isn't at the point where it gets everything 100% right", and advises checking and editing auto-captions before publishing.
  • A safe-zone guide for Meta Reels and Stories ads suggests leaving about 14% free at the top and 20% to 35% at the bottom, so labels and buttons do not cover the words (Billo).
  • Another guide for TikTok and Reels gives pixel margins on a 1080 by 1920 frame, with roughly 320 px free at the bottom on TikTok and 420 px on boosted Reels (Ignite Social Media).

What captions do on a silent autoplay ad

On autoplay, the picture moves and the sound is off. Captions are then the only way to deliver anything spoken. They also act as a second hook: a viewer who ignores the image may still read a line. The aim is not to transcribe everything. It is to give a silent viewer the same reason to stay that a listener gets.

Done badly, captions can hurt. A wall of small text is hard to read on a phone, and a long line makes people look down and away from the picture. Captions that sit under the app's own buttons are not seen at all.

Step-by-step: build captions for a silent ad

  1. Write the hook as text first. Decide the one line a silent viewer must read in the first two seconds.
  2. Show it on frame one. Do not wait for the speech to start.
  3. Generate captions, then correct them. Auto-captions are fast but not perfect. Fix names, numbers and product terms line by line.
  4. Keep lines short. Two short lines at a time read better than one long line.
  5. Match the speech. Captions should appear as the words are spoken, not before or after.
  6. Place them in the safe area. Leave the top 14% and the bottom third clear on Reels and Stories, and check TikTok's margins separately.
  7. Make them readable on a phone. Use a large, plain typeface, strong contrast and a background or outline if the picture is busy.
  8. Do not cover the face or the product. If the caption sits on the subject, move it.
  9. Emphasise the key word. A colour or weight change on the word that matters guides the eye.
  10. Keep the same words on screen long enough to read. A common test is to read the line aloud once, slowly.
  11. Watch muted. Check the ad on a phone with the sound off, then ask someone who has not seen it what it is for.
  12. Check retention. Compare viewers who pass the first five seconds before and after the caption change.

Accuracy and fairness

Captions also help viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing, and anyone watching in a noisy place. Accuracy matters for them as well as for the ad. Wrong captions can change the meaning of a claim, and in advertising a wrong figure in a caption is still a claim, so check any number, price or promise against what the ad is allowed to say.

When captions are not enough

If the hook only works when spoken, rewrite it. A caption cannot rescue a line that has no picture to support it. The strongest silent ads combine an image that shows the point, a few words that name it and a change of shot that keeps the eye moving.

Can I rely on auto-captions?

Not without checking. Even the guide that recommends them says the technology does not get everything right, so read every line.

How many words should a caption line have?

Keep it short, often two short lines at a time, so it can be read in a glance.

Where should captions go in a vertical ad?

Above the bottom third and below the top margin, away from the buttons. Check each platform's current safe area.

Should the hook be a caption or a spoken line?

Both, if you can. The caption carries it for silent viewers and the voice for the rest.
