Should you put a video in your event invitation emails?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Usually not as an embedded, playable video, because most inboxes won't play it. Gmail and many Outlook versions strip the video tag. Instead, put a short animated GIF or a thumbnail with a play button in the email, linking to a landing page where a short promo starts on its best moment and the register button sits right below it.

Key takeaways

  • Can I Email lists Gmail as not supporting the HTML video element; video tags are replaced, so videos don't play.
  • Apple Mail supports it; Outlook support is mixed. Can I Email estimates overall support at about 24%, from tests dated 2019.
  • Litmus recommends an animated GIF as the most reliable way to get motion into an email, noting GIFs are "supported almost everywhere".
  • The video still matters: on the landing page, the first seconds decide whether someone watches long enough to register.
  • Always include a fallback so the email works when nothing moves.

What the support data says

Can I Email's page on the video element records Apple Mail as supporting video from macOS and iOS 12.4, with limits on iOS (controls needed, no autoplay). It summarises Gmail as not supported, with video, source and track tags replaced. Outlook is mixed: some desktop versions are listed, while Outlook.com and the mobile apps either stripped the tags in 2019 tests or can't play video under a strict content security policy in later entries. The page estimates overall support at about 24% and notes the data was last tested in 2019, so check your own audience's email clients in your sending tool.

What Litmus recommends

Litmus's guide to video in email says that when native video isn't viable, "instead of using a static image, try an animated GIF", because "GIFs in email are supported almost everywhere." The trade-off is that a GIF is short and silent. Litmus is also lukewarm on static play-button images and on simply sending people to a landing page, saying they don't use video's full power in the inbox. For event invitations, a GIF that links to the full promo usually balances reach and effort.

Make the GIF and the landing page work together

  1. Pick 3-5 seconds from the promo that show the event at its best: a packed room, a big reaction, a well-known speaker on stage.
  2. Keep it small. Short, cropped GIFs load faster.
  3. Link the GIF to the event page, ideally to a version of the page where the promo is at the top.
  4. Open the promo on its best moment. YouTube's audience retention report shows how many viewers are still watching after 30 seconds; a promo that opens with a logo animation loses people before they see the reason to come.
  5. Put the register button beside or right under the video, not after three paragraphs.

Example: a conference early-bird email

Example: an events company sends 20,000 invitations for a one-day marketing conference.

  • Their sending tool shows most opens come from Gmail and Outlook, so embedded video is ruled out.
  • The designer makes a 4-second GIF from last year's recap: the keynote speaker's big laugh line and a wide shot of a full room, looping.
  • The GIF links to the event page, where a 45-second promo opens on the same laugh, then shows three speakers, then the date and the early-bird deadline.
  • The register button sits directly below the promo.
  • A text line under the GIF, "Watch the 45-second preview", gives a second click target for people with images off.

The email works in every inbox, and the people who click land on a video built to hold them until the button.

Other things to check

  • Alt text on the GIF or thumbnail, describing what's in it, for people with images turned off and screen reader users.
  • File size. Large GIFs slow loading on mobile data.
  • Accuracy. The promo and email should describe the event honestly; don't claim scarcity you can't back up.
  • Tracking. Tag the link so you can see how many registrations came from the video click.

When embedded video can make sense

If your list is mostly Apple Mail users, for example an internal audience on company iPhones and Macs, a native video with a fallback image can work. Test it in your own inbox and a test tool first, and keep the fallback image linked to the same landing page, so everyone else still gets a working email.

Common mistakes

  • Embedding video that shows a blank box in Gmail.
  • A huge GIF that takes seconds to load.
  • Linking to a page where the video is buried below the fold.
  • A promo that opens on a logo sting.
  • No fallback text link.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches cold opens for short promo videos?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches cold opens and pacing for YouTube, which carries over to event promos. For email coding, use Litmus and Can I Email.

Does video play in Gmail?

No. Can I Email lists Gmail as not supporting the HTML video element, so embedded videos don't play there.

Does video play in Apple Mail?

Yes, Apple Mail supports embedded video, with some limits on iOS such as no autoplay.

Is a GIF better than a play-button image?

Litmus recommends a GIF, because it shows motion and is supported almost everywhere. A static image is simpler but less engaging.

How long should the GIF be?

A few seconds is enough to show the event's best moment. Keep the file small.

Where should the video link go?

To an event page where the promo is near the top and the register button is right next to it.
