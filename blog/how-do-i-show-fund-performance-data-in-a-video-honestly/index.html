How do I show fund performance data in a video honestly?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Show the period, the source and a fair comparison on the screen at the same time as the number, give the risks the same visual weight as the benefits, and never pick a window only because it flatters the fund. Pace the chart so a viewer can actually read it: show it long enough, say what it shows, and say what it does not show. Honest presentation is also better retention practice, because viewers who feel misled leave and do not return. Key takeaways

  • The FCA says financial promotions "on all advertising channels should be fair, clear and not misleading" and that firms should "ensure that promotions provide a balanced view of the benefits and risks" (FCA). Video is one of those channels.
  • The FCA's financial promotions page says "all financial promotions must be clear, fair and not misleading regardless of the media type" (FCA). The pages I read did not set out specific past-performance rules, so check the current rulebook for your case.
  • Mayer's signaling principle says "people learn better when there are cues that emphasize information that is more important" (Dartmouth). Use cues to point to the period and the caveat, not only the headline return.
  • YouTube's retention report says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part" (YouTube Help). A chart that is shown too briefly or too densely often creates one.

What "honest" means on screen

A fund performance figure on its own is nearly meaningless. A viewer needs to know the period it covers, what it is compared with, whether it is before or after charges, and what could go differently next time. Honest presentation puts those items in the frame together with the number, not in a footnote the viewer will never read or a voiceover that races past.

It also means choosing windows fairly. Showing a fund's best year or best five years, because that is where the line looks good, tells a different story from showing a standard period. If you show a window, say why you chose it, and consider showing a longer one alongside.

A checklist for every performance chart

ItemOn screenSpoken
PeriodStart and end dates"Over the five years to 31 March"
SourceName of the data provider or fund factsheet"From the fund's own factsheet"
ComparisonBenchmark or relevant alternative"Compared with the sector average"
ChargesWhether returns include charges"After ongoing charges" if true

Use the real wording from the fund's own documents where possible. Do not paraphrase risk language into something softer.

Step-by-step: build the segment

  1. Open with the question, not the number. "How has this fund done against its benchmark?" sets expectations better than "up 40%".
  2. Show the chart with labelled axes. Dates on the horizontal axis, a clear unit on the vertical, and a readable legend.
  3. Name the period and source before the number. A viewer should know what they are looking at before they see the line.
  4. Show the comparison at the same time. Put the benchmark line on the same chart, not on a later slide.
  5. Say what it does not show. "This is past performance. It is not a guide to what happens next." Say it plainly, in your own voice, and show it as text.
  6. Hold the chart long enough. Give a viewer time to read; if you have three key points, reveal them one at a time with a pointer.
  7. Add the risk statement next to the number, not at the end. The FCA's wording on balance suggests risk and benefit belong together.
  8. Keep a calm tone. Avoid fast cuts, riser sounds or excited music over performance numbers; they signal salesmanship.
  9. Put sources in the description. Add links to the factsheet and the data provider, and the date you accessed them.
  10. Update or take down stale numbers. A video with year-old performance should be marked as such or replaced.

Pacing a chart so viewers do not leave

Viewers leave charts they cannot read. Common mistakes are showing a dense chart for two seconds, speaking over it without pointing at anything, and cutting away before the point lands. Fixes:

  • Build the chart in stages. Show the axes, then the line, then the comparison, then the highlight.
  • Point to the one thing. Circle or highlight the feature you are discussing; Mayer's signaling idea says cues help people learn.
  • Limit to one message per chart. If you have two points, use two charts.
  • Use chapters for longer finance videos. YouTube chapters need a first timestamp at 00:00, at least three timestamps and at least 10 seconds each (YouTube Help).
  • Add captions for numbers. Captions help viewers who watch with sound off, but check numbers and dates in the captions for accuracy.

What to avoid

  • Cherry-picked windows. Do not choose a period only because it looks best.
  • Headline returns without charges. If you quote a return, state whether it is before or after charges.
  • Compare-to-nothing. A number with no benchmark can mislead.
  • Hidden risk text. Tiny, fast or low-contrast risk wording does not give a balanced view.
  • Projected numbers presented as expected results. Keep any illustration clearly labelled and based on stated assumptions.
  • Testimonials or "I made X" claims. Treat them with great care; they can be misleading and may fall within financial promotion rules.

Example: a 7-minute fund review

Example: a creator reviews a global equity fund. First version: a flashy five-second chart showing the best five-year window, a rising sound effect, and a risk line in small text at the end. Second version: the chart is shown for ten seconds with dates, source and benchmark, the creator says "this is past performance, not a guide to the future", the risk statement is on screen alongside the chart, and a longer period is shown next. The creator checks the retention graph around the chart and the comments for "what was the period". This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result and not a recommendation of any fund.

If you are regulated, or promoting a regulated product

UK financial promotion rules can apply to content on social platforms and video, including to "finfluencers" in some cases. Whether they apply to you depends on what you say, who you say it to and whether you are authorised or approved. Take advice from a compliance professional or read the FCA's pages directly.
