How do I cut a recorded product webinar into short feature clips?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 7 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A product webinar is usually 45 to 60 minutes of greetings, slides, a demo and questions. The clips worth cutting are the 30 to 90 second moments where one feature solves one problem the viewer already has. Find those moments, start each clip on the problem rather than the greeting, and give every clip one job: make a viewer want to see that feature in action.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube Studio can cut a Short or a clip from an existing long video, and you can choose the segment "by transcript text or by timestamp", then adjust "the start and end points" (YouTube Help).
  • Shorts made this way get automatic captions "where available", and the source video is added as a related video unless the Short is private, unlisted or made for kids (same page).
  • YouTube's retention report says dips "mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video", so the replay's own graph tells you which moments to skip and which to keep (YouTube Help).
  • LinkedIn's own posting tips, as summarised by Social Media Today, say to "make the first six seconds count" and to design video "with extensibility in mind", for example by splitting it into shareable parts (Social Media Today).
  • Mayer's segmenting principle says people "learn better when the information is presented in user-paced segments, rather than in one continuous unit" (Dartmouth). A feature clip is exactly that.

Why feature clips beat one big highlight reel

A webinar recording is built for people who chose to show up and sit through it. A clip is watched by someone scrolling, who owes you nothing. If you cut one generic "best bits" video, you serve nobody well: the feature a prospect cares about is buried at minute 31.

Feature clips fix that. Each one answers a single question ("Can I automatically tag support tickets?") and can be sent to the exact prospect who asked it. Sales teams can drop one into an email or a chat; marketing can post it on LinkedIn or Shorts; support can link it from a help article. One recording becomes a small library.

Step 1: Mine the replay for moments

Start with the transcript, not the video. Read it, or search it, for these markers:

  1. A problem stated by an attendee. Questions in the Q&A are gold because they are real phrasing of real needs.
  2. A "watch this" moment. The point where the presenter switches from slides to the live product.
  3. A before and after. A manual process shown, then the same task done in the product.
  4. A number or result shown on screen that the presenter explains.
  5. A decision moment, such as "this setting is what most teams miss".

Also open the retention graph if the replay was published on YouTube. YouTube describes spikes as "moments in your video that were rewatched or shared". Those are your first candidates, because viewers already voted for them.

Step 2: Decide the clip's one job

Write one sentence before you cut: "After this clip, the viewer knows that [product] can [do one thing] without [pain]." If you cannot finish the sentence, the moment is not a clip yet.

Step 3: Cut the clip

Trim the setup hard. In a live demo, presenters say "so let me just share my screen" and "as you can see". Cut to the first sentence that carries meaning. If the sentence before it was necessary context, put it on screen as a short text line instead of spoken audio.

Start on the problem. A clip that opens with "Hi everyone, welcome back" has used its first seconds on nothing. Open on the pain: "Still tagging tickets by hand?" Then show the feature.

Fix the screen recording for a phone. Webinar demos are wide and tiny. Zoom into the part of the interface that matters, crop out toolbars, and keep text large enough to read on a small screen. A good rule of thumb: if you cannot read the button label at arm's length on your phone, zoom in further.

Add captions. Many people watch with sound off, and screen demos are hard to follow without narration text. YouTube's Short tool adds autocaptions where available, but check them: product names, acronyms and feature names are where automatic captions go wrong.

Use signalling. Mayer's signalling principle says "people learn better when there are cues that emphasize information that is more important." In practice: a highlight box or arrow on the button being clicked, appearing as the narrator names it, and gone once the point is made.

Step 4: Keep it honest

A clip lifted from a webinar can mislead if it hides a limit. If a feature needs a paid tier, an integration or setup time, say so on screen or in the caption. Do not show a polished result that the live demo only reached after several tries without noting it is an example. Prospects who feel misled after a clip will not trust the next one, and the full webinar then looks like bait.

Example: one webinar, six clips

Example: a software company runs a 50-minute webinar on a ticket-routing tool. The editor reads the transcript and marks six moments: an attendee asking about duplicate tickets, a live demo of auto-routing, a before and after of manual triage, a mistake about priority rules, the pricing question, and the closing summary. Each becomes a clip of roughly 40 to 75 seconds. The first frame of each is the problem as text, the audio starts on the first meaningful sentence, and the caption links to the full replay with a timestamp for that moment. The team posts one clip a day for a week instead of one large post. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Step 5: Link clips back to the replay

Every clip should have a next step. In YouTube Studio, the source video is automatically attached as a related video unless the Short is private, unlisted or made for kids. On LinkedIn, put the replay link in the first comment or the post text. On your site, embed the clip next to the full replay so that anyone who wants depth can click through. Add timestamps to the replay's description so it works as chapters: YouTube's chapters need at least three timestamps, the first at 00:00, and each at least 10 seconds long (YouTube Help).

Common mistakes

  • Cutting by time, not by topic. Slicing a webinar into ten-minute pieces gives you ten weak clips, not ten useful ones.
  • Keeping the presenter's greeting. Cut it.
  • No caption fixes. Wrong product names in captions look careless.
  • Clips with several features. One clip, one feature.
  • No link back. A clip with no next step is a dead end.
  • Hiding limits. Say what plan or setup a feature needs.

FAQ

How long should a feature clip from a webinar be?

Long enough to show one feature solving one problem, usually 30 to 90 seconds. There is no single correct length; check the retention report for your own clips and shorten whatever loses viewers early.

Can YouTube Studio cut clips from my webinar replay automatically?

You can create a clip or Short from an existing video in YouTube Studio by choosing a transcript range or timestamps. AI suggestions are only available in select countries and languages, so you may need to choose the segments yourself.

Should I cut clips from the live recording or re-record the demo?

Cut from the live recording when the demo ran cleanly and the questions were real. Re-record only when the screen was unreadable or the presenter stumbled on the key step.

Do I need captions on webinar clips?

Yes, in practice. Screen demos are hard to follow with sound off, and WCAG says captions should convey spoken dialogue and meaningful sound so people who are deaf or hard of hearing can follow (W3C).
