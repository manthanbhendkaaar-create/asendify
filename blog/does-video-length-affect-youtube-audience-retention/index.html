Does video length affect YouTube audience retention?

Longer videos usually show a lower retention percentage, simply because viewers have more minutes in which to leave, so a 25-minute video at 35% can be holding attention better than a 6-minute video at 55%. That is why YouTube compares each video with videos of similar length. Judge length by relative retention and total watch time, not by the percentage alone.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's typical retention line compares a video with your 10 latest videos of similar length, not with all your videos.
  • The "See more" comparison and the API's relative retention metric compare with YouTube videos of similar length on a 0 to 1 scale.
  • YouTube lists video length among the content changes that can shift audience interest and watch time.
  • Average view duration can hint at your ideal length, according to YouTube's own analytics tips.
  • The right length is the shortest one that fully delivers the title's promise.

Why length and percentage are linked

Average percentage viewed is average view duration divided by length. Add minutes and, unless every extra minute is as gripping as the first, the percentage falls. The YouTube Analytics API defines averageViewPercentage as "the average percentage of a video watched during a video playback", so a longer denominator pulls it down.

YouTube corrects for this in two places. Its key moments page says the "See more" view compares a video with videos of similar length, and the grey typical retention band reflects your 10 latest videos of similar length. The API's relativeRetentionPerformance measures how well a video retains viewers compared with similar-length YouTube videos, from 0 to 1 with 0.5 as the median.

What YouTube says about length

YouTube's Content tab analytics tips describe average view duration as a metric that "can signal changing audience interests and suggest ideal video length". Its watch time drop tips list video length alongside topic, editing style and upload frequency as content changes that can affect audience interest. Neither page gives an ideal length, because there isn't one.

How to find your right length

  1. Group your videos by length. For example, under 10 minutes, 10-20, over 20.
  2. Compare within each group. Use the typical retention band and the "See more" comparison rather than raw percentages.
  3. Compare total watch time per view across groups. Average view duration in minutes tells you which length keeps people watching longest in absolute terms.
  4. Find where the curve flattens. Many videos lose viewers fastest early, then settle. If your long videos flatten at 40% and stay there, the length isn't the problem; the opening might be.
  5. Look for padding. A slow, steady slope through the middle of long videos, steeper than the typical band, suggests sections that could go.
  6. Test one length change. Keep topic and style constant, change only the length, and compare at the same age.

Worked example

Example: a science channel has two kinds of video. Its 8-minute videos average 52% viewed (about 4 minutes 10 seconds per view). Its 22-minute deep dives average 34% viewed (about 7 minutes 29 seconds per view).

On percentage, the short videos look better. On minutes watched per view, the deep dives deliver nearly twice as much. Studio's "See more" comparison shows the deep dives sit above the median for their length for most of the video, while the short videos sit slightly below it after 3:00.

Conclusion: the long format is working; the short videos have a weak middle. The creator keeps the deep dives and tightens the short videos rather than cutting everything to eight minutes. These figures are illustrative.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Chasing a percentage. Cutting a strong 20-minute video to 10 minutes can raise the percentage and lower total watch time.
  • Padding for length. Extra minutes that viewers skip show up as dips and a steeper slope.
  • Comparing Shorts with long videos. YouTube's tips say audience behaviour differs across formats.
  • Changing length and topic at once. You won't know which one moved the numbers.

Questions to ask before changing video length

  1. Where does the curve flatten? If most viewers who stay past two minutes watch to the end, the middle is fine and length is not the problem.
  2. Is there a steady slide steeper than the typical band? That suggests padding or slow sections to cut, not necessarily a shorter format.
  3. What do viewers come for? Tutorials often need room; quick answers do not. Match length to the job the title promises.
  4. What happens to total watch time? Estimate minutes watched per view before and after; a shorter video that halves minutes per view may cost reach.
  5. Are you comparing the same format? Keep Shorts out of the comparison entirely.

If you test a new length, keep the topic type, thumbnail style and upload day the same for two or three uploads so the comparison is fair.

FAQ

Is there a tool that shows where long videos lose viewers, with a fix for each drop?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Doctor flags each drop-off with a fix after every upload, using read-only access. YouTube Studio's similar-length comparison is free.

What is the best length for a YouTube video?

There is no single best length. It is the shortest length that fully delivers what the title promises, judged against your own similar-length videos.

Do longer videos rank better on YouTube?

YouTube doesn't say so. Its recommendation system values watch time and viewer satisfaction, and padding that viewers skip can hurt both.

Why is my average percentage viewed lower on long videos?

Because there is more time to leave. Compare long videos with long videos, or use relative retention.

Should I split a long video into parts?

Only if viewers clearly want the parts separately. Check whether the curve flattens or keeps falling before deciding.
