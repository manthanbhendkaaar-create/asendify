How do you tell a beneficiary's story in a charity video without exploiting them?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 3 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Treat the person as a co-author, not a subject. Get informed consent for each use, let them decide how they appear (face, name, voice, what's left out), show their agency and the change in their life rather than only their hardship, and never exaggerate. Done well, this doesn't cost you attention: a real person telling their own story in their own words is often the most watchable video a charity makes.

Key takeaways

  • The UK Fundraising Regulator's key values are legal, open, honest and respectful; "honest" means "not misleading the public".
  • Consent should be informed (where it will appear, for how long, in ads or not) and revisited before publishing.
  • People's images and stories are personal data; store consent records with the footage.
  • Showing change and agency ("she now runs the group") keeps viewers watching to the payoff, while unrelenting suffering drives them away.
  • Research on the identifiable victim effect (Stanford GSB summary) explains why one person's story moves people, which is exactly why it must be told responsibly.

Exploitative vs respectful choices

ExploitativeRespectful
Close-ups of distress without contextThe person framed with dignity, often in their own space
Narrator speaks about themThey speak for themselves where they wish to
Story ends at the lowest pointStory includes what they did and what changed
Exaggerated details for impactAccurate details they've approved
Faces of children in crisisChildren protected; identities hidden where appropriate
One-off consent buried in a formClear explanation, a real choice, and a chance to review

A consent and production checklist

  1. Explain plainly: who will see it, where, for how long, whether it'll be used in ads or by partners.
  2. Offer choices: first name only, a pseudonym, face hidden, voice only, or a staff member telling the story instead.
  3. No pressure: services must never depend on taking part.
  4. Record consent in writing, and check again before publishing.
  5. Let them review the final edit, and remove anything they're unhappy with.
  6. Agree a removal process in case they change their mind later; explain that public posts can be copied.
  7. Safeguarding: extra care for children, people at risk, or anyone whose location could put them in danger.

Editing for attention, ethically

  • Open on their words, not your logo: "I didn't think anyone would listen" is a strong, honest hook.
  • Follow their story order: before, turning point, now. That structure gives viewers a reason to stay.
  • Use B-roll they chose: their hands at work, their garden, the group they attend.
  • Let silences breathe, and don't cut lines in a way that changes their meaning.
  • Music restraint: sad music over vulnerable people feels manipulative.
  • Captions that match exactly what they said.

Example: a mental health charity

Example: a charity plans an appeal around a young man who used its helpline. He agrees to take part but doesn't want his face shown. The edit uses his voice over shots of the city at night, his hands making tea, and his walk to the group he now helps run. He sees the cut and asks for one detail about his family to be removed; the charity removes it. Details are illustrative.

FAQ

Is there a book on story structure for charity videos?

The Retention Editing Playbook (£1,720 one-time) teaches hooks and structure for YouTube; it doesn't cover fundraising ethics.

Do beneficiaries need to see the video before it's published?

It's good practice. Reviewing the edit protects them and your charity's reputation.

Can we use a beneficiary's story in paid ads?

Only if consent explicitly covers advertising.

Can we change details to protect someone?

Yes, if you're open about it (for example, "names changed"). Don't add details that didn't happen.

What if someone withdraws consent later?

Remove it where you can, and explain at the start that copies of public posts can't always be recalled.
