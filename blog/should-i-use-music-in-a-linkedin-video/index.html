Should I use music in a LinkedIn video?

Key takeaways

  • LinkedIn's video ad tips say "think like a silent film director: a large portion of LinkedIn members will watch your ad with the sound off" and suggest burning in subtitles (LinkedIn Marketing Solutions).
  • The same page says to show what you want your audience to see in the first 10 seconds, because "viewer attention drops after that point".
  • Captions are "a text version of the speech and non-speech audio information needed to understand the content" (W3C WAI), so music that carries meaning should be described.
  • Learning research says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded" (Dartmouth, summarising Mayer); music can be extra noise.

Why music is a minor question on LinkedIn

On many platforms, sound is part of the hook. On LinkedIn, much of the audience is at work, in an open office or on a commute, and LinkedIn tells advertisers plainly that many viewers watch without sound. That changes what music is for. It cannot be the reason someone stops scrolling, and it cannot carry the message.

So the real question is not "which track". It is "does this video still work when the sound is off, and is the sound pleasant when it is on?" Get the first right and the second becomes a finishing touch.

LinkedIn's guidance is for ads, but the same audience sees organic videos, so the advice is a reasonable starting point for both. Test on your own posts.

How to add music without hurting clarity

  1. Edit the video silent first. Add captions and on-screen text until the message is clear without sound. LinkedIn recommends burning subtitles in for this reason.
  2. Add the voice or speech. Clean it up before touching music.
  3. Add a track with no vocals. Words in a song compete with words in your speech.
  4. Set the music well under the voice. Many editors start with music around 15 to 20 decibels below dialogue and then adjust by ear. This is a working rule of thumb, not an official standard.
  5. Dip the music under key lines. Lower it a little as the main point is spoken, and raise it between sentences.
  6. Cut on the beat where it is easy. Matching cuts to a steady beat gives pace, but do not force it.
  7. Check on a phone speaker. Many people listen on small speakers where low frequencies vanish and the track may sound thin or loud.
  8. Check the licence. Use music you have the right to use on LinkedIn; platform libraries and licensed tracks differ in what they allow.

Frame.io's loudness guide says YouTube "tends to shoot for -12 to -14 LUFS" and advises judging by ear (Frame.io Workflow Guide). That figure is about YouTube, and LinkedIn does not publish its own target in the sources checked here, so keep your final mix consistent and not louder than a normal speaking voice.

Describe music that carries meaning

If the music tells the viewer something, such as a tense sting before a reveal, a caption can say so. The W3C's definition of captions includes non-speech audio that is needed to understand the content. For most LinkedIn videos, background music does not need a caption. If a song lyric or a sound effect is central to the point, describe it in text.

Does music affect how long people watch?

The sources checked here do not give a measured effect of music on LinkedIn watch time. What they do give is clear guidance on what to prioritise: the first 10 seconds, readable visuals and captions. Wistia's analysis of video length says videos under a minute average a 52% engagement rate (Wistia), which is marketing video data across platforms, not music data. Keep videos short, put the point first, and treat music as support.

If you want to know whether your track helps, test it. Post two similar videos with different music treatment, or compare a version with music and one without, and look at views, watch time and comments. Change one thing at a time.

Example: a 45-second founder update

Example: a founder records a 45-second video about a product change. Her first draft opens with a 3-second music intro and a title card, then she speaks. She reworks it. The video now starts with her saying the headline, with the same sentence burned in as text. She removes the music intro and adds a soft instrumental bed, dipped under each key sentence, and she checks the video on her phone with the sound off. The point still lands from captions alone. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.
