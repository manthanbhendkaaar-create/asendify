Should online course videos show the instructor's face or just the slides?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Show your face for part of the video. An edX study found videos that mix a talking head with slides were more engaging than slides alone, but larger field experiments found showing the face did not change test scores, and about a third of learners chose to hide it. Use the face to open, connect and summarise; use slides when detail matters.

Key takeaways

  • Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014), from 6.9 million edX sessions: "Videos that intersperse an instructor's talking head with slides are more engaging than slides alone."
  • Kizilcec, Bailenson and Gomez (2015), Journal of Educational Psychology, ran two field studies (2,951 and 12,468 learners) and found "learning outcomes and attrition did not differ" between showing the face all the time and part of the time.
  • In the same work, "35% watched videos without the face for self-reported reasons including avoiding distraction."
  • The full paper cites an earlier eye-tracking study in which "learners spent 41% of their time looking at the face when it was shown in the video". A face competes with the slide for attention.
  • Brame's review of educational video recommends a "conversational rather than formal" style, which a visible, relaxed presenter makes easier.

FormatStrengthWeaknessBest used for

The edX study also found "Khan-style tablet drawing tutorials are more engaging than PowerPoint slides or code screencasts", which suggests the key is movement and a sense of someone thinking, not the face itself.

Why "engaging" and "learned more" are different answers

The two research findings look like they disagree, but they measure different things. Guo's team measured how long people watched. Kizilcec's team measured what people learned and whether they dropped out of the course. A face can make a video feel warmer and keep people watching for longer without making them score higher on a quiz.

For a course creator, both matter. If learners leave, they learn nothing. If they stay but are distracted, they learn less. The practical answer is to show your face where it helps people stay, and hide it where it competes with the content.

A simple rule for each part of a lesson

  1. Open on your face (10-30 seconds). Say what the learner will be able to do by the end. A face builds trust quickly.
  2. Switch to full-screen slides or screen recording for detail. When the learner needs to read, compare or follow a cursor, remove the face.
  3. Cut back to your face at transitions. "That's the theory. Now let's try one." Brief returns reset attention.
  4. Use your face for stories and mistakes. Personal examples land better with eye contact.
  5. Close on your face. Summarise and set up the next lesson.
  6. Let learners choose if your platform allows. If a third of people prefer no face, an option costs little.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on face and slide cuts for course videos?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers pacing and pattern interrupts with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. The research papers above are free companions.

Does showing my face improve learning?

The large field experiments found no difference in test scores or dropout between showing the face constantly and showing it part of the time. It does tend to make videos feel more engaging.

Do learners dislike seeing the instructor?

Some do. In one study about a third chose to watch without the face, often to avoid distraction.

Is picture-in-picture a good compromise?

It can be for light slides. For detailed diagrams or code, go full screen on the content.

I'm camera shy. Can a slides-only course work?

Yes. Use a warm, conversational voice, add movement such as hand-drawn annotations, and consider a short on-camera welcome.
