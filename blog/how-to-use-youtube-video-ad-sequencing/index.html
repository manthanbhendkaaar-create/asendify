How do I use video ad sequencing to tell a story on YouTube?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Video ad sequencing in Google Ads lets you show a series of YouTube ads to the same person in the order you choose, so you can tell a story across several short ads instead of cramming it into one. A simple sequence is: a short hook ad that introduces the problem, a longer skippable ad that shows the solution to people who saw the first, and a 6-second bumper that reminds them. You set rules for when someone moves to the next step, such as after an impression, a view or a skip.

Key takeaways

  • Google defines a sequence as "a series of video ads that you'd like to show to a person" in an order you set.
  • Each step contains one ad group and one video ad; viewers progress based on impressions, views or skips.
  • Steps can use skippable in-stream, non-skippable in-stream and bumper ads, or a mix.
  • Bidding is Target CPM, and the default frequency cap is one sequence per person in 7 days (30 days optional).
  • You can't target keywords, topics or placements in sequence campaigns, and they run on YouTube only.

How sequencing works

From Google's guide to video ad sequencing:

SettingDetail
StructureNumbered steps, each with one ad group and one video ad
ProgressionPer step: after an impression, a view or a skip
Ad formatsSkippable in-stream, non-skippable in-stream, bumper, or a mix
BiddingTarget CPM (optimises to show the whole sequence)
FrequencyOne sequence per person per 7 days by default; 30 days optional
TargetingAudiences and demographics at campaign level; no keyword, topic or placement targeting
WhereYouTube only; no traffic estimates

The progression rule is a useful lever. Google's set-up guide says "skip" and "view" rules are only available on Target CPM campaigns with skippable in-stream ads, so you can, for example, move people to the next step only once they have watched at least 30 seconds of a skippable ad.

Why tell a story across ads

A single 15-second ad has to hook, explain, prove and ask all at once. A sequence lets each ad do one job, which fits Google's ABCDs advice to "keep messaging and language focused and simple". Each ad still needs its own hook, because viewers don't know they're in a sequence.

A three-step template

StepFormatJobProgression rule
1Bumper (6 s) or short non-skippableIntroduce the problem with a striking imageAfter impression
2Skippable in-stream (30 to 60 s)Show the solution and proof to people who saw step 1After impression (or after view, to keep step 3 for engaged viewers)
3Bumper (6 s)Reminder with offer and call to actionAfter impression

Google's ad formats guide confirms bumpers are 6 seconds and not skippable, while skippable in-stream ads can be skipped after five seconds, so step 2's opening still has to earn attention.

Writing for a sequence

  • Consistent look. Same person, colours and music, so viewers recognise step 2 as related.
  • Each ad stands alone. Someone may only ever see one step.
  • Escalate. Problem, then solution, then proof or offer. Don't repeat the same message three times.
  • End each step with an open loop. "Next: what happened when we tried it."

Worked example

An illustrative online language course:

  1. Step 1 (bumper): A traveller freezes at a café counter, unable to order. Text: "Holiday in 6 weeks?"
  2. Step 2 (45-second skippable): The same traveller, six weeks of 10-minute lessons later, ordering confidently, with clips of the lessons. Ends: "First week free."
  3. Step 3 (bumper): "Your first week is free. Start tonight."

The advertiser compares the sequence with a standalone 30-second ad to a matched audience and looks at sign-ups, not just views. All claims must be true and typical; see can I use a curiosity hook without breaking the rules. For making the short steps, see how to make a 6-second bumper ad.

Measuring whether a sequence works

Sequences are harder to judge than single ads, because the value is spread across several steps. Plan the measurement before you launch.

Compare with a single-ad control. The cleanest test is a sequence campaign against a standard campaign with your best single ad, aimed at a comparable audience with similar budget. Compare the business result you care about, such as sign-ups or sales, not just views.

Read the step report. Google's set-up guide says that in reporting you can see the path of steps and the interactions needed to reach each step. Look at how many people reach each step. A big fall between step 1 and step 2 may mean the progression rule is too strict, or that step 1 isn't creating enough interest.

SignalWhat it suggests
Few people reach step 2Step 1 too weak, or progression rule too strict
Many reach step 2 but skip itStep 2's opening doesn't connect to step 1
Strong completion, weak resultsStory works, offer or call to action doesn't
Results no better than the single-ad controlSimpler single-ad campaigns may be enough

Keep each step's hook strong. Viewers don't know they're watching part 2. Each step's first five seconds still decide whether a skippable ad is watched. See how to stop viewers skipping a YouTube ad after 5 seconds.

Mind the frequency settings. The default is one sequence per person every 7 days. If your sequence is meant to run over a longer consideration period, the 30-day option may fit better. Google advises against changing the cap after saving.

Plan for the limits. You can't target keywords, topics or placements in sequence campaigns, and there are no traffic estimates. Use audience targeting carefully, and start with a modest budget while you learn how the sequence behaves.

Iterate one step at a time. If step 2 underperforms, replace just step 2 and keep the rest. Changing everything at once makes it impossible to learn which part was the problem. For writing the short steps, the 6-second bumper ad guide helps.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches open loops and story structure?

The Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops and structure at £1,720 one-time. Google's sequencing help and ABCDs are free.

Which ad formats work in a sequence?

Skippable in-stream, non-skippable in-stream and bumper ads, or a combination.

Can I target keywords in a sequence campaign?

No. Google says you can't target keywords, topics or placements, though you can exclude them.

How often will someone see the sequence?

By default, once per person every 7 days; you can choose 30 days.
