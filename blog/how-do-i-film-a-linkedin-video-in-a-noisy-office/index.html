How do I film a LinkedIn video in a noisy office?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The fix for a noisy office is mostly about the microphone and the room, not editing software. Put a microphone close to your mouth, film in the quietest corner you can find at the quietest time of day, and add captions so the message survives even if some noise gets through. Software noise reduction is a rescue tool, not a plan.

Key takeaways

  • LinkedIn's video tips, as summarised by Social Media Today, say to "use microphones to cut out background noise", to keep the camera steady, to record "horizontally or vertically – but not both", and to "make the first six seconds count" (Social Media Today).
  • The same summary says to "add subtitles if you can". Captions are also the accessibility route: WCAG says captions "convey not only the content of spoken dialogue" but also meaningful sound (W3C).
  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded" (Dartmouth). Background chatter is extraneous sound.
  • YouTube's retention report treats dips as moments where "viewers are abandoning or skipping" (YouTube Help). Where you publish the same video on YouTube, a dip at a noisy moment is a clue.

Why noise hurts more than you think

Viewers forgive an average camera far more readily than bad audio. A shaky or soft picture is annoying, but if people cannot hear you, they cannot follow the point at all. In an open-plan office the problem is mixed: constant low hum from ventilation and keyboards, plus intermittent speech from colleagues, which our ears treat as meaningful and so distract from your voice.

The key insight is that noise is a ratio. What matters is how loud you are compared with the room. Moving the microphone closer to your mouth raises your voice relative to everything else, which no filter can fully do afterwards.

Step 1: Choose the room and the time

Before you buy anything, change where and when you film.

  1. Use a meeting room or phone booth if your office has one. Small rooms with soft furnishings absorb sound; glass-walled rooms echo.
  2. If you must film at your desk, face a wall or a corner, not an open floor. Noise reaching the mic comes from behind the camera, so put the busy part of the room behind you and the microphone close to you.
  3. Film early or late. Before most people arrive or after they leave is often the quietest.
  4. Turn off what you control. Pause the printer, close the window by the road, mute notifications.
  5. Do a ten-second test. Record, put on headphones, and listen. Phones lie when played from the speaker.

Step 2: Fix the microphone

LinkedIn's guidance says to use microphones to cut out background noise. That is general advice, and the model that fits depends on your setup.

Whatever you choose, place the mic close to your mouth, typically a hand's width or less for a desk mic, and out of your breath stream so it does not pop. A wind cover or pop filter helps with plosives.

Step 3: Frame and hold the camera steady

LinkedIn recommends keeping things steady with a tripod where possible. A steady frame also lets you crop in tighter, which brings your face and any desk microphone closer. Choose vertical or horizontal once and stick to it; the tip is to record one orientation, not both. Looking straight into the lens, with the lens at eye level, makes the video feel direct without needing extra production.

Step 4: Plan the video for a noisy environment

If you cannot control the room fully, design the video to survive it.

  • Keep it short. Fewer seconds means fewer chances for an interruption. Edit to the point.
  • Script the first line. The first six seconds count, so deliver your hook cleanly; do not start before the room is quiet.
  • Use a retake rhythm. Say a sentence, pause, listen for noise, repeat if needed. Pauses can be cut out.
  • Add on-screen text for the key point. If a loud interruption hits one word, the text still carries it.
  • Avoid numbers and names in noisy moments. They are hardest to guess from context.

Step 5: Repair what you can in editing

Editing can reduce steady noise (hum, hiss) much better than sudden noise (voices, clatter). Use the built-in noise reduction in your editor lightly: heavy settings make voices sound underwater. Then:

  1. Cut around interruptions. A cut and a short zoom can hide a retake.
  2. Lower the music if you use any, so the voice stays on top. Better still, use none in a noisy video.
  3. Add captions and check them. Captions help sound-off viewers and give a second path to the words. Auto-generated captions make mistakes, especially with names and jargon; proofread them.
  4. Listen on phone speakers and earbuds. Many viewers use both.

If the audio is not good enough after this, re-record. A short re-shoot beats a long upload nobody finishes.

Example: a founder's 60-second update

Example: a founder films a product update at their desk in an open office at 1 pm. The raw clip has colleagues talking behind and a loud laugh in the middle. Second attempt: the founder books a meeting room at 8:30 am, plugs in a wired lavalier, uses a tripod at eye level, and records in vertical. They script one sentence for the first six seconds, pause between sentences, and cut the pauses. They add captions and check that the product name is spelled correctly. The result is shorter and clearer than the first take. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Common mistakes

  • Fixing it in software only. Close-up audio beats any filter.
  • Heavy noise reduction. Voices sound processed and tiring.
  • Filming near a glass wall. Echo is hard to remove.
  • Skipping the headphone test. Problems are invisible on a phone speaker.
  • Starting before the room is quiet. The hook is the worst place for noise.
  • Unchecked captions. Wrong names look careless.

FAQ

What is the cheapest fix for a noisy office video?

Move the microphone closer, even if that means wired earbuds with a mic, and film in a quieter room or time. Placement beats price.

Can editing software remove office noise?

It can reduce steady noise such as hum. Voices and sudden sounds are much harder to remove, so prevent them at recording.

Should I add captions to a LinkedIn video?

LinkedIn's tips say to add subtitles if you can, and captions help people watching without sound. Proofread them.

Vertical or horizontal for LinkedIn?

LinkedIn advises choosing one and not recording both. Pick the one that suits your content and where you post.
