What does a spike in the middle of a YouTube retention graph tell me to do?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 25 November 2026 · Last updated 25 November 2026

A spike in the middle of the graph means more viewers are watching, rewatching or sharing that moment. Check what is on screen. If it is a strong beat, repeat it earlier and in other videos. If viewers are rewinding to catch something, slow it down or explain it better.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube Help says "spikes appear when more viewers are watching, rewatching, or sharing those parts of your video" and that a spike can also mean the content was not clear and viewers had to rewatch.
  • Search Engine Journal reports that YouTube's example of spikes was UI demo segments, which led the channel to plan more of them.
  • YouTube Help also says the absolute number of views for a segment can exceed a video's overall view count because of rewatching.
  • Decide first whether the spike is a strength or confusion. The edit is opposite for each.

Two meanings for one shape

A spike looks like good news, and often it is. It tells you that, at that moment, more people were watching than at the moments around it. YouTube Help lists the reasons: viewers watching, rewatching or sharing. But the same page also says a spike can mean your content was not clear and the audience had to rewatch a section. You have to look at the video to tell which.

A third possibility is a skip target. Some viewers jump forward to find a specific moment, such as a result or a step. That can show as a bump later in the graph and a dip before it. Check what viewers are seeking.

How to tell a strength from confusion

Watch the spike section at normal speed, as a viewer would.

  • If it is a payoff, a reveal, a strong example, a joke or a visual that stands out, it is likely a strength.
  • If it is a fast step, a dense explanation, a small text on screen or a quick transition, it is likely a rewatch for clarity.
  • If it is a short clip that someone would share, such as a quote or a result, it may be a share moment.
  • If viewers are skipping to it, your opening may be hiding the thing they want.

Steps to act on a spike

  1. Note the exact time. Use the graph and the key moments card.
  2. Watch the section in context. What changes at that moment? Look at picture, sound and script.
  3. Label it: strength, confusion or skip target.
  4. If a strength, preview it. Put a short glimpse in the hook so viewers know it is coming. YouTube notes that audience numbers decrease over a video, so strong content earlier reaches more people.
  5. If confusion, clarify. Slow the step, increase text size, add a label or split it into two moves.
  6. If a skip target, move it up. Put the thing viewers want nearer the start.
  7. Reuse the pattern. Look for what the strong moment does that others do not, and repeat it.
  8. Check across videos. Search the channel-level report for spikes, as Search Engine Journal reports YouTube describes. One spike is a clue; a pattern is evidence.

What not to do

Do not treat every spike as a trophy. A spike made of rewinding is a sign of friction. Do not rebuild a whole video around one bump; test the idea in the next upload and look at the graph again. Do not forget the dips next to it: a spike that follows a deep dip may show that viewers jumped ahead.

And do not compare spikes across videos of very different lengths without care. Typical retention is a better yardstick for the shape of the whole line, while spikes are best read as local clues.

The YouTube Help page on key moments adds that top moments are where almost no one dropped off, and that spikes and dips are worth reviewing together (YouTube Help).
