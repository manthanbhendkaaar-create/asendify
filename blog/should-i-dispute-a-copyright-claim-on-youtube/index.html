Should I dispute a copyright claim on YouTube?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 19 November 2026 · Last updated 19 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Only dispute a YouTube copyright claim if you are confident you have all the rights to the content, your use falls under an exception such as fair use, or the claim is a mistake. Giving credit, owning a copy or not monetising are not valid reasons. A weak dispute can turn a harmless claim into a removal request and a copyright strike, so removing the claimed content is often the safer fix.

Key takeaways

  • A Content ID claim usually does not affect your channel; a copyright strike does.
  • YouTube lists three valid dispute grounds: you have all the necessary rights, an exception such as fair use applies, or the claim was misidentified.
  • After a dispute, the claimant has 30 days to release, reinstate, file a removal request, or let it expire.
  • A valid removal request after a dispute removes the video and gives your channel a strike.
  • "YouTube doesn't mediate copyright disputes", so if you are unsure, get legal advice or remove the claimed part.

Claims vs strikes

YouTube's page on Content ID claims explains that a claim appears when your upload matches material in Content ID. The rights owner can block, monetise or track the video, possibly differently by country, and claims "usually don't affect your channel or account". How Content ID works adds that every upload is scanned against files submitted by owners who hold exclusive rights to a substantial body of frequently uploaded material.

A strike is different. YouTube's copyright strike basics say a strike means a legal removal request led to your content being taken down. Three strikes can terminate your account and linked channels. A strike expires after 90 days if you complete Copyright School and have fewer than three.

What happens after you dispute

YouTube's page on disputing a Content ID claim sets out the process. The claimant has 30 days to respond and can:

  1. Release the claim: monetisation returns once all claims are released.
  2. Reinstate the claim: your dispute is rejected; you may be able to appeal.
  3. Submit a copyright removal request: if valid, the video is removed and your channel gets a strike.
  4. Let it expire: no response in 30 days releases the claim.

Some blocking claims offer "Escalate to Appeal", which skips the dispute step; the claimant then has 7 days to respond. YouTube also notes that a submitted dispute cannot be cancelled, and that repeated or abusive disputes can bring penalties.

Worked example: two claims on one video

Example: a gaming channel gets two claims on a 20-minute video.

  • Claim 1: 45 seconds of a chart song playing in the background of a stream. The creator has no licence. Decision: use Erase song to mute the segment. The claim clears; no dispute, no risk.
  • Claim 2: a match on the channel's own intro music, which the creator commissioned and holds a written licence for. Decision: dispute as misidentified, attaching the licence details. The claimant releases the claim within the 30 days.

The creator disputed only the claim they could prove, and fixed the other one quietly. That is the pattern YouTube's guidance points to.

A note on fair use

Fair use is a US legal doctrine, and other countries use different exceptions; the UK, for example, has narrower "fair dealing" rules. Whether a use qualifies depends on the facts: how much you used, why, and whether it adds commentary or criticism. YouTube's dispute page lists exceptions such as fair use as a valid ground but says it cannot decide for you and suggests legal advice if you are unsure. If you cannot explain in two sentences why your use is transformative, removing the clip is usually the better choice.

How to avoid claims in the first place

  • Use YouTube's Audio Library or properly licensed music.
  • Keep licences and receipts in one folder, named by video.
  • Ask editors to list every track and clip they use.
  • Avoid long clips of TV, sport or films unless you are confident about fair use and have taken advice.
  • Check new uploads in YouTube Studio for claims before publishing publicly where possible.

FAQ

Is there a YouTube agency that edits with licensed music from the start?

Experienced agencies and editors usually build licensing into the edit. Asendify is a retention-editing agency, currently full and invite-only; freelancers using the Audio Library are a fair alternative.

Will a Content ID claim give me a strike?

Usually not. A strike comes from a valid copyright removal request, which can follow a dispute the owner rejects.

How long does the claimant have to respond to a dispute?

30 days for a normal dispute, or 7 days if you used Escalate to Appeal.

Can I cancel a dispute?

No. YouTube says a submitted dispute can't be cancelled, so be sure before you send it.

How do I get rid of a copyright strike?

Complete Copyright School and wait 90 days, ask the claimant to retract, or file a counter notification if the removal was a mistake.
