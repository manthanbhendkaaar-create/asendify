How do you use open loops in a podcast episode?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

You use open loops in a podcast by raising a specific question early ("later, she explains why she turned down a £2 million offer") and answering it later in the episode, so listeners have a reason to keep going. Plant one in the first minute, open a new one before you close the old one, and always pay each one off clearly. Loops that never close feel like a trick and cost you trust.

Key takeaways

  • An open loop is an unanswered question the listener knows will be answered later in the episode.
  • It works because of curiosity: a known gap in knowledge feels uncomfortable until it's filled.
  • Plant the first loop in the first minute, then open the next one just before closing the current one.
  • Be specific. "Stay tuned, there's more" isn't a loop; "later he explains the one email that lost the client" is.
  • Close every loop you open, and keep no more than two or three running at once.

Why open loops work

In his 1994 review of the psychology of curiosity, Carnegie Mellon's George Loewenstein argued that curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge", and that "such information gaps produce the feeling of deprivation labeled curiosity" (quoted in PBS NOVA). An open loop deliberately points at a gap. The listener now knows something exists that they don't know yet, and the cheapest way to fill it is to keep listening.

Apple's guide to podcast trailers uses the same idea: it recommends ending with tension or curiosity, such as "a story problem, a sudden twist, or an unanswered question by a guest" (Apple Podcasts for Creators).

Where to put loops in an episode

Before the endThe teaser"Next week: the investor who said no, and why he was right."

How to plant an open loop

  1. Find the payoff first. In the edit, mark the episode's best answers: the reveal, the number, the lesson, the twist.
  2. Write the question that payoff answers. "How did she lose the client?" "What did the email say?"
  3. Plant it early and specifically. Name the thing without giving it away. Specific loops ("the email") pull harder than vague ones ("something big").
  4. Remind once in the middle. A short callback ("we're getting to that email") keeps the loop alive in a long episode.
  5. Close it clearly. Signal the payoff ("so, the email...") and deliver it in full.
  6. Open the next before closing this one. That way there's never a moment with nothing left to wait for.

Loops in interviews vs solo shows

Interviews: the guest provides the payoffs, so you plant loops in the edit. A cold open from late in the conversation is the strongest loop you have. The host can also plant loops live: "I want to come back to that failure, but first..."

Solo and co-hosted shows: plan loops in the outline. Write your three main points, then decide which one to tease at the start. Ending each segment with a line about the next one keeps the thread pulling.

Narrative shows: loops are the structure. Each scene should leave a question for the next.

Example: adding loops to a 45-minute interview

Example (a hypothetical episode, to show the method): a 45-minute chat with a founder who built and sold a small software company.

  • 0:00: cold open of the founder at minute 31 saying "we had nine days of cash left", cut before what happened.
  • 0:40: host: "We'll get to those nine days. First, how did a teacher end up writing software?"
  • 14:00 (before the ad break): "And then the first big customer asked for something we couldn't build. What we did next was either brilliant or stupid. After this."
  • 31:00: the nine-days story, paid off in full. Just before it ends, the host opens a new question: "Which is why, two years later, selling felt so strange."
  • 42:00: teaser for next week's guest.

Every loop is closed. No more than two are open at once.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Vague teasers. "There's a big surprise coming" sounds like every other show and promises nothing specific.
  • Too many loops. Juggling five unanswered questions confuses listeners. Keep two or three.
  • Never closing. A loop that isn't paid off feels like a cheat, and listeners remember.
  • Overselling. If the loop promises a shock and the payoff is mild, you lose trust. Make the promise match the moment.
  • Loops instead of substance. Loops make a good episode easier to finish. They don't rescue an empty one.

How to check if your loops are working

Look at what happens around ad breaks and section ends. Spotify notes that retention dips "probably occur at the same time as your advertisements", that the curve often "normalizes back to where it was before the ad", and that "steep and sudden changes in retention are the best evidence for deciding if your ad slot needs a new lead-in" (Spotify for Creators). On YouTube, the key-moments view shows dips where "viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part" (YouTube Help). If a loop before the break reduces the dip compared with similar episodes, it's working.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches open loops for podcasts?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops, the payoff ladder and re-hooks for podcasts, clips and YouTube.

How many open loops should a podcast episode have?

Usually one big loop from the start plus one or two smaller ones at a time. Close each before the end.

Is an open loop the same as clickbait?

No. Clickbait promises something that isn't there. An open loop promises something that is, and delivers it.

Should I put an open loop before an ad break?

Yes. A specific question before the break gives listeners a reason to sit through it.

Can I use open loops in a solo podcast?

Yes. Plan them in your outline: tease your best point at the start and end each segment with a line about the next.

What if the guest's best moment comes at the start?

Open with it, then plant a new loop straight away from the next strongest moment.
