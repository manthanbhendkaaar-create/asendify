Should I hire a video ad editor or learn to edit ads myself?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Hire an editor if you need finished ads quickly, you have budget but little time, and your ads need polish you can't produce yourself. Learn to edit if you plan to test many hook variations, want to react fast to your ad data, or can't yet afford a regular editor. Many small advertisers do both: they learn enough to plan, judge and brief edits (hooks, structure, pacing) and hire help for production. Either way, someone on your side needs to understand retention, or you won't know whether an edit is good.

Key takeaways

  • Ad performance depends heavily on creative: a Facebook-commissioned report summarised in 2021 said on average 70% of potential video ad ROI comes from the creative.
  • Testing many openings is now normal practice, which favours having some editing ability in-house.
  • Hiring buys speed and polish; learning buys control and lower cost per variation.
  • You still need to judge edits against data such as hook rate and quartile drop-offs.
  • A clear brief (hook, promise, proof, call to action, length) makes any editor better.

Why creative skill matters

Search Engine Journal's summary of Facebook's video ad tips (2021) reported Facebook's finding that creative quality is the largest factor in video ad performance, with on average 70% of potential ROI coming from the creative and 30% from execution. That's Facebook's own commissioned figure, but it matches what most advertisers see: changing the first few seconds often moves results more than changing targeting.

TikTok's creative effectiveness article recommends "iterating upon a central concept" to get enough creative volume for a long campaign. Iteration is cheap if you can edit, expensive if every version is a new invoice.

A good editing brief

  1. Goal and platform (for example, Reels, sales, 20 seconds, 9:16).
  2. Three hook options to cut as separate versions.
  3. The one promise and the proof shots.
  4. Offer and call to action wording.
  5. Must-haves: captions, brand in frame early, bottom 35% clear on Reels (per Meta's Reels specs).
  6. What the last ad's data showed, and what to fix.

Questions to ask before you decide

Before you hire or start learning, answer these honestly. They usually make the choice obvious.

QuestionPoints towards hiringPoints towards learning
How many ad variations do you need each month?A few polished adsMany hook variations to test
How fast do you need to react to data?Weekly or monthly is fineSame day
Do you enjoy editing, or will it drain your time?You'd rather spend time sellingYou're happy to learn a tool
What's your budget for creative?Enough for regular editing feesTight, so your time is the main resource
Will the skill be needed long term?Ads are a short campaignAds are a permanent part of growth

Start small either way. If you hire, start with a paid trial edit of one ad with three hooks, and judge it on the data, not just how it looks. If you learn, start by cutting new openings for an ad that already works, rather than editing a whole new ad from scratch. Changing the first few seconds is where most of the gains are, as Google's ABCDs suggest with "jump in" and "get to the heart of the story faster".

Decide how you'll judge edits. Agree in advance which numbers decide a winner: hook rate for the opening, played to 50% or ThruPlays for the middle, and cost per result overall. This stops edits being judged on taste, and makes feedback to an editor specific ("viewers leave at second 8; can we show the result earlier?").

Keep the raw footage. Whoever edits, make sure you own and keep the original files, project files and any music licences. That way you can re-cut later, switch editors, or bring editing in-house without starting over.

Review every few months. Many businesses start by hiring, then bring hook variations in-house once they understand what works, keeping the editor for polished main versions. Others start alone and hire once volume grows. Neither is permanent.

Budget for both. Even if you mainly hire, set aside a little time each week to review data and write hooks. That keeps the strategy in your hands.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches retention editing for briefing or editing ads?

The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, pacing and a scorecard at £1,720 one-time. Google's ABCDs and Skillshop are free alternatives.

Is it cheaper to edit ads myself?

Usually per variation, once you've learned. Your time still has a cost.

What should I look for in a video ad editor?

Examples of ads with data, comfort with vertical formats, and willingness to cut multiple hooks.

Can I hire an editor and still test lots of hooks?

Yes. Ask for several openings per ad, or cut extra hooks yourself.
