What should the first seconds of a Steam game trailer show so players keep watching?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Show real gameplay within the first few seconds: the core thing the player does, from the player's view, with the HUD visible. Valve's Steamworks documentation says it highly recommends "that the first trailer you list on your store page is primarily gameplay" and warns that "you may have less than 10 seconds to make an impression", possibly without audio. So skip the studio logo, the slow lore crawl and the black-screen title card. Open on the most characteristic moment of play, make it readable with the sound off, and put the logo and wishlist call at the end.

Key takeaways

  • Valve recommends your first listed trailer is "primarily gameplay", and notes it is "often beneficial to have the in-game HUD elements visible".
  • Store visitors "may have less than 10 seconds" and may be watching "without any audio", per the Steamworks trailer docs.
  • Only the first two trailers show before screenshots, a change Valve made in May 2023.
  • First seconds: the core verb of your game (what the player actually does), not logos, publisher cards or story setup.
  • Make the opening readable muted: clear action, simple on-screen text if needed.
  • Logo, release date and "Wishlist now" belong at the end.

PC Gamer reported the two-trailer limit in May 2023, quoting Valve: "Ideally you'll want to give players a good look at the gameplay of your game." Valve also added video categories (General/Cinematic, Teaser, Gameplay, Interview/Dev Diary).

Why players leave a trailer early

  • Logos first. Studio, publisher and engine cards spend the most valuable seconds on information players don't care about yet.
  • Cinematic without play. Beautiful pre-rendered scenes that don't answer "what do I do in this game?"
  • Slow build. A moody 15-second intro that only makes sense if you already know the game.
  • Unreadable action. Fast cuts of chaotic combat where the viewer can't tell which character is theirs.
  • Text walls. Feature lists or lore paragraphs on screen.

A structure for a 60 to 90 second gameplay trailer

SecondsWhat to showWhy
0 to 5The core loop in its most characteristic moment (a jump, a build, a combo, a heist going wrong)Answers "what is this game?" instantly
5 to 20Two or three variations of that loop: different places, enemies or toolsShows depth without explaining
20 to 45What makes it different: the mechanic, art style or systems no one else hasThe reason to wishlist this game
45 to 70Escalation: bigger moments, harder challenges, a surpriseGives viewers a reason to stay to the end
70 to 90Title, release window or date, platforms, "Wishlist on Steam"The ask, once they care

The length here is a common choice for store trailers, not a Valve rule; Steamworks doesn't set a recommended trailer length. Shorter is fine if your loop reads instantly.

Making the opening work with sound off

  • Pick a shot that explains itself. A tower-defence game: enemies walk a path, towers fire, a wave is held. No narration needed.
  • Use short text cards only where play can't explain. "Every room is generated" or "Co-op for 1 to 4 players", three to six words, large and on screen long enough to read.
  • Keep the camera steady on the opening clip. Readability beats energy for the first seconds.
  • Then use sound as a bonus. Music hits and satisfying sound effects reward people who unmute; don't rely on them for the hook.

Gameplay capture tips

  • Record with a clean build: no debug text, consistent settings, the HUD you'll ship.
  • Play for the camera: deliberate movement, fewer mistakes, the camera pointed where the action is.
  • Capture far more than you need, and log the best moments with timestamps as you go.
  • Export to Valve's specs: up to 1920x1080, 30 or 60 fps, a high bit rate (Valve says 5,000+ Kbps), H.264 video and AAC audio preferred, 16:9 preferred.
  • Choose a custom thumbnail frame that is "representative of unique content in your trailer", and Valve advises you "avoid text" in it.

Example: re-ordering a 95-second trailer

Example: a solo developer's farming-and-dungeon game trailer opens with an 8-second studio logo, a 12-second painted lore intro, then 10 seconds of the farm at sunrise. The first combat appears at 0:30.

The re-cut opens at 0:00 on the player planting a crop that grows into a weapon mid-fight, which is the game's unique idea. Seconds 5 to 20 show three different crops and weapons. The painted art appears at 0:55 as a single beat of story. The logo and "Wishlist now" close the trailer at 1:20. The original lore intro becomes a separate cinematic teaser listed second. FAQ

Is there a book that teaches how to hook viewers in the first seconds?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks and pacing for £1,720 one-time. Steamworks documentation covers Steam's own rules for free.

Should my Steam trailer start with my studio logo?

Valve doesn't ban it, but with "less than 10 seconds to make an impression", most developers move logos to the end.

Can my first Steam trailer be a cinematic?

You can, but Valve recommends the first listed trailer is "primarily gameplay". Put a cinematic second.

How many trailers does Steam show before screenshots?

The first two valid trailers. Additional videos appear after the screenshots.

What resolution should a Steam trailer be?

Up to 1920x1080 at 30 or 60 fps; Valve prefers H.264 with AAC audio and a 16:9 frame.
