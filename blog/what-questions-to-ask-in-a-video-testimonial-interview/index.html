What questions should I ask a customer in a video testimonial interview to get footage people watch?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Ask open questions that walk the customer through three stages (what life was like before, the moment they decided to try you, and what is different now), and ask for specifics at every stage: "What exactly were you doing at the time?", "Can you give me an example?". Ask them to answer in full sentences that include the question, so each clip makes sense on its own. The best testimonial footage is a real person describing a specific moment in their own words; you can't edit that in later if you never recorded it. And in the UK, a testimonial must be genuine, used with permission, and not edited in a misleading way.

Key takeaways

  • Structure the interview as before, turning point, after; that gives the editor a story, not just praise.
  • Open questions only ("Tell me about...", "What happened when..."); yes/no questions produce useless clips.
  • Ask for one concrete example at each stage; specifics are what hold viewers.
  • Ask the customer to repeat the question in the answer ("Before we used X, we...").
  • Record more than you need: 20 to 30 minutes of conversation usually yields a 60 to 90 second testimonial.
  • UK advertising rules (CAP Code 3.45 to 3.52): testimonials must be genuine, with permission, and not edited to mislead.

The rules first

The ASA's guide to testimonials and endorsements summarises the CAP Code:

RuleWhat it saysWhat it means for the interview
3.47Marketers need documentary evidence that a testimonial is genuine, with contact details for the sourceKeep the raw footage and the customer's details
3.48"Testimonials should not be taken out of context or edited in a way which is misleading"Don't stitch half-sentences into claims they didn't make
3.49"Claims within a testimonial must not mislead"If they say "it doubled our sales", you need evidence for that claim
3.50"Marketing communications must not feature a testimonial without permission"Get a signed release before filming

The ASA's separate guidance on claims in testimonials adds that "testimonials alone do not constitute substantiation". If a customer makes an objective claim (time saved, money earned, a health result), you either need evidence for it or should leave it out.

Why so much testimonial footage is unusable

  • Yes/no questions: "Were you happy with the service?" "Yes." Nothing to cut.
  • Generic praise: "They're great, really professional, would recommend." True, but nobody keeps watching that.
  • Answers that need the question: "Oh, about three months, I think." Without hearing the question, the clip means nothing.
  • Scripted lines: a customer reading a quote you wrote sounds exactly like that.
  • No story: praise with no before or after gives the editor no hook and no payoff.

The questions

Warm-up (not for the edit)

  • "Tell me your name, your role and what your business does."
  • "What does a normal week look like for you?"

These relax the person and give you clean name and title shots.

Before

  • "Before you worked with us, what was the problem you were trying to solve?"
  • "What was that costing you, in time, money or stress? Give me an example of a bad day."
  • "What had you already tried?"

The turning point

  • "What made you decide to try something different?"
  • "What nearly stopped you?" (Doubts make testimonials believable.)
  • "What was the first moment you thought this might work?"

After

  • "What's different now? Walk me through a normal week today."
  • "What's one specific thing you can do now that you couldn't before?"
  • "If a friend in your position asked you about this, what would you tell them?"

Pick-ups

  • "Could you say that again, starting with 'Before we used...'?"
  • "Can you sum up the difference in one sentence?"

Never feed them lines. Asking them to rephrase their own point in a full sentence is fine; asking them to say something they didn't believe is not.

How the answers become a video people watch

The editor will usually open with the strongest "before" moment or the most surprising "after" line, then tell the story in order. Because you asked for full sentences and examples, each clip stands alone. A typical result:

SecondsClip
0 to 6"We were losing a whole day a week just chasing invoices."
6 to 25Before: the bad-day example
25 to 40Turning point and the doubt they had
40 to 70After: the specific change, with B-roll of them at work
70 to 85The friend recommendation line

Lines are illustrative. Keep any number a customer quotes only if you can support it.

Example: the same customer, two interviews

Example: an accountancy software company films a bakery owner. In the first interview, the questions are "Do you like the software?" and "Would you recommend it?". The footage is 40 seconds of polite praise.

They return with open questions. She describes doing the books at the kitchen table at midnight on a Sunday, her doubt about moving her records, and the first month she finished her VAT return in an afternoon. The 75-second video opens on the midnight kitchen-table line. The customer approved the final cut. Business and details are illustrative.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches how to open on the strongest moment?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks and pacing for £1,720 one-time. The ASA's guidance on testimonials is free.

Should I send the questions in advance?

Send the topics, not the exact questions, so answers stay natural rather than rehearsed.

Can I edit a customer's words in a testimonial?

You can shorten, but CAP Code rule 3.48 says testimonials must not be "edited in a way which is misleading".

How long should the interview be?

Usually 20 to 30 minutes of conversation for a 60 to 90 second video.

What if the customer makes a claim I can't prove?

Leave it out. The ASA says testimonials alone don't substantiate a claim.
