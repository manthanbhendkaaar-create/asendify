What is the Sharpe ratio and how do traders use it?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 21 November 2026 · Last updated 21 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The Sharpe ratio measures how much return you earned for each unit of risk you took: the average excess return (your return minus a risk-free rate) divided by the standard deviation of those returns. Higher is better. William Sharpe introduced it in 1966 as the "reward-to-variability ratio" for judging mutual funds. Traders use it to compare strategies with different returns and volatility, but it has blind spots, especially for small samples and strategies with rare large losses.

Key takeaways

  • Formula: Sharpe ratio = (average return − risk-free rate) ÷ standard deviation of returns.
  • Sharpe's own example: a stock market with a 6% average annual excess return and 15% standard deviation has a Sharpe ratio of 0.40.
  • It treats upside and downside volatility the same, which can mislead for lopsided strategies.
  • It needs enough data; a ratio from 20 trades is mostly noise.
  • For a personal trading account, R-multiples, expectancy and maximum drawdown are often more practical.

Where it comes from

In The Sharpe Ratio, first published in The Journal of Portfolio Management, William Sharpe writes that he introduced the measure in 1966 under the name "reward-to-variability ratio", and that it later became known as the Sharpe ratio. He builds it on Markowitz's mean-variance approach, which assumes the mean and standard deviation of returns are enough to judge an investment's prospects. Sharpe shared the 1990 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Harry Markowitz and Merton Miller.

In the same article he gives a worked figure: if the stock market's annual excess return has a mean of 6% and a standard deviation of 15%, its Sharpe ratio is 0.40.

How to calculate it

StepWhat you do
1Collect returns for equal periods (daily, weekly or monthly)
2Subtract the risk-free rate for each period (for example, a short-term government bond yield)
3Take the average of those excess returns
4Take the standard deviation of those excess returns
5Divide step 3 by step 4
6Annualise if needed: for monthly data, multiply by the square root of 12

Annualising by the square root of time assumes returns are independent from period to period. Sharpe's article discusses this and other adjustments.

Worked example: two strategies

Example (illustrative): two traders report a year of monthly results. The risk-free rate is taken as 0.3% a month for simplicity.

Trader A made more money on average, but with large swings. Trader B made less but far more steadily, earning about twice as much return per unit of volatility. With leverage, B's steadier strategy could in theory be scaled to match A's return with less volatility, though leverage brings its own risks. The numbers are made up to show the calculation.

Limits of the Sharpe ratio

  • Upside counts as risk. A month with a big gain raises the standard deviation just as a big loss does.
  • Hidden tail risk. Strategies that make small steady gains and occasionally lose a lot (such as selling options) can show a high Sharpe ratio until the big loss arrives.
  • Small samples. A year of monthly data is only 12 numbers.
  • Not comparable across frequencies unless annualised the same way.
  • Easy to flatter by smoothing returns or choosing the period.

Some traders also look at the Sortino ratio, which only counts downside volatility, and at maximum drawdown, the largest peak-to-trough fall.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a daily Sharpe with an annual one.
  • Ignoring the risk-free rate when rates are not near zero.
  • Trusting a high ratio from a short backtest.
  • Using it alone. Always look at drawdowns and the worst month too.

Then use AVERAGE on column D, STDEV on column D, divide the first by the second, and multiply by SQRT(12) to annualise. With fewer than 24 months of data, treat the result as a rough indication only.

Sharpe ratio for a small personal account: a reality check

With one or two years of monthly results, a Sharpe ratio can swing widely from one month to the next. One very good or very bad month changes it a lot. Use it to compare periods and strategies over time, alongside the number of trades and the worst drawdown, rather than as a single score that proves a strategy works.

What is a good Sharpe ratio?

There is no universal cut-off. Sharpe's own stock market example was 0.40 a year; higher means more return per unit of volatility, but check the sample size.

Who invented the Sharpe ratio?

William F. Sharpe, in 1966, originally calling it the reward-to-variability ratio.

What is the difference between Sharpe and Sortino?

Sortino only counts downside volatility; Sharpe counts all volatility.

Can the Sharpe ratio be negative?

Yes, if your average return is below the risk-free rate.

Should I annualise my Sharpe ratio?

Yes, if you compare it with other strategies; use the same frequency and method for all.
