How do I edit a bilingual interview with subtitles so viewers keep watching?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Treat the subtitles as part of the edit, not a layer added at the end. Keep each line short, time it to the speech, put it in one stable position, and edit the translation for reading speed rather than word-for-word accuracy. Cut pauses so viewers are never waiting for the next line. A bilingual interview asks viewers to read and listen at once, and the edit is what makes that feel easy. This is practical editing judgement, so check it on your own retention graph.

Key takeaways

  • The W3C separates the terms: captions are in the same language as the audio, while "translated spoken audio" gives subtitles (W3C WAI). A bilingual interview usually needs both: captions for one speaker and subtitles for the other.
  • W3C also says "automatically-generated captions do not meet user needs or accessibility requirements" unless fully accurate, but "automatic captions can be used as a starting point." Auto-transcribe, then edit by hand, especially for the language you do not speak.
  • For text on screen in ads, TikTok recommends "displaying 5-10 words per second when using text" (TikTok Ads Help). That is ad guidance, not an interview rule, but it is a useful ceiling to test against.
  • YouTube Help says dips in the retention graph mean "viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). Look for dips at the start of long subtitled stretches.

What makes bilingual interviews hard to watch

In a single-language interview, the viewer listens. In a bilingual one, some viewers listen to one speaker and read the other, and some read throughout. Three problems cause most drop-offs:

  1. Subtitles that are too long. A long block of text takes more time to read than the speaker takes to say it, so viewers fall behind and stop trying.
  2. Subtitles that arrive late or leave early. If the text is not on screen while the person is speaking, the viewer loses the link between voice and meaning.
  3. Dead air. The interviewer asks in one language, there is a pause for translation, and the guest answers in another. If the pauses stay in, the pace sags.

Step by step: the subtitle edit

  1. Transcribe first. Use an automatic transcript in each language as a draft. W3C notes automatic captions are only a starting point, so plan to review every line.
  2. Get the translation checked by a speaker of both languages. A machine translation can be literal in ways that sound odd. A human check is the biggest quality jump you can make.
  3. Edit for reading speed. Subtitle the meaning, not every word. Drop repeated phrases and filler. A good subtitle can be shorter than the transcript and still be accurate.
  4. Keep lines short. Two short lines are easier than one long one. Break at natural phrase boundaries, not mid-phrase.
  5. Time the in and out points to the speech. The text should appear as the person starts and clear shortly after they finish. Leave a short gap between subtitles so the eye can reset.
  6. Pick one position and one style, and keep them. Moving the text around makes the viewer search for it.
  7. Distinguish speakers. Use a different colour or label for each language or speaker, applied consistently. Check contrast against your footage.
  8. Export a caption file as well as burned-in text. W3C notes that caption files commonly use WebVTT, with SRT and TTML as other options. A separate file lets viewers turn captions off or choose their language, and burned-in text suits Shorts and social clips.

Pacing the bilingual conversation

  • Cut translation pauses. If an interpreter or host repeats a question in the second language, cut it when the subtitles already carry the meaning.
  • Trim silence between languages. A half-second pause is natural. A three-second pause is a place viewers leave.
  • Use a cutaway when someone reads long text. If a subtitle runs longer than usual, hold a close-up or cutaway so the viewer is not hunting for the speaker's face.
  • Let some lines breathe. If a guest says something emotional in their first language, a subtitle that appears slightly later can let the sound land first. Use this sparingly.

Making the first 30 seconds work

Viewers decide quickly. YouTube Help describes an "Intro" figure showing "what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds" (YouTube Help). For a bilingual interview, that first half-minute must teach viewers how to watch the video:

  • Start on a strong, short line from the guest, already subtitled.
  • Show the subtitle style straight away so viewers know reading is part of the experience.
  • Say in one line what the interview offers, in the viewer's likely first language, then play on.
  • Do not open with a long explanation of the translation set-up. Put it in the description.

How to check it on your retention graph

  1. Publish with burned-in subtitles or a caption track, whichever you plan to keep.
  2. Open the audience retention report and look at the first minute and at any long subtitled answer.
  3. A steep dip at the first long block of text suggests the lines are too long or the pause before them is too slow.
  4. Compare with your last single-language interview if you have one. YouTube lets you compare against videos of similar length.
  5. Change one thing at a time on the next episode: line length, speaker colours or pause trimming.

Example: an interview between a UK host and a guest speaking Hindi

Example: a host speaks English and the guest answers in Hindi. In the first cut, the English translation of each answer appears as a three-line block that stays up for the whole answer, and the editor leaves a two-second pause after each question. In the re-edit, the translation is split into short one- and two-line subtitles timed to each phrase, pauses are trimmed to under a second, and the guest's speaker label has its own colour. The first 20 seconds open on the guest's strongest line. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Common mistakes

  • Subtitling every word, including fillers.
  • Using auto-translation without a human check.
  • Placing subtitles over a busy part of the frame.
  • Changing the font, colour or position between scenes.
  • Skipping a caption file and burning in text only, so viewers cannot choose.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on pacing interview videos?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, pacing and pattern interrupts with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time.

Should I use burned-in subtitles or a caption file?

Both if you can. Burned-in text suits social clips, and a caption file lets viewers choose.

Can I rely on automatic translation?

Use it as a draft only. W3C says automatic captions are a starting point and must be accurate.

How many words should each subtitle have?

There is no universal number. Keep lines short and test them on your retention graph.

Should I cut the interpreter's pauses?

Yes, when the subtitles already carry the meaning.
