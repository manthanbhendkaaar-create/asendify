Key takeaways

  • A capped client list means fewer channels per editor and more time on each channel's data.
  • The trade-off is availability: capped agencies are often full, so you may wait.
  • Ask whether a waitlist is free, whether it needs a deposit, and how places are offered.

Neither model is automatically better. A creator who uploads daily Shorts may need volume. A creator making two long videos a month, where each one matters, may want depth.

Why capacity matters for retention work

Retention-led editing is detailed. It means reading each video's audience retention graph, which YouTube's key moments for audience retention page breaks into flat sections, gradual declines, spikes and dips, plus the intro figure (the share still watching after 30 seconds). It also means connecting those moments to packaging. YouTube's impressions and CTR FAQ warns that a thumbnail with high click-through rate but low average view duration is a sign of clickbait, so the editor has to check the opening keeps the thumbnail's promise.

Doing that properly for every video takes hours. A team with dozens of channels often falls back on a house style. A capped team has more room to treat each channel differently.

How waitlists usually work

Waitlists vary, so ask:

  1. Is it free? Asendify's agency waitlist is free to join.
  2. Is there a deposit? Some agencies take one to hold a place; ask whether and when it is refundable.
  3. How are places offered? In order of joining, or by fit?
  4. How long is the wait? Honest agencies won't promise a date when places depend on clients leaving.
  5. What happens when a place opens? Usually a short check of fit, then onboarding.

What to check before you join any agency

  • Access. It should be through YouTube's channel permissions. Viewer (Limited) lets someone read analytics without seeing revenue data; Editor (Limited) can also manage content. Never hand over a password.
  • Ownership. GOV.UK's guidance on ownership of copyright works says the first owner of commissioned work is whoever created it, unless agreed otherwise in writing. Put ownership or a clear licence in the contract.
  • Reporting. Ask for 30-second hold, average percentage viewed and the biggest drop points, video by video, compared with your earlier videos.
  • Exit terms. How much notice, and do you keep all project files?

While you wait: do the first stage yourself

The first stage of most retention systems is a diagnostic you can start alone:

  1. Pick your last five long videos.
  2. For each, note the share still watching at 30 seconds and the three biggest dips.
  3. Watch each dip and write one line on what was on screen.
  4. Look for repeats: recaps, slow set-ups, sponsor reads, off-topic stories.
  5. Fix the most common one in your next edit and compare the new graph with the old ones.

This costs nothing, and it is the same evidence any good editor will ask for on day one.
