How fast should a 30-second UGC ad be paced?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Pace a 30-second UGC ad by beats, not by a cut count: roughly 0 to 3 seconds for the hook, 3 to 12 for the problem, 12 to 24 for the proof or demo, and 24 to 30 for the action. Cut on sentence ends and on new information, keep it human rather than slick, and change one thing at a time when you test. These timings are editing practice for a starting structure; no source I opened sets a required number of cuts.

Key takeaways

  • TikTok's creative guidance says to "prioritize your hook in the first 6 seconds", to "introduce your content proposition in the first 3 seconds", and to "go for a DIY or not overly polished style so that it fits in with the user-generated content" (TikTok for Business).
  • The same page recommends "5-10 words per second when using text", a useful limit for on-screen words (TikTok for Business).
  • YouTube's retention report says dips mean "viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part", so any video ad you also run on YouTube shows where your pacing loses people (YouTube Help).
  • Wistia's data says videos "under a minute average a 52% engagement rate" (Wistia). That is marketing video in general, not UGC ads in particular, but it supports keeping a short ad tight.

What "fast" should mean for UGC

UGC-style ads work because they feel like a person talking, not an advert. Over-editing destroys that feel. So "fast" should mean "no dead air", not "constant flashing cuts". A person speaking naturally at a normal pace, with cuts that remove pauses and fillers, usually feels right. Flash-cut montages with stock transitions feel like an ad and may lose the style.

A beat map for 30 seconds

BeatTimeJobTypical cuts (practice guide)
Hook0 to 3 sState the problem or result, show the product or face1 to 2
Problem3 to 12 sMake the viewer recognise themselves2 to 4
Proof or demo12 to 24 sShow it working, a before and after, or a real use3 to 6
Action24 to 30 sOne clear next step1 to 2

Totals of about 7 to 14 cuts across 30 seconds are common for this style, but treat them as a starting range and compare with your own results.

How to edit for pace

  1. Transcribe the raw take first. Cut the script on paper, removing every sentence that does not move a beat.
  2. Remove the breaths and fillers. Jump cuts on "um" and long breaths tighten without changing the voice.
  3. Cut on the end of a thought, not mid-word. It feels natural even when quick.
  4. Change the frame every beat. Move from face to product to hands to screen, so each beat has fresh visual information.
  5. Put proof on screen. Show the product working while the voice explains.
  6. Add text sparingly. Short phrases at readable size; stay under 10 words per second.
  7. Keep audio constant. Quiet music under the voice; do not let the music drive cuts.
  8. Watch it without sound, then with sound. Both versions should make sense.

Pace by audience attention

A product with a clear, visual demo, such as a cleaner or gadget, can pace quicker because the screen does the explaining. A product that needs trust, such as skincare or a financial app, usually needs a slightly slower, more personal delivery. Do not copy a pacing style from a different category without testing.

Example: a stain remover ad

Example: a brand edits a 30-second UGC ad for a stain remover. Draft one is 26 cuts, with music-driven flashes between product shots; the creator's voice becomes hard to follow. Draft two is 11 cuts. The hook is the creator holding a stained shirt and saying the problem in one line. The problem beat shows two failed attempts, the proof shows the stain lifting in one continuous shot, and the action beat is a single line and button text. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result. The team runs both versions to a small audience and compares view-through rates on the platform's own dashboard, changing nothing else.

Mistakes that make UGC feel like an ad

  • Over-polished colour grading and stock transitions.
  • Music louder than the voice.
  • Captions that duplicate every word in big, flashing animation.
  • A hard sell from the first second.
  • Cutting out every pause so the person sounds robotic.

How to test pace

Pick one ad and make two versions that differ only in pace, for example one with the dead air removed and one with all cuts at a fixed interval. Run them with the same audience and budget and compare the platform's own view-through or watch-time numbers. Do not draw conclusions from a handful of impressions; wait for a sample you would trust, and be aware that creative fatigue and audience differences can confound results. If you also run the ad on YouTube, use the retention graph to see where people leave.

Pacing checklist

  • Is the problem or result clear within three seconds?
  • Does each beat change the visual?
  • Have you removed fillers without removing personality?
  • Is on-screen text under ten words a second?
  • Does it make sense with sound off?
  • Is there exactly one action at the end?

Keeping a simple log

Whatever you change, write it down. A table with the date, the video, the single change you made, the metric you watched and what happened takes two minutes to update and saves a great deal of guessing later. Include a column for what else was different, such as a bigger topic, a holiday week or a different upload time, because those confound results. After five or six entries you will see which changes helped repeatedly and which only looked good once. Treat any single result as a hint rather than proof, and only promote a change to your standard routine once it has held up on more than one video.

How many cuts should a 30-second UGC ad have?

Roughly 7 to 14 is a common starting range, but test it.

Should I cut out every pause?

No. Remove dead air and fillers but keep the person's natural rhythm.

Should the ad have music?

Quiet music under the voice is typical, but test sound-off performance too.

How do I know if the pace works?

Compare view-through or retention across versions that differ only in pace.
