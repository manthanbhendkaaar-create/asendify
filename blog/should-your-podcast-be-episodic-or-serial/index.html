Should your podcast be episodic or serial?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Choose episodic if each episode stands on its own, such as interviews, advice or news, and serial if the story only makes sense from the start, such as a narrative investigation or a course. Episodic shows let new listeners start anywhere; serial shows ask more of them but can pull them through every episode in order. Pick the one that matches how people will actually listen.

Key takeaways

  • Apple defines episodic shows as ones "that can be listened to in any order" and serial shows as "designed to be listened to in sequential order".
  • The setting changes the show page: episodic shows list the newest episode first, serial shows list episode 1 first.
  • Episodic suits interviews and topic shows; serial suits stories, investigations and step-by-step courses.
  • Serial shows live or die on the end of each episode. Every episode must leave a question the next one answers.
  • Many shows mix the two: an episodic feed with occasional multi-part series inside it.

What Apple means by episodic and serial

Apple's guidance is short and clear. "Episodic shows are those that can be listened to in any order", and their episodes are shown "in reverse chronological order with the newest episode at the top of the show page." "Serial shows are designed to be listened to in sequential order — from the first episode to the last", and their episodes are presented "in sequential order with the first episode at the top", with episode numbers shown above the title instead of the date (Apple Podcasts for Creators).

Apple also recommends putting episode and season numbers in the metadata fields, not in the episode title: "Do not include episode or season numbers in the episode title." For serial shows with seasons, the show page displays the newest season by default.

How the choice affects retention

Inside an episode, the job is the same either way: get to the point fast and give people a reason to keep going. Spotify notes that "listeners have a tendency to drop off early, so it's crucial to capture people's attention as quickly as possible" (Spotify for Creators).

Between episodes, the two formats behave differently. An episodic show earns its next listen through habit and trust: people come back because the last episode was worth it. That's why a steady schedule matters so much for topic shows. Spotify warns that when creators don't keep a steady schedule, it can be the first sign of "podfading" (Spotify for Creators).

A serial show earns its next listen through the story. Spotify's production guide quotes a producer who suggests thinking of the story "as a series of questions you are creating in the mind of the listener" (Spotify for Creators). In a serial, those questions run across episodes, not just within them. If episode 1 answers everything, there is no reason to press play on episode 2.

How to choose

Ask three questions:

  1. Would episode 7 make sense to someone who never heard episodes 1 to 6? If yes, you're episodic. If no, you're serial.
  2. How will most new listeners find you? If they arrive through a single interview a guest shared, episodic is kinder. A serial show sends them back to the start, which some will do and some won't.
  3. Can you keep the thread going for every episode? A serial needs a planned arc and a reason to continue at every ending. If you can't plan that far ahead, start episodic and run a short series inside the feed when you have a story big enough.

Example: the same topic, two formats

Example (hypothetical, to show the difference): a show about small businesses that nearly failed.

  • Episodic version: each week a different owner tells their story in 40 minutes. Episode titles name the business and the crisis. A new listener can start with whichever title grabs them. Retention work focuses on the cold open, open loops inside the interview and a strong ending.
  • Serial version: one bakery's year, told over eight episodes. Episode 1 ends with the landlord's letter arriving, unopened. Episode 2 opens with what it said. The show page lists episode 1 first, numbered. Retention work focuses on the cliffhanger at the end of every episode, plus a 30-second "previously" at the start of each one so returning listeners don't get lost.

Both can work. The mistake is setting a story-driven show to episodic, so new listeners start at episode 6 and feel lost, or setting an interview show to serial, so they get sent to a two-year-old first episode.

Mixing the formats

Plenty of episodic shows run occasional multi-part series. If you do, make the order obvious: put "Part 1" and "Part 2" language in the episode description, link the parts to each other, and open part 2 with a short recap. Keep the show type set to what most of the feed is.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches cliffhangers for podcasts?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops, re-hooks and endings that pull people into the next episode.

Can I change my show from episodic to serial later?

Yes, the show type is a setting in your hosting provider or Apple Podcasts Connect. Changing it reorders the show page, so add episode numbers first.

Should I put "Episode 12" in my episode titles?

Apple says not to include episode or season numbers in the title; put them in the metadata fields instead.

Are interview podcasts ever serial?

Rarely. An interview series built around one story or one course can be, but most interview shows are episodic.

Do serial podcasts need seasons?

No, but seasons help. With season numbers, Apple shows the newest season by default, which suits a show that tells one story per season.

What if listeners start a serial in the middle?

Add a short recap at the start of each episode and say in the description where to begin.
