How long should an explainer animation be?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Most explainer animations work best between about one and two minutes for a single idea, and up to roughly five minutes for a fuller product or process explainer. The right length is set by the number of ideas the viewer must grasp and by where the video will be seen, not by a fixed rule. If the film needs more than five minutes, split it into a series or add chapters, and let your audience's retention graph confirm the choice.

Key takeaways

  • Wistia's analysis says "the 1-5 minute range is the sweet spot" and that it is "ideal for explainer videos, product overviews, or quick feature walkthroughs", while "engagement naturally declines after the five-minute mark" (Wistia). It is a broad marketing dataset, not a study of animation alone.
  • The same article reports that "videos under a minute average a 52% engagement rate" (Wistia), so very short cuts can suit awareness and social placements.
  • Mayer's segmenting principle says "people learn better when the information is presented in user-paced segments, rather than in one continuous unit" (Dartmouth). Longer explainers benefit from clear sections.
  • YouTube lets you compare "your 10 latest videos of similar length" using typical retention (YouTube Help), a way to test your own length choices.

These figures are planning ranges from editing practice, anchored loosely by the 1-5 minute range Wistia reports. Your own audience may differ.

The script test

Animation length is script length. A common narration planning range is about 120 to 160 words per minute, which gives roughly 120 to 160 words for a one-minute film and 240 to 320 for two minutes. That is a practical estimate, not a standard. Write the script first, count the words, and set the length from the ideas, not the other way round.

To decide how many ideas fit:

  1. List every point you want to make.
  2. Mark each as essential, helpful or nice to have.
  3. Keep only essential points for the first cut.
  4. Estimate 20 to 40 seconds per idea including visuals and pauses. This is a rough planning figure.
  5. Add 10 to 15 seconds for the hook and 5 to 10 for the call to action.
  6. Total the time. If it exceeds five minutes, split.

Where the video will live

  • Landing page or homepage. Visitors decide in seconds; lean shorter.
  • YouTube. Viewers choosing to watch tolerate a bit more, but check the graph.
  • Social feeds. Cut shorter, vertical versions.
  • Email or sales follow-ups. Short, with a clear single point.
  • Training or onboarding. Longer is fine if sectioned and searchable.

How to cut length without losing meaning

  • Start with the problem, not the company history.
  • Cut transitions that do not explain anything.
  • Replace a spoken list with a visual.
  • Cut examples that repeat a point.
  • Move edge cases to documentation.
  • Speed up holds on static diagrams.
  • Replace long intros with a single line.

Example: an invoicing tool explainer

Example: a studio is asked for a "two-minute" explainer for an invoicing tool. The script lists nine features and runs to 480 words. The studio keeps three: send, track, remind. It adds a hook about late payments and a short call to action, and the script falls to about 260 words, or just under two minutes. The other six features become short clips for social and a help centre page. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result. After publishing, the studio checks the retention graph and compares with another explainer of similar length.

Using the graph to refine length

After publishing, look for the point where the graph falls steeply. If it falls near the end of a section, check whether the section overstayed. If the whole graph falls evenly, the length may be fine and the hook or topic is the issue. YouTube says dips mean "viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part" (YouTube Help). Remember that a shorter film with a higher percentage is not always better, since watch time and the action taken afterwards also matter.

Mistakes

  • Fixing the length first and filling or cramming to fit.
  • Putting every feature in one film.
  • Slow openings.
  • Ignoring where the video will play.
  • No chapters in a longer film.

Checklist

  • Ideas listed and prioritised.
  • Script written and counted.
  • Length matches purpose and placement.
  • Longer than five minutes: chaptered or split.
  • Hook and one call to action only.
  • Graph checked after publishing.

Reviewing your own data

Keep a simple record of each explainer you publish: length, purpose, placement, average view duration and any action taken afterwards, such as sign-ups or clicks. After four or five films, patterns usually appear. You may find that your homepage film does best at ninety seconds while a YouTube walkthrough holds viewers well for four minutes. That local evidence is worth more than any general range, including the ones on this page.

Splitting a long explainer into a series

When a topic honestly needs more than five minutes, a series often beats one long film. Give each episode one question and one answer, a short recap line at the start that tells new viewers what they need to know, and a teaser at the end that points to the next episode. Name the episodes so each makes sense in search on its own, and add chapters inside any episode over a few minutes. Publish a playlist so viewers can move through in order. This follows Mayer's segmenting idea in practice: each episode is a segment the viewer can take at their own pace. Check each episode's graph separately, since a series sometimes shows a healthy first episode and a falling second, which tells you where to improve.
