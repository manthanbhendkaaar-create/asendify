How do I know when a video ad has creative fatigue?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A video ad has creative fatigue when the same people have seen it too often and stop paying attention: frequency climbs, hook rate and click-through rate fall, and cost per result rises, even though nothing else changed. Watch those numbers week by week against the ad's own best period. The fix is usually not a brand-new idea but a fresh version of the one that worked: a new opening, a different first frame or a new person on screen, with the proven middle kept.

Key takeaways

  • TikTok defines creative fatigue as "when audiences become overexposed to ads, causing diminished attention, sentiment, and long-term brand-building potential."
  • TikTok's article cites System1 research that ads creating happiness hold up at heavy frequency at twice the rate of surprise.
  • Google Ads lets you cap how often a video campaign's ads show to the same person per day, week or month.
  • The typical pattern: frequency up, hook rate down, then clicks and conversions down.
  • Refresh the hook first; keep what still works in the body.

Compare against the ad's own best weeks, not against other ads. For how to calculate the attention ratios, see hook rate and hold rate in video ads.

What the platforms say

TikTok's article on creative effectiveness describes fatigue as overexposure that reduces attention and sentiment. It recommends building on a strong concept rather than constantly inventing new ones ("iterating upon a central concept can give brands the volume they need for a prolonged campaign"), and cites System1's 2025 research that happiness sustains effectiveness at heavy frequency at twice the rate of surprise, and that brand recognition in the first 2 seconds was linked to 19% less attention decay. These are System1's findings as reported by TikTok, from a study of heavy TikTok users, so treat them as directional.

Search Engine Land carried a practitioner's view that a TikTok ad's "half-life" is shorter than on other platforms, suggesting filming five to seven hook variations per concept. It's opinion based on agency experience, not a study, but the advice to plan variations in advance is sensible on any platform.

Slowing fatigue down

Cap frequency. Google's frequency capping help lets you limit how often a video campaign's ads appear to the same person, by impressions, views or both, per day, week or month. Caps for video campaigns are set at campaign level and apply to in-stream and bumper ads in the auction.

Broaden the audience. A small audience sees the same ad more often. A wider one spreads impressions.

Make the ad enjoyable. Ads people like wear out more slowly than ads that rely on a single surprise.

Brand early. Recognising the brand quickly helps the ad keep working even when people have seen it.

How to refresh without starting over

KeepChange
The core offer and proofThe first 3 seconds: new first frame, new opening line
The structure that held viewersThe person on screen, setting or angle
The call to action wording that convertedColour, music, text style

A single shoot can supply many variations if you film several openings for the same body. The guide to the first 3 seconds of a video ad lists opening types to rotate: the result, a specific number, a contradiction, a reveal in progress, or the viewer's exact question.

Worked example

By week 3, frequency has nearly doubled and hook rate has dropped six points while the offer and audience stayed the same: classic fatigue. The team launches three new openings on the same body. The best one restores hook rate to 26% at a frequency of 1.5 in its first week, and the old version is paused.

Planning creative so fatigue doesn't hurt

The best defence against fatigue is planning variations before you need them.

Shoot for variation. In one session, film the main body once and record four or five different openings, two people or settings if possible, and a few extra product shots. This gives you weeks of fresh versions without another shoot.

Keep a refresh calendar. Rather than waiting for results to collapse, schedule a check every week of frequency, hook rate and cost per result for each live ad. Have the next version ready when the warning signs appear.

Planned assetUse
4 to 5 hooks per bodyRotate as each tires
2 presenters or settingsMake the ad feel new without new messaging

Seasonal versionsTimely refresh with the same core

Retire gracefully. When a refreshed version beats the old one, pause the old ad rather than letting both compete for the same audience. Keep the old ad's data for future reference.

For which openings to rotate, see how to A/B test video ad hooks.

Don't confuse fatigue with seasonality. If results drop across all ads at once, check for seasonal changes or a market shift before assuming every ad is tired.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches how to make fresh hooks?

The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks and pacing at £1,000 one-time. TikTok's creative articles and Google's ABCDs are free alternatives.

What frequency causes ad fatigue?

There's no universal number. Watch for the point where hook rate and CTR start falling as frequency rises.

Should I pause a fatigued ad?

Launch a refreshed version first, then pause the old one once the new one is delivering.

Does fatigue happen faster on TikTok?

Some practitioners say so, but that's experience rather than published data. Track your own numbers.
