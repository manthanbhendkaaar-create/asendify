Key takeaways

  • YouTube's key moments report shows "how well different moments of your video held viewers' attention".
  • Four shapes matter: the intro drop, dips, spikes and the slope. YouTube defines dips as moments where "viewers are abandoning or skipping" and spikes as moments "rewatched or shared".
  • Compare like with like. Typical retention compares your 10 latest videos of similar length; relative retention compares with all YouTube videos of similar length.
  • For Shorts, start with "stayed to watch", the share who "stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short".

The four shapes and what they mean for editors

ShapeWhat YouTube saysLikely editing causeFix to test
Steep intro dropIntro shows who stayed past 30 secondsSlow start, unclear promise, mismatch with titleHook first; match the title's promise
DipViewers "abandoning or skipping"Repetition, tangent, sponsor, static shotTrim, re-hook before it, add visual change
Spike"Rewatched or shared", or unclear and rewatchedA great moment, or a confusing oneKeep great ones; clarify confusing ones
Steep overall slopeSteady loss through the videoPacing too slow throughoutTighten every section, more variety
Flat end then dropOutro reachedLong outroShorten; one next video

How to read a graph in 10 minutes

  1. Open the video's audience retention report in YouTube Studio.
  2. Check the intro figure first, and compare with typical retention.
  3. Note the three biggest dips and their timestamps.
  4. Play 15 seconds before each dip. What changed on screen or in the audio?
  5. Check spikes. Was it a highlight, or did viewers rewind because they missed something?
  6. Write one fix per finding for the next edit.

Common mistakes reading graphs

  • Comparing a 30-minute video with a 6-minute one.
  • Treating every spike as good.
  • Fixing everything at once, so you can't tell what worked.
  • Ignoring packaging. A steep intro drop can come from a title that over-promised.

Reporting graph findings to a creator or client

Editors often spot the problem in a graph but struggle to explain it. A short, consistent format helps.

SectionContent
HeadlineOne sentence: "Viewers leave during the 40-second intro."
EvidenceScreenshot of the graph with the dip marked, plus intro figure vs typical retention
CauseWhat was on screen at that moment
FixOne change for the next video
Follow-upWhich number you'll check next time

Keep it to one page. Creators and clients act on one clear finding far more readily than on ten. Use plain words rather than jargon: "people left here" is clearer than "negative retention delta".

Be honest about uncertainty. A dip might come from the edit, the topic or the title, and a single video rarely proves which. Saying "this is our best guess; we'll check it on the next two videos" builds more trust than certainty you can't back up.

FAQ

Is there a book on reading YouTube retention graphs?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook links graph patterns to editing fixes, with a tracker, for £1,720 one-time. YouTube's Help pages explain the metrics free.

What's the difference between typical and relative retention?

Typical compares with your 10 latest videos of similar length; relative compares with all YouTube videos of similar length.

Is a spike always good?

No. YouTube notes a spike can mean a section was unclear and viewers rewatched it.

How many videos should I compare?

Several of similar length. One video can be an outlier.

Which metric matters most for Shorts?

Start with "stayed to watch", then average percentage viewed.

Can I see retention for a competitor's video?

No. Retention graphs are private to the channel owner. The public "most replayed" graph on some videos shows popular moments, but not where viewers left.
