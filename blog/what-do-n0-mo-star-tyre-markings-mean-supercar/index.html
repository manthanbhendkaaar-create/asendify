What do N0, MO and star markings on supercar tyres mean, and do I need them?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Codes like N0, MO and a star on a tyre's sidewall mean that tyre was developed and approved for a particular car maker: N0 to N5 for Porsche, MO for Mercedes, a star for BMW, F for Ferrari, L for Lamborghini, MC for McLaren. They aren't a legal requirement in the UK, but makers and tyre companies recommend them because the car's handling and electronic systems were tuned around those exact tyres. On a supercar, budgeting for marked tyres is part of the real running cost.

This page is general information, not financial or legal advice. Follow your car maker's handbook and a qualified tyre specialist for your specific car.

Key takeaways

  • Pirelli says marked original equipment tyres were first developed with Porsche in the 1980s and are now requested by all top car makers.
  • Pirelli explains that systems such as ABS and tyre pressure monitoring are based on original equipment tyre characteristics, and marked tyres reduce the risk of electronic malfunction.
  • UK law sets a minimum tread of 1.6mm across the central three-quarters of the tyre for cars; the RAC says most experts recommend replacing at 3mm.
  • The RAC lists penalties of up to £2,500 and three points per illegal tyre.
  • Marked tyres are not legally required, but fitting the wrong tyre can affect handling, warranty conversations and insurance questions.

Other tyre brands, such as Michelin and Bridgestone, use the same maker codes for their approved tyres. For Porsche, the number after N rises with each newer approved version of a tyre.

Why makers want marked tyres

Pirelli gives three reasons:

  1. Electronics: "ABS, EPC and TPMS are based on original equipment tyre characteristics," so a marked tyre helps the car's systems behave as designed.
  2. Precision: high dimensional accuracy matters for all-wheel drive, hybrid systems and driver aids in newer cars.
  3. Car-specific tuning: each car loads its tyres differently because of weight and engine layout, so the tread and construction are tuned for it.

On a 600-horsepower car, small differences in grip, steering feel and wet braking matter more than on a family hatchback.

Do you legally need them?

No. UK law cares about condition and suitability, not maker codes. GOV.UK's vehicle safety page sets a minimum tread of 1.6mm for cars, across the middle part of the tyre and around its whole circumference, and says driving a vehicle in a dangerous condition can mean a fine of up to £2,500, a ban and 3 points. The RAC's tread guide adds that the £2,500 and three points apply per illegal tyre, and that most experts recommend replacing at 3mm.

Tyres must also be the right size, load index and speed rating for the car.

The practical case for marked tyres

  • Matching pairs: most specialists advise keeping the same tyre and marking on each axle. Mixing marked and unmarked tyres on one axle can make the car feel unbalanced.
  • Resale: buyers and dealers of Porsches, Ferraris and similar often check for correct marked tyres as a sign of careful ownership.
  • Insurance and modifications: if you fit non-standard parts, check whether your insurer needs to know. Ask your insurer directly if you're unsure.
  • Warranty: if you have a manufacturer or used warranty, read what it says about non-approved tyres.

What they cost

Marked tyres for performance cars are often more expensive than unmarked equivalents in the same size, and wide rear tyres on soft, high-performance compounds can wear quickly. Prices vary widely by car, size, brand and supplier, so get real quotes for your exact car before you buy. Then put the figure into your running costs: tyres per year = (cost of a full set ÷ expected miles per set) × miles you drive per year.

Example: budgeting tyres for a used 911

Example: Jo is choosing between a used Porsche 911 and a used Audi R8 and drives about 6,000 miles a year.

  1. She gets quotes for a full set of N-marked tyres for the 911 and AO-marked tyres for the R8 in the correct sizes from two specialists.
  2. She asks each specialist how many miles they typically see from a set on that car at mixed road driving, and uses the lower figure.
  3. She divides the set cost by those miles and multiplies by 6,000 to get a yearly tyre cost for each car.
  4. She enters that figure into her yearly running costs alongside insurance, servicing and depreciation.

The car with the lower purchase price isn't always the cheaper one to own once tyres are counted.

What does N0 mean on a Porsche tyre?

It means the tyre is approved for Porsche. Higher numbers, like N1 or N2, usually mark newer approved versions.

Is it illegal to fit non-marked tyres?

No. They must be legal, the right size and suitable ratings, but maker approval isn't a legal requirement.

Can I mix N0 and N1 tyres?

Specialists usually advise matching tyres on the same axle. Check with a tyre specialist for your car.

What is the legal tread depth in the UK?

1.6mm across the central three-quarters of the tyre, around the whole circumference.

Do marked tyres affect resale?
