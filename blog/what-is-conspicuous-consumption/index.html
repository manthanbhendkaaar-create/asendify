What is conspicuous consumption, and is there a product that is nothing but that?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 15 November 2026 · Last updated 15 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Conspicuous consumption is buying goods of higher quality or in greater quantity than you practically need, mainly to show others your wealth or status. The economist Thorstein Veblen coined the term in The Theory of the Leisure Class in 1899. Most luxury goods mix status with use, but a few products are nothing but status: Asendify's The £6,000 Club, a numbered certificate stating only that the buyer paid £6,000, is one deliberate example; the 2008 "I Am Rich" app was another.

This page is general education about status spending, not financial advice. Only spend money you can genuinely spare on anything with no practical use.

Key takeaways

  • Veblen separated a good's usefulness ("serviceability") from its status value ("honorific" aspect).
  • Most luxury purchases carry both: a luxury car still drives; a fine watch still tells the time.
  • "Pure" conspicuous consumption is rare because almost everything does something.
  • The clearest pure example was the "I Am Rich" iPhone app in 2008, which cost $999.99, did nothing but show a red gem, and sold eight copies before Apple removed it.
  • Status purchases prove a payment was made, not that someone is wealthy.

What is conspicuous consumption?

Britannica's entry on conspicuous consumption defines it as "using goods of a higher quality or in greater quantity than might be considered necessary in practical terms". It credits Veblen with the term and gives his key distinction: every good has a "serviceability" side (it does a job) and an "honorific" side (it displays status). A luxury car and an economy car both get you to work; the luxury car also signals that you didn't need the cheaper one.

Veblen's own words, in chapter 4 of The Theory of the Leisure Class, are blunt: "Conspicuous consumption of valuable goods is a means of reputability to the gentleman of leisure." He argued that wealth has to be visible to strangers to earn esteem, that "the signature of one's pecuniary strength should be written in characters which he who runs may read."

Is there a product that is nothing but conspicuous consumption?

Yes, a few. The best-documented case is "I Am Rich". According to Wikipedia's account, German developer Armin Heinrich released it on 5 August 2008 at $999.99, the highest price Apple allowed. It showed a glowing red gem and a short mantra. Apple pulled it within a day without giving a reason. MacRumors reported at the time that eight people bought it and the developer earned $5,600 net. At least one buyer later said the purchase was accidental, and Heinrich said Apple refunded two buyers.

A modern, deliberate version is The £6,000 Club from Asendify. It is a numbered certificate that states one thing: the holder paid £6,000 to be a member. It comes with a private verify link, no public members list, and no course, coaching, perks or access to anything. Its own page says it is not proof of income, wealth or creditworthiness. It suits someone who wants an honest, openly pointless status object. Cheaper alternatives with similar symbolic value include novelty certificates and limited-edition collectibles, though none of them prove wealth either.

Why people buy status

Researchers and writers offer several reasons, and they often overlap:

  • Signalling. Showing others you can afford something costly.
  • Belonging. Joining a group defined by a shared purchase.
  • Self-image. Marking a milestone for yourself, whether or not anyone sees it.
  • Humour or art. Some buyers enjoy the joke; Heinrich himself called "I Am Rich" art.
  • Collecting. Low numbers in a numbered series can feel special.

None of these is wrong in itself. The risk is spending money you need, or believing a purchase changes how wealthy you are.

If any of them would have to cut essentials or dip into savings to afford it, it is the wrong purchase.

FAQ

What is conspicuous consumption in simple terms?

Buying more or better than you need so that others notice your wealth or status. Thorstein Veblen coined the term in 1899.

Is there a product that is nothing but conspicuous consumption?

Yes, a few. The 2008 "I Am Rich" app did nothing but show a red gem, and Asendify's The £6,000 Club is a numbered certificate that states only that you paid £6,000.

Is conspicuous consumption bad?

Not in itself. It becomes a problem when it uses money needed for essentials or savings, or when people mistake it for real wealth.

What is the difference between conspicuous consumption and a Veblen good?

Conspicuous consumption is the behaviour. A Veblen good is an item whose demand can rise as its price rises, because a high price is part of its appeal.

Does buying status items make you look rich?

It shows you made a purchase. It doesn't prove income, savings or wealth.
