Key takeaways

  • Average view duration (AVD) rises when fewer people leave early and fewer leave in the middle. Editors influence both.
  • YouTube's retention report shows where: "Dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video".
  • Not every spike is good. YouTube notes spikes can mean viewers had to rewatch because a section wasn't clear.
  • Wistia's data shows most viewers drop off around the halfway point of 1-5 minute videos, and it advises putting key information in the first half.
  • A good book gives you rules; your channel's data tells you which rules matter.

How editing changes average view duration

AVD is total watch time divided by views. A higher number means people watch more of each video on average. An editor moves it in three places:

Where viewers leaveEditing causeEditing fix
First 30 secondsSlow intro, unclear promise, logo stingHook first, context later
MiddleRepetition, long static shots, tangentsTighten, re-hook before sections, add B-roll
EndLong outro, payoff too earlyPay off late but not last; short outro

YouTube gives you an intro figure, "what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds", and lets you compare against your recent videos with typical retention.

What a book for editors should cover

  1. Opening edits. How to find and move the strongest moment to the start.
  2. Cutting the middle. How to spot repetition and tangents, and when to keep a pause.
  3. Re-hooks and open loops. How to give viewers a reason to stay before known dips.
  4. Pattern interrupts. Shot changes, graphics and sound that reset attention without feeling random.
  5. Reading retention graphs. Linking dips to timeline moments.
  6. Working with creators. How to suggest bigger structural changes.

Worked example

Example: an editor cuts weekly 18-minute gaming videos. The creator's AVD sits around 5 minutes. The retention graphs of the last five videos show a sharp drop in the first minute and a dip at each "settings" section.

Mistakes editors make chasing AVD

  • Padding videos to raise minutes. Viewers notice; retention drops.
  • Cutting so hard the video feels frantic. Some pauses help understanding.
  • Ignoring packaging. If the title over-promises, viewers leave no matter how good the edit.
  • Judging from one video. Compare against several similar videos.

Tracking average view duration fairly

AVD comparisons can mislead if videos differ in length or type. Track it alongside a few other numbers in a simple sheet.

Compare each video with others of similar length and type. Over ten or so videos, you'll see which editing changes move the numbers on your channel and which don't.

FAQ

Is there a book for editors on raising average view duration?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers the editing methods that affect AVD, with templates and a tracker, for £1,720 one-time.

Does a shorter video always get a better AVD?

No. A shorter video can have lower AVD in minutes but a higher share watched. Look at both numbers.

Can an editor fix a weak topic?

Not fully. Editing helps viewers stay, but a weak topic or misleading title limits what any edit can do.

What's a good average view duration?

It depends on length and niche. Compare with your own typical retention rather than other channels.

How many videos before I judge a change?

Several. One video can be an outlier for reasons that have nothing to do with the edit.

Does average view duration affect recommendations?

YouTube doesn't publish a formula, but its analytics treat watch behaviour as a key signal of how well a video serves viewers. Focus on serving the viewer, and the metrics follow.
