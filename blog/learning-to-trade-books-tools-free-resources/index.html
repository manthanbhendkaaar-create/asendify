Learning to trade: books, tools and free resources

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 October 2026 · Last updated 4 November 2026

Learning to trade comes down to three skills: reading what a chart shows, sizing every trade so one loss cannot hurt you badly, and keeping an honest record of what works. Start with free official education, a free charting plan and paper trades. Add paid books or tools only once you know which skill you need help with.

Key takeaways

  • Start with free, official education before risking any money.
  • Practise on paper or in a demo account first; most retail traders of leveraged products lose money.
  • Size every trade from a fixed risk per trade, not from how confident you feel.
  • Keep a journal and review it weekly; your own records teach you the most.
  • No book, tool or course can promise profits.

A sober starting point

Before choosing any resource, it helps to know the odds. When the European Securities and Markets Authority restricted contracts for difference (CFDs) for retail investors, it reported that 74-89% of retail accounts typically lose money on these products. That is not a reason to never learn. It is a reason to learn slowly, start on paper, and treat risk control as the first skill, not the last.

Start here: the best free resources (not ours)

  • Investor.gov from the US Securities and Exchange Commission: free, official education on investing basics, calculators and how to protect yourself from fraud. A sensible first stop.
  • TradingView Basic: a free charting plan with no card needed. It allows one chart per tab and two indicators per chart, which is plenty for learning.
  • Babypips School of Pipsology: a free online course on forex basics in 11 parts.
  • Tradervue Basic: a free online trading journal for up to 30 trades a month, covering stocks, options, futures and forex.
  • A spreadsheet. A simple sheet with date, market, setup, entry, stop, exit, size and one line on why you took the trade is free and enough to start reviewing.

Worked example: sizing one trade

Example: Sam has a practice account of £5,000 and a rule to risk no more than 1% on any trade. These numbers are for illustration only.

  • Money at risk: 1% of £5,000 = £50.
  • Planned entry: £100 a share.
  • Stop-loss: £95 a share, below a level he has marked on the chart.
  • Risk per share: £100 − £95 = £5.
  • Position size: £50 ÷ £5 = 10 shares.
  • Target: £110, so the possible reward is £10 a share, or £100 in total.
  • Reward-to-risk: £100 ÷ £50 = 2 to 1.

At 2 to 1, the breakeven win rate before costs is about 33%: he needs to win roughly one trade in three just to stand still. Fees, spreads and slippage push that higher. Sam records the trade in his journal with one line on why he took it.

After 30 paper trades, his journal shows he wins about 40% of trades with this setup, but often moves his stop when a trade goes against him. The lesson is not about the chart. It is about following his own rules. That is what a journal is for.

How to judge any trading resource

  • Does it put risk first? Good resources teach position sizing before entries.
  • Does it avoid promises? Anyone promising returns or a win rate is a warning sign.
  • Does it show losing examples? Honest material does.
  • Can you check it before paying? Look for samples, outlines or free tiers.

FAQ

What is the single best free resource for a new trader?

Official education such as Investor.gov for the basics, plus a free charting plan to practise reading charts without risking money.

Do I need a paid course to learn trading?

No. Many people learn the basics from free resources. A paid book or tool mainly buys structure and saves time; none can guarantee profits.

Does Chart Reader tell me what to buy or sell?

No. It gives an educational read of a chart screenshot and makes no buy or sell calls.

Is a free trading journal good enough?

Often, yes. A spreadsheet or Tradervue's free plan is enough for many traders. What matters is recording every trade and reviewing it weekly.

How much should I risk per trade while learning?

Many educators suggest a small fixed percentage, such as 1% of the account, but start with paper trades. This is education, not advice; decide your own limits.
