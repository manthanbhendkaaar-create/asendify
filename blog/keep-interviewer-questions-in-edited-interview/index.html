Should I keep the interviewer's questions in an edited interview video?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Keep the question only when the viewer would be lost without it. If the answer can stand alone, cut the question and let the guest's first sentence carry the context. For everything in between, shorten the question, show it as a text card, or ask the guest to restate it in the answer.

Key takeaways

  • Descript advises "finding the spine first, then cutting everything that does not serve it" and "be ruthless: cut anything that won't keep a viewer captivated" (Descript). A long question rarely serves the spine.
  • Descript notes most raw interviews hold "a fifteen minute story inside an hour of tape" (Descript), so something has to go, and questions are the first candidate.
  • NYFA's editor asks "how many sentences or words can I take out, and still tell the same story?" (NYFA).
  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded, rather than included" (Dartmouth). It is about learning, but it is a fair test for any question you are unsure about.

What the sources say, and what they do not

None of the guides we checked gives a rule for interviewer questions. Descript's and NYFA's editing guides both focus on finding the story and cutting what does not serve it, and neither says "always keep" or "always remove" the question. So the advice below is a method built on that general principle, not a quote from a rule.

The principle is simple: every second in the edit should earn its place. A question earns its place when the answer would be unclear or less interesting without it.

Step-by-step: deciding on each question

  1. Start with the story, not the transcript. Write the point of the video in one sentence. Descript's phrase is the "spine".
  2. Mark every question and answer pair. Highlight the unneeded parts first, as Descript advises, so they are easy to remove.
  3. Play each answer without its question. If the first sentence makes sense, cut the question.
  4. If it does not make sense, try the cheapest fix first. Often a text card with six to eight words ("Why did the first launch fail?") gives the context with no audio.
  5. Next, shorten the question. Keep the key clause and cut the lead-in. Make sure the shortened version is still what was asked; do not change its meaning.
  6. If you cannot cut it cleanly, consider a restated answer. For future interviews, ask guests to answer in full sentences that include the question. For this one, you may be able to cut between sentences.
  7. Cover the cut. Descript suggests close-ups and multiple angles to hide audio edits. NYFA's editor uses cutaways "to cover the jump cuts" but would rather keep a jump cut than use a shot that does not fit.
  8. Keep the guest's voice continuous. Where you remove a question between two answers, check that the tone and background sound match.
  9. Check meaning. Re-read the answer after the question is gone. If the answer could now be read as saying something the guest did not mean, put the context back.
  10. Watch it once without looking at the timeline, as a viewer would, and mark where you felt lost.
  11. Review the retention graph. YouTube describes a dip as viewers "abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). If a dip lines up with a removed or kept question, that tells you what to do next time.

Text cards, captions and voiceover

Text cards are the simplest replacement for a question. They keep the video moving, work with sound off and can double as chapter markers. Keep them short and in the same style as your other graphics. Mayer's redundancy principle warns that "graphics, voiceover, and on-screen text can be overwhelming for learners" (Dartmouth), so if the card repeats what the guest says in the next sentence, shorten it or drop it.

If you caption the video, remember captions should "identify who is speaking" (W3C). If the interviewer's question stays in, label the speaker. If it is replaced by a card, the card is not a substitute for captions on the guest's words.

A short voiceover from the interviewer can also replace a messy question, but it changes the feel of the piece from conversation to narration. Use it only if that suits the video.

Example: a 12-minute founder interview

Example: a founder interview is recorded in one hour. The editor starts with a three-line summary of the story: why the first product failed and what changed. In the first cut, every question is kept, which adds about 40 seconds of interviewer speech. In the second cut, six answers that restate the question lose their questions. Two answers that start with "because" get an eight-word text card. One surprising question ("Did you ever think of giving up?") is kept, because it is the hook for the next section. When to keep more of the interviewer

Keep more of the interviewer when the relationship is the point: a debate, a tough interview, an investigative piece where the questions show what was asked and what was avoided. Cutting questions there can look like hiding something. Keep the question as asked and do not edit it so it changes the meaning.

Also keep questions when the audience is learning from them. In a how-to interview, a good question shows viewers what they might ask themselves.

Ethics and consent

Cutting a question is normal editing, but do not let the removal change what the guest appears to say. If a guest might be surprised by how their answer is framed, share the cut with them before publishing, where that is possible. This is editing guidance, not legal advice.

Is it dishonest to cut the interviewer's question?

No, if the answer's meaning is unchanged. Re-read the answer without the question and put context back if it could be misread.

What is the quickest replacement for a question?

A short text card of six to eight words. It adds context without a second voice.

Should I keep the question in a debate or tough interview?

Usually yes, as asked, because the question shows what was and was not answered.

Do I need captions if I use text cards?

Yes. Cards give context; captions give access to the guest's spoken words.
