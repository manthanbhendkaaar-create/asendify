What is the difference between colour correction and colour grading?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Colour correction fixes footage so it looks natural and consistent: correct exposure, white balance and matching shots. Colour grading comes after, and adds a deliberate look or mood, such as warm and soft, cold and moody, or a film-style finish. Correct first, then grade, because a creative look applied to unbalanced footage just exaggerates the problems.

Key takeaways

  • Adobe: "Color correction consists of correcting issues and balancing shots, so they look natural and unified."
  • Adobe: "Color grading consists of adjusting color and contrast to serve a creative or stylistic choice."
  • Vimeo calls correction "a technical clean-up pass" and says grading "builds on the corrected image to create a deliberate style."
  • Use scopes (waveform, vectorscope), not just your eyes, especially for correction.
  • For most YouTube videos, good correction matters more than a dramatic grade.

Adobe's guide gives the definitions above and examples for each: correction tools such as Auto Color, Color Match and colour wheels for shadows, midtones and highlights; grading choices such as vibrance and tint for mood, isolating a single colour, adding a vignette, or applying Lumetri looks and custom LUTs. It says you can then "use color grading tools to add drama, visual interest, and emotion to your story."

Why order matters

Vimeo's explainer describes correction as adjusting "exposure, white balance, and saturation so the video images look natural and consistent from shot to shot", and says "color grading polishes your color correction". If you put a teal-and-orange look over a shot that is already too blue, the blue gets worse. If camera A is warm and camera B is cool, a grade won't make them match; correction will.

The Evergreen State College's editing wiki puts the practical rule simply: "Always use the scopes" (Evergreen Help Wiki). Monitors and eyes adapt; a waveform and vectorscope don't.

A simple YouTube colour workflow

  1. Finish the edit first. Vimeo notes correction usually happens "after you're happy with the edit", so you don't colour shots you later cut.
  2. Set white balance. Use the white balance picker on something neutral (a white wall, a grey card) or adjust temperature and tint until whites look white.
  3. Set exposure and contrast. On the waveform, keep highlights from clipping and shadows from crushing unless that's intentional.
  4. Check skin tones. On the vectorscope, skin usually sits near the skin-tone line; adjust until faces look healthy, not orange or grey.
  5. Match shots. Use colour match or manual adjustments so every angle in a scene looks like the same room.
  6. Then grade. Apply a subtle look across the video with an adjustment layer: a gentle warmth, a slight saturation change or a light LUT.
  7. Check on a phone. Most YouTube viewers watch on phones; make sure faces and text still read well.

Example: a two-camera talking-head video

Example (a typical setup, to show the steps): a creator films with a main camera and a second angle, near a window, in the late afternoon.

  • Problem: the main camera looks warm and slightly dark; the second angle looks cool and bright, and the light changes over a 20-minute recording.
  • Correction: balance whites on the wall behind, lift exposure on the main camera, cool it slightly, then colour-match the second angle to it. Add a small exposure keyframe as the window light drops.
  • Grade: an adjustment layer with a slight warm tone and a touch of contrast, kept subtle so skin stays natural.
  • Result: cutting between angles no longer looks like two different rooms, which matters for retention because jarring changes pull attention away from what's being said.

How much time should colour take?

For a weekly YouTube video, colour should be a small part of the edit, not a project of its own. Build a routine:

  • Save a correction preset for each camera and lighting setup you use regularly.
  • Keep one house look as an adjustment-layer preset, so every video looks like your channel.
  • Fix it at the shoot. Locking white balance and exposure on the camera, and using steady light, saves more time than any grade.
  • Spend the saved time on the story. Hooks, pacing and structure decide whether people keep watching far more than colour does.

Does colour affect retention?

Colour alone rarely decides whether people keep watching. But mismatched shots, unflattering skin and murky exposure make a video look careless and can distract from the story. Clean correction removes that friction. Grading is optional for most talking-head and tutorial channels; for story-driven or cinematic channels it can be part of the identity.

Common mistakes

  • Grading before correcting. The look exaggerates every problem underneath.
  • Over-saturating. Faces turn orange and skies look fake on phone screens.
  • Relying on a LUT alone. A LUT is a starting point for a known camera profile or look, not a fix for bad white balance.
  • Ignoring consistency. One great-looking shot next to an uncorrected one looks worse than two average, matched shots.
  • No scopes. Your eyes adapt; the scopes don't.

FAQ

Is there a self-paced course on retention editing for YouTube creators?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Accelerator has 6 modules and 24 lessons on hooks, pacing and structure, with an assignment in every lesson.

Do I need to colour grade YouTube videos?

Not always. Good colour correction is usually enough for talking-head and tutorial videos.

Is a LUT colour correction or colour grading?

It can be either: a technical LUT converts a camera's log profile, while a creative LUT adds a look. Correct the footage first either way.

Can I colour correct in free software?

Yes. DaVinci Resolve's free version and most editors include scopes and colour tools.

What are scopes in video editing?

Graphs such as the waveform and vectorscope that measure brightness and colour, so you don't rely on your eyes alone.

Should I correct before or after editing?

Usually after you lock the edit, so you only correct shots that stay in.
