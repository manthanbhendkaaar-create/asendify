Do learners who watch course videos at 1.5x or 2x speed still learn?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Mostly yes, up to about double speed. A UCLA study of 231 students found that watching lecture videos at 1.5x or 2x gave test scores close to normal speed, while 2.5x clearly hurt comprehension. The effect may be bigger for complex material. For course creators, the lesson is that many learners want a faster pace, so tight editing matters more than slow delivery.

Key takeaways

  • UCLA researchers (Murphy, Castel and colleagues, Applied Cognitive Psychology) had 231 undergraduates watch 13-15 minute lectures at 1x, 1.5x, 2x or 2.5x. On an immediate 40-point test, normal speed averaged 26 and 2x averaged 25; 2.5x fell to about 22.
  • A week later, normal speed averaged 24, 1.5x and 2x averaged 21, and 2.5x averaged 20, so a small gap appeared over time.
  • The British Psychological Society's Research Digest notes the authors' caution that "perhaps it might not work for more complex subject matter".
  • 85% of surveyed UCLA students said they speed-watch lectures, according to the UCLA release.
  • In the edX study by Guo, Kim and Rubin, "Students generally engaged more with videos where instructors spoke faster", with speaking rates ranging from 48 to 254 words per minute.

The study used two fairly simple, single-topic lectures (the Roman Empire and real estate appraisal) with no pausing or note-taking. Your learners may be studying something harder, may pause, and may be watching on a phone in a noisy place. Treat the numbers as a direction, not a promise.

Why learners speed up

Learners speed up when the video feels slower than their thinking. Common causes are long introductions, repeated points, slow speech, pauses while the instructor clicks around, and filler between sections. The edX finding that faster speakers were more engaging points the same way: people like a brisk pace.

That gives course creators two options. You can accept that learners will speed up and make sure the video still works at 1.5x or 2x. Or you can remove the reasons they speed up in the first place. The best courses do both.

How to make course videos work for speed-watchers

  1. Cut the slack before you think about slowing down. Remove pauses, false starts and repeated sentences in the edit.
  2. Keep on-screen text up long enough. At 2x, a caption shown for three seconds is on screen for 1.5. Hold key text or diagrams a little longer.
  3. Make audio clean. Fast playback amplifies mumbling and background noise. Clear audio stays understandable at speed.
  4. Slow down only where it is hard. For a complex derivation or a tricky step, slow your speech and add a pause; learners can always skip forward.
  5. Add chapters. Speed-watchers often jump around. Chapters let them find the part they need.
  6. Offer a summary or worksheet. The UCLA team suggests using saved time for practice such as "reviewing flashcards or taking practice tests".

A note on revision

The study also found that watching at 2x twice, with the second viewing just before the test, could match or beat a single normal-speed viewing. For a course, this suggests making lessons easy to revisit: chapters, summaries and short recap videos help learners who come back before an exam or a project.

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers pacing and structure with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. The UCLA research is a free companion.

Is 2x speed bad for learning?

In the UCLA study, 2x was close to normal speed on an immediate test, with a small gap a week later. 2.5x was clearly worse.

Should I disable speed controls on my course?

No. Many learners use them, and taking them away is frustrating. Make the video work well at higher speeds instead.

Does fast talking help or hurt?

The edX data found more engagement with faster speakers, but clarity matters. Speak briskly, and slow down for hard parts.

Does speed matter more for complex topics?

Probably. The researchers warned the result may not hold for especially complex or difficult material.
