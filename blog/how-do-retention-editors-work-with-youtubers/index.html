How do retention editors work with YouTubers?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Most retention editors work in a weekly loop: the YouTuber sends raw footage and a short brief (title, thumbnail idea, key payoff), the editor returns a rough cut and then a fine cut with timestamped review notes, and after publishing both look at the retention graph to decide what to change next time. The best partnerships agree up front on access to analytics, turnaround, revision rounds, file sharing and who owns the work.

Key takeaways

  • The editor should know the title and thumbnail before cutting, because the opening has to deliver what they promise.
  • Retention work needs feedback. YouTube's audience retention report shows the 30-second "Intro" figure, "dips" and "spikes", and compares each video with the channel's recent videos of similar length.
  • Give editors analytics access without passwords. YouTube's channel permissions include a "Viewer (limited)" role that "can't access revenue data".
  • Agree ownership in writing. Under UK law, a freelancer working under a contract for services "will usually retain copyright" in what they produce unless the contract says otherwise, according to GOV.UK.
  • A short weekly review of the graph is where most of the improvement comes from.

What a good brief includes

A retention editor can't fix an opening if they don't know what it's opening into. A useful brief is short:

  • Title and thumbnail concept, so the first seconds can confirm them.
  • The payoff: the moment or answer the video is built around.
  • Must-keep and must-cut moments, such as sponsor reads or sensitive comments.
  • Style references: one or two of the channel's own videos that worked well.
  • Deadline and publish date.

Access and tools

Editors need three kinds of access:

  1. Files. A shared drive or a review tool where the YouTuber can leave timestamped comments.
  2. Analytics. The Viewer (limited) role lets editors see channel details and analytics without revenue data. If the creator prefers not to give access, weekly screenshots of the retention graph work too.
  3. Upload (optional). Some editors upload drafts as private or unlisted videos. YouTube offers Editor roles for this; only grant what's needed.

Contracts and ownership

Put the basics in writing: scope, number of revision rounds, turnaround, payment terms, who owns the project files and finished video, whether the editor can show the work in a portfolio, and how confidential information is handled. Because UK freelancers usually keep copyright unless agreed otherwise, most creators include an assignment or licence clause. Take advice for anything complex. Worked example

Example: a tech reviewer posting one 12-minute video a week hires a retention editor.

WeekWhat happenedChange for next week
1Intro figure 58%; graph shows a steep drop in the first 20 seconds of channel newsMove news to the end of the video
2Intro 63%; dip at 6:30 during a spec listShow specs as a single on-screen table
3Intro 64%; smaller dip at 6:30; new dip at the sponsor readShorten read and place after the first test result
4Intro 65%; flatter middle sectionKeep the format, test a new opening style

The figures are illustrative. The pattern matters: one or two specific changes each week, checked against the graph, not a full rewrite.

Common friction points, and fixes

  • Too many revision rounds. Agree a number up front; most notes should come in one pass.
  • Brief arrives after the edit. Make the title and thumbnail part of the handover.
  • Editor never sees results. Share the graph weekly; it's the editor's main training data.
  • Unclear ownership. Put it in the contract before the first invoice.

FAQ

Is there a playbook a YouTuber and their editor can share?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook from Asendify includes templates and a tracker for £1,720 one-time. YouTube's help pages are a free alternative.

Should I give my editor access to YouTube Studio?

The Viewer (limited) role shows analytics without revenue data, so you don't need to share your password.

How many revisions are normal?

It varies; many editors include one or two rounds and charge for more. Agree it in writing.

Who owns the edited video?

In the UK a freelancer usually keeps copyright unless the contract assigns it, so put ownership in the agreement.

How soon can we judge whether the editing works?

Look at trends over several videos, not one upload. A month of weekly reviews gives a clearer picture.
