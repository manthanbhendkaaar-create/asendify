How do I script a LinkedIn video so it is not a sales pitch?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Lead with one useful idea for a named reader, show the reasoning or a concrete example, and keep the ask small and honest at the end. A LinkedIn video feels like a pitch when it opens with your company, lists features and closes with "book a demo". It feels useful when the first line is the reader's problem, the middle teaches something they can use without buying, and the end offers a next step that fits (a checklist, a comment prompt, a conversation). Write it for sound-off viewing: captions plus short on-screen text.

Key takeaways

  • A LinkedIn marketer writes in an IAB UK article that "79% of videos are watched on mute" on LinkedIn, based on LinkedIn's own analysis, and calls subtitles "table stakes" for videos with a speaker (IAB UK). The figure is self-reported, but it is a reason to script for silent viewing.
  • The same article advises that overlays "stick to a maximum of 14 words per scene" and that "close-ups elicit higher engagement". Treat these as one practitioner's guidance, not a universal law.
  • Google's video guidance says to "humanize the story", "feature people", keep messaging simple and "avoid overloading the ad" (Google Ads Help). The same logic helps an organic LinkedIn video feel less like an ad.
  • Wistia's data says "engagement naturally declines after the five-minute mark" and suggests under a minute for social (Wistia). Keep it short.

What makes a video feel like a pitch

Viewers recognise a pitch in the first few seconds by structure, not by words. The signs are: the company or product is named before the problem, the speaker describes features rather than outcomes, there is no concrete example, and the ending asks for a commitment out of proportion to what the viewer has received. Any one of these can be fine on its own; together they read as an ad.

The test is simple: if you remove your company name, is the video still worth watching? If yes, it is useful content. If no, it is a pitch, and it will be treated as one.

A script structure that teaches first

BeatLengthPurposeExample line
1. The reader's problem3 to 6 secName who it is for"If you manage a team that never reads your updates..."
2. The idea5 to 10 secOne useful point"...try ending every update with one decision you need."
3. Why it works10 to 20 secReasoning or example"Here is the same update before and after."
4. How to do it15 to 25 secSteps the viewer can copyThree short steps, on screen
5. The small ask3 to 5 secFit-for-purpose next step"If you want the template, comment 'update' and I will send it."

This runs roughly 40 to 70 seconds. Your company appears only where it is relevant: a sentence of credibility in beat 1 or 3, and your name or logo in a lower-third caption.

Step-by-step: from pitch to post

  1. Write your pitch version first. Capture what you want to say about the product.
  2. Find the lesson inside it. What does your product teach? A way of thinking, a checklist, a mistake to avoid. That is your video.
  3. Name the reader. "Heads of operations at logistics firms" is better than "business leaders". A specific reader makes the video feel written for them.
  4. Write the first line as their problem. Not "At [Company] we...", but a sentence they would say themselves.
  5. Include one concrete example. A before and after, a mini case with names removed, or a screen recording of the technique. Do not invent client results or cite numbers you cannot support.
  6. Cap on-screen text. Keep each text overlay short; the IAB piece suggests no more than 14 words per scene, spread over at most three lines.
  7. Add captions. Burned-in or uploaded, but check them for accuracy, particularly names and numbers.
  8. End with a proportionate ask. Comment, save, follow, or a short conversation. A "book a demo" CTA after a 45-second tip asks for a lot.
  9. Read it aloud and cut. Anything that does not serve the reader's problem goes.

Tone: how to sound like a person

  • Use first person and plain verbs. "I tried this with my team" reads better than "Our solution enables".
  • Say what did not work. One honest limitation makes the rest more credible.
  • Avoid buzzwords. "Leverage" and "synergy" mark a pitch faster than anything.
  • Let a real person present. Google's guidance suggests featuring people to relate to the viewer; if you do not want to appear, a voiceover with screen visuals can work.
  • Keep claims modest. Do not promise outcomes. "This worked for our team" is a statement about your team.

Example: a software founder's video

Example: the founder of a scheduling tool first scripts a video that opens with the company name, lists three features and ends with "book a demo". The re-scripted version opens with "If your team spends Monday morning rescheduling meetings, here is a three-step fix", shows a before and after calendar on screen, and ends with "comment 'template' and I will send the rule set". The product is mentioned once, in a lower-third. The founder compares the intro figure and total watch time on the two posts. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Check your draft

  • Remove the company name; is it still useful?
  • Can the viewer follow with sound off?
  • Is the first line about them, not you?
  • Is there one concrete example?
  • Is the ask proportionate?
