Should every online course lesson video start with an intro?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Yes, but only a short one that does a teaching job. Spend the first 10 to 20 seconds telling the student what they will be able to do by the end of the lesson, then start teaching. Drop the repeated branded title sequence, the long greeting and the full recap of the previous lesson. Research on educational video favours removing extraneous material and keeping videos short.

Key takeaways

  • A lesson intro should state the outcome, not the course brand. One sentence is often enough.
  • Brame's review in CBE Life Sciences Education recommends "weeding": eliminating interesting but extraneous information that does not contribute to the learning goal. A repeated 15-second branded sting is exactly that.
  • The same review cites Guo et al.'s study of 6.9 million edX sessions: median engagement was close to 100% for videos under 6 minutes, falling to about 50% at 9 to 12 minutes.
  • Wistia says viewers typically watch over 50% of educational and tutorial videos of 1 to 5 minutes, so the opening should carry the most useful promise.
  • If your lessons are on YouTube, chapters help students skip what they already know; the first timestamp must start at 00:00 and chapters must be at least 10 seconds.

Why lesson intros get bloated

Course creators often build a "show open" for every lesson because it looks professional: logo animation, music, "Welcome back to Module 3, Lesson 4 of the Complete Bookkeeping Masterclass", then a recap. Across a 40-lesson course, a 30-second intro adds 20 minutes of material nobody learns from. Students notice by lesson three and start dragging the playhead.

Signalling: tell them where the lesson is going

Brame's review describes signalling as using on-screen text or symbols to direct attention. In a lesson intro that can be a simple three-step outline on screen ("1. Export, 2. Match, 3. Check"), which you then repeat as section titles inside the video. Students know what's coming and where they are.

If a lesson runs much longer, split it. The research above suggests engagement falls steeply as videos lengthen.

Example: re-cutting a coding lesson intro

Example: an online JavaScript course opened every lesson with a 12-second animated logo, then 40 seconds of the tutor saying hello, recapping the previous lesson and reminding students to join the community group. The first line of code appeared at 1 minute.

The re-cut:

  • 0:00 to 0:08: "In this lesson you'll write a function that filters a list of products by price." Finished result shown running on screen.
  • 0:08 to 0:15: Three-step outline on screen.
  • 0:15: Editor open, first line typed.
  • Logo moved to a small corner bug; community reminder moved to the lesson notes; recap reduced to one sentence linked to the previous lesson.

Across 30 lessons, that removes roughly 22 minutes of non-teaching material. Details are illustrative; check your platform's completion data before and after.

Should the first lesson of a course be different?

Yes. The welcome lesson is where you introduce yourself, how the course works and what students will achieve overall. It can be longer and warmer. Every lesson after that should get to the point. FAQ

Is there a book that teaches pacing for course videos?

The Retention Editing Playbook teaches pacing and hooks for £1,720 one-time; it is YouTube-first. Brame's free CBE Life Sciences Education review covers learning-focused video design.

How long should a course lesson intro be?

About 10 to 20 seconds: the outcome, why it matters and a quick outline.

Should I recap the previous lesson?

In one sentence at most. Link to the earlier lesson for anyone who needs more.

Should every lesson have a branded intro?

No. Keep the full branded open for the welcome lesson or module openers, and use a corner logo elsewhere.

What if students skip my intros?

That's a sign the intro isn't useful. Cut it to the learning outcome and start teaching sooner.
