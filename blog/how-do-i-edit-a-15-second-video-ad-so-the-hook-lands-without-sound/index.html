How do I edit a 15 second video ad so the hook lands without sound?

Key takeaways

  • A HubSpot guide to silent Facebook ads quotes a Facebook ads director estimating that "up to 40% of Facebook video ads fail to communicate when the sound isn't on" (HubSpot). The figure is old and second-hand, but the habit it points to still holds: do not rely on audio.
  • The same article cites Facebook's claim that 65% of people who watch the first three seconds of a video ad go on to watch at least ten seconds, so the opening carries most of the weight.
  • Meta's Reels and Stories placements cover the top and bottom of the frame with interface elements, so a safe-zone guide suggests keeping about 14% free at the top and 20% to 35% at the bottom (Billo).
  • Google's study of thousands of YouTube ads found that faces and smiling people in the first five seconds were linked to more viewing, while any music in that window was linked to lower brand awareness (Think with Google). Sound is not the only thing doing the work.

Why a 15 second ad has to work silently

Many people watch social video with the sound off, in a queue, on a train or in bed. If your hook is a spoken line or a music sting, those viewers get nothing for the first few seconds, and a short ad cannot afford that. HubSpot's guide says the same thing in practical terms: build the ad so the message survives without audio, and treat sound as a bonus.

The numbers in that guide come from older third-party sources, some from 2016 and 2017, so treat them as a direction and not as a current benchmark. The method does not depend on them. If you can understand the ad with the sound off, you lose nothing when the sound is on.

What the first three seconds must carry

A silent hook has three jobs. It must show something worth looking at, say what the ad is about in a few words, and promise what comes next. The table sets out the choices.

Hook elementSilent versionCommon mistake
ImageThe product in use, a face reacting, or a clear before stateA logo card or a slow fade-in
Words4 to 7 words on screen that state the pointA full sentence in small type
MovementA cut or a camera move by second 2 or 3One static shot for the whole opening
ProofA number, a result or a visible outcomeA vague claim with no evidence
SoundOptional; the ad works without itA spoken hook that carries all the meaning

Step-by-step: edit the hook for silent viewing

  1. Write the hook as one line a stranger could read in two seconds. For example, "Fix a leaking tap in 5 minutes". Keep it to a handful of words.
  2. Pick the frame that shows it. Choose the shot where the result or the problem is plain without explanation.
  3. Put the words on the first frame. Do not wait until second 2. Silent viewers decide in the first moment.
  4. Add a change by second 3. A cut to a close-up, a zoom or a second shot tells the eye that something is happening.
  5. Keep text inside the safe area. Leave the top 14% and the bottom third clear on Reels and Stories placements, as the safe-zone guide suggests, so buttons and captions do not cover your words.
  6. Caption any speech. If someone speaks, show the words. Facebook can generate captions, but HubSpot warns that "captioning technology isn't at the point where it gets everything 100% right", so read every line before you publish.
  7. Cut the dead air. A 15 second ad has no room for a slow intro. Start on the action.
  8. Place the offer or the point early. Do not save the reason to care for the last three seconds.
  9. Mute and watch. Play the ad with the sound off on a phone, at arm's length. Can you say what it sells?
  10. Then play it with the sound on. Check that music or voice does not fight the captions.
  11. Test two hooks. Change only the first three seconds and compare the early drop-off in your ad reports.

Example: a 15 second ad for a kitchen scale

Example: a small brand has a 15 second ad that opens on a logo, then a presenter says "Tired of guessing your portions?" while the product slides in. With the sound off, the viewer sees a logo and a slow slide for three seconds and scrolls.

In the new cut, frame one shows a bowl on the scale with the number "180 g" and the words "Stop guessing portions" in large type, placed well above the bottom buttons. At second 2 the shot cuts to a hand pouring oats and the number climbing. At second 4 a short caption reads "Weighs to 1 g". The presenter's line stays as a captioned voiceover. The brand compares the two versions on the share of viewers who reach second 5. This scenario uses invented numbers and is not a measured result.

Captions and safe areas

Captions do two jobs in a silent ad: they carry the speech, and they repeat the hook. Place them where the platform will not cover them. A safe-zone guide for Reels and Stories ads suggests a top margin of about 14% and a bottom margin of 20% to 35%, because the profile label, the call-to-action button and the caption text from the platform sit there. Treat the larger figure as the danger zone, since caption length and phone size change how far the platform text reaches. Platform specs change, so check Meta's current creative specifications before you publish.

Should the sound be bad, then?

No. Sound still helps viewers who have it on. Google's analysis of YouTube ads found that humour and smiling faces in the opening were linked to better viewing, and that music in the first five seconds was linked to lower brand awareness, so the ad's audio choices matter too. Those results are averages for YouTube ads, not a rule for every ad. The sensible approach is to design for silence first, then add sound that supports the picture.

When the hook will not fit 15 seconds

If you cannot explain the point in three seconds without sound, the idea may be too complicated for this length. Cut the second benefit, show one result and save the rest for a longer ad. A clear single point in 15 seconds beats three points rushed.

Do I need captions on every ad?

If anyone speaks, yes. Many people watch with the sound off, so captions carry the message. Read auto-captions before publishing, as they are not always right.

How many words should be on screen in the first three seconds?

A handful, about four to seven. The viewer should be able to read them in a glance.

Where should I put text in a vertical ad?

Away from the top and bottom edges, where the app places its labels and buttons. A safe-zone guide suggests about 14% free at the top and 20% to 35% at the bottom.

Should the ad have music?

It can, but design the ad to work without it. Google's study linked music in the first five seconds to lower brand awareness on average, so test with and without.
