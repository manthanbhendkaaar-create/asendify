What is costly signalling?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 14 November 2026 · Last updated 14 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Costly signalling is when someone proves a hidden quality, such as ability, quality or wealth, by doing something that would be too expensive for people without that quality to copy. Economist Michael Spence showed in 1973 that a signal only works if it costs more for "low-quality" senders than for "high-quality" ones, and he shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for work on markets with asymmetric information.

Key takeaways

  • Signalling solves a trust problem: one side knows something the other cannot see.
  • A signal is only believable if faking it would cost too much.
  • Spence's classic example is education as a signal of productivity to employers.
  • Companies signal too, with dividends, strong guarantees and costly advertising.
  • Luxury goods and status purchases are a form of signalling, which Thorstein Veblen called conspicuous consumption in 1899.

Where the idea comes from

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the 2001 prize to George Akerlof, Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz "for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information". The Nobel Prize press release says Spence showed how better-informed people can take costly actions to convey information credibly to those who know less, and worked out when signalling actually works.

The Nobel Prize popular information page explains the key condition: signalling "requires economic agents to take observable and costly measures to convince other agents", and it "cannot succeed unless the signaling cost differs sufficiently among the 'senders'". If a signal costs everyone the same, everyone can send it and it tells you nothing.

How it works: the education example

Without signallingWith signalling
An employer cannot see who is more productiveApplicants can choose to get a qualification
So it offers everyone an average wageThe qualification is easier, so cheaper in effort, for more productive people
Productive people are underpaid; less productive people overpaidProductive people get it; others decide it is not worth it
Good candidates may go elsewhereThe employer can tell groups apart and pay accordingly

In Spence's model, the qualification works as a signal even if it teaches nothing useful, because it is harder to get for people who would be less productive. That is the controversial edge of the theory: some of what we pay for may be proof, not skill.

The Nobel page specifically mentions dividends, "far-reaching guarantees" and costly advertising as signals.

Status goods and conspicuous consumption

Long before Spence, Thorstein Veblen described the same pattern in spending. Britannica's entry on conspicuous consumption explains that Veblen, in The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899), described buying goods of higher quality or in greater quantity than needed, where admiration comes from the visible evidence of wealth rather than practical use. A luxury car and an economy car both get you there; the luxury car also signals affluence.

The signalling lens explains why some luxury goods become more desirable as they get more expensive: the price is part of what makes the signal believable. Our page on what a Veblen good is covers that effect.

When signals stop working

ProblemWhat happens
Signal becomes cheapEveryone can send it, so it means less (fake designer goods, inflated grades)
Receiver cannot verify itFake reviews, invented credentials and counterfeit certificates break the signal
Signal becomes the goalPeople chase the badge instead of the quality it was meant to prove
Wasteful arms raceEveryone spends more to stand out, and nobody ends up better off

Worked example: two freelance designers

Example: Sam and Alex both want a £5,000 branding job. The client cannot see who is better.

SignalSamAlex
Says "I'm the best designer in town"YesYes
Public portfolio of 30 real client projects with resultsNoYes
Offers to do a paid two-day test projectNoYes
Free redesign if the client is not happy with the first conceptNoYes

The claim "I'm the best" costs nothing, so it carries no information. Alex's portfolio, paid test and redesign offer are all costly to provide if the work is poor, so they are believable. Alex gets the job, not because of better words, but because of signals that would be too expensive for a weak designer to copy.

FAQ

What is the difference between signalling and screening?

In signalling, the informed side acts first, such as a job applicant getting a qualification. In screening, the uninformed side designs a test or choice to sort people, such as an employer's assessment day.

Who invented signalling theory?

In economics, Michael Spence developed it in his 1973 work on job market signalling, which was recognised in the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.

Is education just a signal?

Spence's model shows education can work as a signal even if it adds no skills. In reality, education usually both builds skills and signals ability; economists debate how much is which.

Why are some signals wasteful?

Because their value comes from the cost. When everyone competes with costly signals, much of the spending only keeps people level with each other.

Are luxury goods costly signals?

Often, yes. Their high, visible price makes them hard to fake as a display of wealth, which is part of what Veblen described as conspicuous consumption.
