Should the estate agent appear on camera in a listing tour?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Usually yes, but briefly. A short on-camera opening and close gives the buyer a person to trust, and the middle of the tour should belong to the property. Whatever the agent says or shows on video has to be accurate, and flaws must not be hidden, because video is treated as part of the sales material.

Key takeaways

  • Propertymark's guidance on video viewings says "the content of any video footage whether live or pre-recorded is likely to be considered a commercial practice", and that "agents should make reasonable checks to verify the truth and accuracy of any representations made" (Propertymark).
  • It adds that "a video viewing should include all the information that a physical one would" and that "no attempt should be made to hide any bad points, as this may constitute a 'misleading omission'" (same document).
  • Mayer's personalization principle says "learning is more effective when teachers use a friendly, conversational style, rather than a formal style" (Dartmouth). A relaxed human voice helps, though the principle comes from learning research, not property.
  • YouTube's retention report treats dips as moments where "viewers are abandoning or skipping" (YouTube Help), which is your test for whether the on-camera segments earn their time.

The real question: what is the viewer deciding?

A buyer watching a listing tour wants to know whether the home is worth a visit. Every second should help that decision. An agent on camera helps with three things: trust (a real person is accountable), context (what the area is like, why the layout works) and guidance (what to look at). An agent on camera hurts when they talk over rooms, read the particulars aloud or stay in frame so long that the home becomes a backdrop.

So the answer is a balance: be present at the start, at moments that need explaining, and at the end.

Step 2: Open with the home, then the person

A common mistake is a long on-camera introduction in front of the house, with a speech about the agency. Try an opening that gives the viewer the home immediately: a short shot of the front and a line such as "Three-bedroom house, a ten-minute walk to the station, and a kitchen that opens onto the garden. I will show you around." Then walk in.

Keep the first on-camera moment under 15 seconds if you can, and give the key facts the viewer needs to decide whether to continue: type of property, number of bedrooms and the headline feature.

Step 3: Say only what you can verify

Propertymark's guidance puts the agent's video content in the same category as other sales material. Spoken and on-screen claims fall under the same rules as written ones. In practice:

  1. Check every claim before filming. Room sizes, tenure, lease length, council tax band and services should match the listing and the paperwork.
  2. Avoid vague superlatives you cannot support, such as "the best street in the area".
  3. Show the things a viewer would see in person, including less attractive parts. The guidance says that hiding bad points can constitute a misleading omission.
  4. Say what is not included. If furniture or a shed is not part of the sale, say so.
  5. Be careful with what a vendor says. If someone else speaks on the video, the guidance notes the agent may not be responsible for things said by people beyond their control, so make that clear to viewers. Check the document for the exact wording.

This page cannot give legal advice; take advice from your compliance lead or professional body about your own videos.

Step 4: Keep the camera work and edit simple

  • Shoot horizontally or vertically depending on where you will publish and stay consistent.
  • Use a steady camera or gimbal and walk slowly.
  • Light the rooms by opening curtains and turning on lights; do not alter the look in ways that misrepresent the space.
  • Cut dead time: doors opening, long pauses and repeated shots.
  • Add captions so people watching without sound get the facts. Check names, street names and measurements.
  • Keep a stable on-screen label for each room so viewers know where they are.

Step 5: Pace the tour

A tour with long unbroken takes can feel like waiting. A tour that cuts every second feels frantic. Work to the content: one clear shot per feature, a change of room as a natural break, and a short voiceover or on-camera line where it helps. Consider a short map or floor plan graphic at the start so the viewer understands the layout.

Step 6: Decide on-camera versus voiceover by the agent

Some agents are comfortable on camera; others are stiff. If an agent freezes in front of the lens, use a voiceover recorded separately and show the person only at the opening and close. A natural voice over good footage is better than a stiff face over the same footage. Practice a few takes and choose the most relaxed.

Step 7: Check the numbers

After publishing, check where viewers leave. If many leave when the agent talks at length, shorten or replace with footage. If they leave at the first room, the opening may be too slow. Compare tours of similar properties and change one thing at a time.

Example: a two-bedroom flat tour

Example: an agent films a two-minute tour of a flat. The opening shows the front door and then the agent, on camera, saying the type of flat, the floor, the nearest station and the headline feature. Common mistakes

  • A long on-camera intro before any property is shown.
  • Reading the particulars aloud. The listing already has them.
  • Hiding flaws. It risks a misleading omission.
  • Unverified claims. Check before you film.
  • Stiff delivery. Use voiceover.
  • No captions. Facts are lost when sound is off.

FAQ

Should the agent talk through the whole tour on camera?

Not usually. Use on-camera moments at the opening, where something needs explaining and at the close, and let footage carry the rest.

Do I have to show the flaws of a property?

Propertymark's guidance says a video viewing should include all the information a physical one would and that hiding bad points may be a misleading omission.

Is a voiceover better than appearing on camera?

If the agent is stiff on camera, a relaxed voiceover with a short on-camera opening and close often works better.

Should I add captions to a listing tour?

Yes. Captions help people watching without sound; check street names and measurements.
