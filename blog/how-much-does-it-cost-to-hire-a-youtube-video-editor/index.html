How much does it cost to hire a YouTube video editor?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Hiring a YouTube video editor can cost anything from a per-video freelance fee to a monthly subscription or a full salary, depending on volume, length and how much work each edit needs. On 13 November 2026, subscription services listed plans from $495 a month for four long videos, and the National Careers Service put a UK in-house editor's salary at £24,000 to £48,000.

Key takeaways

  • There are three main ways to pay: per video (freelancer), per month (subscription service or agency) or per year (an employee).
  • Work out a cost per finished video so you can compare options fairly.
  • Length, motion graphics, turnaround speed and revisions push the price up more than anything else.
  • Cheap editing that loses viewers can cost more than it saves, so check what the edit does to your retention.
  • Always get the inclusions in writing: captions, thumbnails, Shorts and revisions are often extra.

Freelance editors

Freelancers are the most flexible option. You agree a price per video or per hour, send the footage and a brief, and pay when the edit is done. Marketplaces such as Contra, Upwork and Fiverr let you compare portfolios and reviews.

There is no single market rate. A ten-minute talking-head video with simple cuts is a very different job from a documentary-style video with animated maps, so freelancers quote per project. To compare quotes, send every freelancer the same raw footage and the same brief, and ask for a price that includes revisions.

Good for: low volume, simple formats, testing whether outsourcing works for you.

Subscription editing services

Subscription services sell a set amount of editing each month. Two examples, from their own pricing pages on 13 November 2026:

  • Vidchops sells credits, where one credit is one long video (5 to 60 minutes) or four short videos. Its Weekly plan is listed at $495 a month for four credits, and its Pro plan at $995 for eight. It lists subtitles as an extra $95 a month.
  • Vidpros lists a Starter plan at $1,000 a month for 5 to 10 long videos or 20 to 40 short ones, a Growth plan at $2,000 and a Scale plan at $4,000, with a dedicated editor from Growth upwards.

Read the small print. Output often resets monthly and does not roll over, and revisions may count towards your monthly output.

Good for: creators who upload every week and want predictable capacity.

Hiring an editor in-house

An employee costs more in total but gives you the most control. The National Careers Service lists the average salary for a video editor at £24,000 for a starter and £48,000 for someone experienced, with typical hours of 39 to 45 a week. On top of salary you pay employer costs, software and equipment.

Good for: channels publishing most days, or a business running several channels.

What pushes the price up

FactorWhy it costs more

Thumbnails, captions and ShortsOften add-ons, not part of the base price
Retention workStudying your analytics and restructuring the video takes longer than trimming pauses

The last row matters more than most creators expect. YouTube says its recommendations learn from clicks, watch time, survey responses, sharing and likes, and the audience retention report shows exactly where viewers leave. An editor who only tidies footage is cheaper, but if your intro loses half your audience, a cheaper edit does not fix the real problem.

Priya should still ask each option for a paid test edit and check the 30-second retention figure on that video against her usual number. The cheapest option per video is only good value if viewers keep watching.

How to get an accurate quote

  1. Pick one real video as your test, with raw footage ready.
  2. Write a one-page brief: length, style, examples you like, and what to cut.
  3. List the extras you need: captions, thumbnails, Shorts, music licensing.
  4. Ask about revisions and turnaround in writing.
  5. Compare cost per finished video, not the headline monthly figure.
  6. Check the result in YouTube Studio a week after publishing.

FAQ

Is it cheaper to hire a freelancer or a subscription service?

For one or two videos a month, a freelancer is usually cheaper and more flexible. Once you upload every week, a subscription often works out cheaper per video and saves you hiring and briefing new people.

Do YouTube editors charge per video or per hour?

Both. Freelancers commonly quote per video or per hour, subscription services charge a monthly fee for a set output, and employees are paid a salary.

What is usually not included in the price?

Check thumbnails, captions, Shorts, music licensing, motion graphics and extra revisions. Some services list subtitles or animations as extras, so get the full list in writing.

Should I pay for a test edit?

Yes. A paid test on one of your real videos shows quality, communication and turnaround before you commit, and it is fairer to the editor than asking for free work.

Does a more expensive editor get more views?

Not automatically. Views depend on topic, title, thumbnail and audience as well as the edit. Judge an editor by how your retention and watch time change, not by price.

Can I edit my own videos instead?

Yes. Many creators edit their first videos themselves and only hire once editing takes more time than filming and planning.
