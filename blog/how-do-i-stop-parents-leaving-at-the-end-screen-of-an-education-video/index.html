How do I stop parents leaving at the end screen of an education video?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Deliver the last useful point before the end screen, then tell parents in one sentence what to watch next and why. Treat the end screen as a single clear next step, not a menu. Parents are usually watching to solve a specific problem (a homework topic, a bedtime routine, a learning difficulty), so once the problem is solved they leave unless the next video solves the next problem. Also check one technical point first: YouTube end screens are not available on videos set as "made for kids".

Key takeaways

  • YouTube says "end screens can be added to the last 5-20 seconds of a video", the video must be "at least 25 seconds long", and 16:9 videos can have up to four elements (YouTube Help).
  • The same page lists exclusions: end screens are unavailable for videos set as "made for kids", and viewers "can't see end screens on mobile web" apart from iPad, so some viewers will never see yours.
  • YouTube's retention report says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). A drop in the last seconds is normal; the question is whether it is a cliff before your last point.
  • Mayer's segmenting principle says "people learn better when the information is presented in user-paced segments" (Dartmouth); a "next lesson" suggestion fits that idea.

Why parents leave at the end

A parent watching an education video is rarely browsing. They have a goal: explain fractions tonight, understand a reading method, plan a revision timetable. When the goal is met, the video has done its job and the viewer stops. Leaving is not necessarily a failure. What you can influence is whether a parent who would benefit from a second video knows it exists and sees a reason to click.

Common end-of-video mistakes are a long outro that repeats the lesson, a spoken list of five other videos, an end screen with four competing options, and a "thanks for watching" that gives no direction. Each adds friction after the viewer has their answer.

First check: is the video made for kids?

YouTube's end screen help says end screens are unavailable for videos set as "made for kids". If your video is aimed at children, end screens will not be available and you need other routes: a playlist, a related-videos approach in the description, or an on-screen suggestion in the video itself that a parent can act on. If your video is aimed at parents or teachers, the audience setting should reflect that. Check YouTube's guidance on audience settings for the right choice; this page cannot decide that for you.

The bridge sentence is the part most videos skip. It names the viewer's likely next problem and points to the answer, which gives a parent a reason to click.

Step-by-step: build a parent-friendly end screen

  1. List the next problem. Ask what a parent who just watched this will need next. For a times-tables video, that might be "practice games at home" or "what if my child gets stuck on the 7s".
  2. Make that next video exist. If the next video does not exist, create a playlist order that does.
  3. Say it in one sentence before the end screen. Spoken, plain, with the benefit: "The next video shows five-minute practice games."
  4. Use the 5 to 20 second window. YouTube allows end screens in the last 5 to 20 seconds; use the shorter end of that range when the content is finished.
  5. Limit elements. One video, one playlist or subscribe. Four options is a menu, and menus slow decisions.
  6. Leave space in the edit. The end screen overlays your video; keep the last seconds visually calm so labels and thumbnails are readable.
  7. Check mobile. YouTube notes viewers "can't see end screens on mobile web", so add a spoken pointer and a pinned description link as well.
  8. Add chapters. YouTube chapters need a first timestamp at 00:00, at least three timestamps and 10 seconds per chapter (YouTube Help). Parents who skim can jump to the part they need and may stay for more.

Choosing what to show

  • A "next step" video is better than "our most popular video". Relevance beats popularity.
  • A playlist works when the topics are a sequence (a reading series, a maths topic).
  • A subscribe element is fine if you add a reason ("a new short lesson each week"), and only if you will deliver it.
  • A link to a resource (a printable) is only available to channels with link elements, which YouTube says are available to YouTube Partner Program members.

What not to do

  • Do not repeat the whole lesson in the outro. A 20-second recap after the end of the lesson invites exit.
  • Do not say "before you go" at the start of the outro. It signals the end and speeds up leaving.
  • Do not promise a video you have not made. Trust matters with parents and teachers.
  • Do not use fake urgency. Education audiences notice it, and it can damage credibility.
  • Do not bury the end screen under busy visuals. Keep the background plain.

Example: a primary-school maths channel

Example: a channel's "Learn the 7 times table" video ends with a 40-second recap, a spoken list of three other videos and a four-element end screen. The creator re-edits the next upload: the final tip lands at 4:10, one sentence at 4:15 says "If your child keeps forgetting the 7s, the next video has a five-minute game", and a two-element end screen (one video, one subscribe) runs for 12 seconds. How to judge success

A retention graph always drops at the end; do not chase a flat line there. Instead, check whether the drop begins earlier than before (a sign the outro is dragging) and whether the end-screen element is clicked. Remember that some viewers cannot see end screens and that YouTube notes your end screen "may not always show, or may show differently than designed". Treat the click numbers as a guide.

How long can an end screen be?

YouTube allows the last 5 to 20 seconds, on videos at least 25 seconds long.

Why is there no end screen on my video?

Videos set as made for kids cannot use end screens, and some platforms do not show them.

How many elements should I use?

Should I recap the lesson at the end?
