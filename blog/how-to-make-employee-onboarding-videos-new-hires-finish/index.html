How do I make employee onboarding videos that new hires actually finish?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Break onboarding into a series of short videos, each a few minutes long and each answering one question a new starter actually has ("how do I book leave?", "who do I ask about X?", "what does good look like in my first month?"). Film them informally with real colleagues, open each one with what the viewer will be able to do by the end, show the actual screens and places, and caption everything. Research on video lectures found engagement falls sharply as videos get longer, and GitLab's handbook tells its teams to turn long recordings into "a series of short, digestible micro videos".

Key takeaways

  • One video, one question. Aim for videos under about six minutes; many can be under two.
  • Open with the outcome: "By the end of this you'll have your laptop set up and be in the right Slack channels."
  • Informal and real beats polished: a colleague at their desk is more watchable than a studio presenter.
  • Show, don't describe: screen recordings for systems, walking footage for places.
  • Order the videos by when the new starter needs them, not by department.
  • Captions, chapters and a written summary under each video help people find answers later.

What the evidence and guidance say

SourceWhat it found or advises
Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014), 6.9 million edX sessionsMedian engagement time was at most about six minutes regardless of video length; videos filmed in an informal office setting held viewers longer than studio-produced ones; the authors recommend segmenting into "chunks shorter than 6 minutes"
GitLab video playbook"Think short and sweet"; long content "can and should be created as a series of short, digestible micro videos"; "clean up auto-generated captions"
CIPD: employee induction"An employee's first impressions of an organisation can have a significant impact on job satisfaction and their integration"

The edX study was about students on online courses, not employees, so treat it as a strong pointer rather than a rule for your company. The pattern (shorter, informal, planned for video) is consistent across these sources.

Why new hires drift off onboarding videos

  • One long welcome video covering history, values, org chart, benefits and IT in 45 minutes.
  • Information they don't need yet: the expenses policy in week one when they won't travel for months.
  • Recorded slide decks with someone reading bullet points.
  • Out-of-date screens that don't match what the new starter sees.
  • No reason to watch: nothing tells them what they'll be able to do afterwards.

Plan the library around the new starter's first weeks

WhenExample videos (2 to 5 minutes each)
Before day oneWelcome from the manager; what to expect on day one; how to get in or log on
Day oneSetting up your laptop and accounts; where things are (office walk-through or tool tour); who's who in your team
Week oneHow we communicate (which channel for what); how to book leave and report sickness; how your first month will be measured
First monthHow a typical project or customer request runs; the main systems you'll use, one video each; who to ask about what
LaterExpenses, benefits, training budgets, career conversations

Each video should still make sense on its own, because people will come back to it months later from a search.

How to structure each video

  1. Outcome first (5 to 10 seconds). "In three minutes you'll know how to raise an IT ticket and when to just message the helpdesk."
  2. The steps, shown. Screen recording or real footage, narrated by someone who actually does it.
  3. The common mistake. "Most new people email IT directly; it goes to a shared inbox no one watches."
  4. Where to go next. Link to the next video or the written policy.

Cut every wait (loading screens, logging in, "um, where is it..."). GitLab's playbook notes that machine captions "often misspell or mangle technical terms", so check names of your own tools.

Production tips that make a difference

  • Use people the new hire will meet. Their manager, their buddy, the person in IT they'll actually message.
  • Film at desks and in real rooms, not in front of a logo wall.
  • Keep the camera simple. GitLab's playbook says that with a laptop camera "you have enough equipment".
  • Show the face at the start, then switch to the screen. Guo and colleagues found that cutting between the presenter and slides held attention better than slides alone.
  • Date each video and give it an owner, so it gets updated when the system changes.

Example: replacing a 50-minute welcome recording

Example: a 120-person company sends new starters a 50-minute recorded welcome webinar. The analytics show most people stop in the first ten minutes.

They replace it with eleven videos of one to four minutes: a two-minute founder welcome, a manager video per team, an IT setup screen recording, a building walk-through filmed on a phone, and separate videos for leave, payroll questions and the first-month check-in. Each has a one-line outcome at the top and a written summary below. The full webinar stays available for anyone who wants it. Company and details are illustrative.

Measuring whether it works

  • Watch-through rate per video (most video platforms show where viewers stop).
  • The questions new starters still ask in their first fortnight: each repeated question is a missing or unclear video.
  • Feedback in the first-month check-in: which videos helped, which were out of date.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches how to keep viewers watching to the end?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks and pacing for £1,720 one-time. CIPD and GitLab publish free guidance on induction and internal video.

How long should an onboarding video be?

Short enough to cover one task or question, usually a few minutes. Research on lecture videos found engagement capped at around six minutes.

Should onboarding videos be professionally produced?

Not necessarily. Informal videos filmed by real colleagues often hold attention better and are easier to update.

Should I make one welcome video or many?

Many short ones, ordered by when the new starter needs them, plus an optional longer welcome.

Do internal videos need captions?

Yes. Captions help people watching without sound and make the videos accessible; check auto-captions for tool names.
