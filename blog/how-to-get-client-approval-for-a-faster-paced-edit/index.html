How do I get client approval for a faster-paced edit?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Don't argue about taste; show the difference. Put the client's current cut next to a tighter version, point to the exact moments in their retention data where viewers leave, and make clear that their must-haves (brand, key message, legal lines) are still in. Then offer a low-risk test: run both versions to the same audience, or publish the faster cut once, and agree in advance which number decides. Most clients approve when they see their own data rather than an editor's opinion.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's audience retention help says dips mean viewers are "abandoning or skipping at that specific part", and suggests you "experiment with different styles" in the first 30 seconds. That gives you neutral evidence to show a client.
  • Google's ABCDs for video ads recommend you "Get to the heart of the story faster, and use engaging pacing and tight framing".
  • Pace has been rising for decades: Cornell research on 150 popular films found shots shortened from about five to ten seconds in earlier eras to "about four seconds" (Cornell Chronicle, 2010). Audiences are used to faster cutting.
  • Meta's creative testing tool lets you compare two to five ads with a small share of budget (Meta recommends under 20%), per Jon Loomer's walkthrough. A test turns a debate into a result.
  • "Faster" should mean less waiting, not more effects. Cut pauses, repeats and slow intros before adding flashy transitions.

Why clients push back

"We paid for that drone shot"Sunk costKeep a shorter version of it where it helps the story

The approval process that works

  1. Agree the goal first. "More people reach the offer" or "more people watch past 30 seconds". Write it in the brief.
  2. Show their data. Screenshot the retention graph or ad quartile numbers and mark the dips.
  3. Present two cuts side by side. Current pace and the faster version, same content.
  4. Walk through the must-have list. Tick each one off in the new cut.
  5. Explain changes in plain words. "We removed 6 seconds of pauses and moved the product shot to second 2."
  6. Offer a test with a decision rule. "If the faster version gets more ThruPlays per impression after a week at the same budget, we use it."
  7. Report back, whichever way it goes.

Quick checklist for the approval meeting

  • The goal is written down and agreed before you show anything.
  • You have one screenshot of the client's own retention data with the dips marked.
  • Both cuts are ready to play back to back.
  • Every must-have item is listed and ticked off in the faster cut.
  • Each change is described in one plain sentence.
  • You have proposed a test, with the deciding metric and the end date agreed in advance.
  • You have planned when you'll report the result, whichever version wins.
  • Your tone is curious, not defensive; the data does the persuading.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches pacing rules a client can sign off on?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook from Asendify includes a cheat sheet and templates for £1,720 one-time. Google's ABCDs guidance is free.

What if the client still says no?

Deliver their version, keep the faster cut on file, and suggest testing it next time. Record the agreed goal so you can compare.

Is faster always better?

No. Shots need time to be understood, and some brands need a calmer feel. Cut waiting, not meaning.

How do I show retention data to a client who isn't technical?

Use one graph with arrows on the dips and one sentence per arrow saying what happens on screen there.

Should the brief mention pace?

Yes. Agree a target, such as "hook in the first 2 seconds, no pause longer than half a second", before editing starts.
