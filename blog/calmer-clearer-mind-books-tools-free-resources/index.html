Mind and self-improvement: books, tools and free resources

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 23 October 2026 · Last updated 4 November 2026

A calmer, clearer mind usually comes from a few practical habits rather than one big change: handling stress and sleep, sorting real problems from worry, thinking more carefully about decisions, setting up your space so focus is easier, and using caffeine without wrecking your sleep. Start with free NHS and official resources, then add books or tools for a specific gap.

Key takeaways

  • Start with free official resources such as NHS Every Mind Matters and its breathing exercise.
  • Pick one area to work on at a time: stress, overwhelm, decisions, environment or caffeine.
  • Small, repeatable habits beat big one-off changes.
  • Books and apps are not treatment; see a doctor if problems persist or affect daily life.
  • If you are in crisis, contact emergency services or a crisis line now.

Disclosure: Asendify publishes six of the resources below (books and their free tools). They are ours. Free resources from the NHS, food-safety regulators and others are listed first, because most people should start there.

This is general information, not medical advice. If you are struggling or thinking about harming yourself, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line now (UK: Samaritans 116 123; US: call or text 988; India: Tele-MANAS 14416). Books and apps are not a replacement for a doctor, a therapist or medication you have been prescribed.

What this category covers

  • Stress and sleep: calming the body and switching off at night.
  • Overwhelm: separating what you can act on from what you cannot.
  • Clear thinking: spotting biases and making better decisions.
  • Environment and habits: designing your space so focus is the easy option.
  • Caffeine: getting the benefit without losing sleep.
  • Big questions: the simulation argument, as a thought experiment.

Start here: the best free resources (not ours)

  • NHS Every Mind Matters: the NHS mental wellbeing site. Its short Mind Plan quiz asks five questions and gives you a personalised plan, with guidance on stress, anxiety and sleep.
  • NHS breathing exercise for stress: breathe in gently through your nose for a count of 5, out through your mouth for a count of 5, and keep going for at least 5 minutes.
  • Calibrate Your Judgment by Clearer Thinking: a free tool, with a free account, that trains you to match your confidence to how often you are actually right.
  • EFSA on caffeine: the European Food Safety Authority's view that, for healthy adults, single doses up to 200 mg and up to 400 mg a day do not raise safety concerns, and that pregnant women should stay at or below 200 mg a day. It also notes that 100 mg close to bedtime may affect sleep in some adults.
  • FDA on caffeine: the US Food and Drug Administration's guide, which gives similar limits and lists who should talk to a health professional first.
  • simulation-argument.com: the home of Nick Bostrom's 2003 paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?", with explainers and replies.

Worked example: a two-week reset

Example: Aisha works from home, drinks three coffees a day with the last at about 4pm, and lies awake most nights going over her to-do list. These details are illustrative.

Week 1:

  • She takes the NHS Mind Plan quiz and picks two actions from her plan.
  • She does the NHS breathing exercise for 5 minutes before bed each night.
  • She moves her last coffee to before 1pm. Her total stays at about three cups, which for brewed coffee the FDA puts at well under 400 mg in most cases.

Week 2:

  • Before bed, she writes everything on her mind on paper and marks each item do, plan or let go.
  • She moves her phone out of the bedroom.

After two weeks she notices she falls asleep sooner on most nights. That is her own observation, not a measured result, and everyone responds differently. If her sleep had not improved, or if low mood or worry were affecting her days, the next step would be her GP rather than another book.

How to judge any self-help resource

  • Does it say who it is not for? Honest resources do.
  • Does it point you to professional help where needed? It should.
  • Does it make promises? Be wary of anything that guarantees results.
  • Can you try one small step today? Good advice is practical.

FAQ

What is the best free place to start for stress?

The NHS Every Mind Matters site and its breathing exercise for stress. Both are free and official.

Can a book replace therapy or medication?

No. Self-help books can teach useful habits, but they do not replace professional care. Never stop prescribed medication without talking to your doctor.

How much caffeine is safe?

EFSA says up to 400 mg a day and up to 200 mg in a single dose do not raise safety concerns for healthy adults; pregnant women should stay at or below 200 mg a day.

Is the simulation book about religion or science?

It explains a philosophical argument, Bostrom's simulation argument, as a thought experiment rather than as proof either way.

When should I see a doctor instead of using self-help?

If stress, low mood, anxiety or poor sleep lasts for weeks, gets worse or affects your work, relationships or daily life, speak to your GP or doctor.
