What is the disposition effect in trading?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 19 November 2026 · Last updated 19 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The disposition effect is the habit of selling winning trades too soon and holding losing trades too long, usually in the hope of getting back to break-even. Economists Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman named it in 1985, and Terrance Odean's study of 10,000 brokerage accounts found investors strongly preferred selling winners to losers, even though later performance did not justify it.

This page is education only, not financial advice. Trading is risky and you can lose money.

Key takeaways

  • Shefrin and Statman described the effect as a "predisposition to get-even-itis": the urge to at least get back what you paid.
  • Odean's study of trading records for 10,000 accounts at a large US discount broker found a clear preference for selling winners over losers.
  • That preference was not explained by rebalancing or trading costs, and the stocks investors held on to did not go on to justify holding them.
  • In taxable accounts it is doubly costly: selling winners realises taxable gains while holding losers delays losses you could use. Odean found tax-motivated selling of losers mainly in December.
  • A trading journal that records your planned exit and what actually happened is the simplest way to see whether you do it.

Where the idea comes from

In a 2010 NOVA article, The Disposition Effect, finance writer David Adler explains that behavioural economists Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman coined the term and linked it to "get-even-itis". He describes the result as a slow version of buying high and selling low: you cash in the trades that are working and keep the ones that are not.

The idea draws on a well-known finding in psychology: losses feel more painful than equal gains feel good. Selling a loser makes the loss real. Holding it keeps alive the hope that it will come back.

What the research found

Terrance Odean, of the University of California, Berkeley, tested the effect on real trades. His paper Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?, published in the Journal of Finance in 1998, analysed trading records for 10,000 accounts at a large discount brokerage. A later working paper by Brad Barber and Terrance Odean, The Courage of Misguided Convictions (1999), summarises the numbers from the same 10,000 accounts and 97,483 trades: over the full year investors realised about 14.8% of their gains but only 9.8% of their losses, so a rising stock was roughly 50% more likely to be sold than a falling one. The winners they sold went on to beat the losers they kept by about 3.4% over the following year. The ratio fell from 2.1 in January to 0.85 in December, consistent with year-end tax-loss selling.

The CFA Institute's digest of the paper summarises the method and findings:

Question Odean askedWhat he found
Do investors sell winners more readily than losers?Yes, strongly, across the year
Is it just rebalancing a portfolio?No
Is it avoiding higher trading costs on low-priced stocks?No
Do the losers they hold go on to do better?No: later performance does not justify it
Is there an exception?December, when tax-motivated selling of losers is more common
Does it cost money?Yes, especially in taxable accounts, where it lowers after-tax returns

The digest notes the result held whether or not commissions and dividends were included.

Why traders do it

  • Loss aversion. A realised loss hurts more than a paper loss, so selling is postponed.
  • The purchase price as an anchor. Your entry price means nothing to the market, but it becomes the line you want to get back to.
  • Wanting to feel right. Banking a winner gives a quick sense of success; closing a loser admits a mistake.
  • No exit plan. If you did not decide your stop and target before entering, every decision is made in the moment, when emotions are loudest.

How it shows up in a trader's numbers

The disposition effect usually leaves a clear fingerprint in a trading journal:

Sign in your recordsWhat it suggests
Average winning trade is much smaller than average losing tradeWinners cut early, losers left to run
Losing trades held far longer than winnersWaiting to get back to break-even
Many exits before the planned targetTaking profit out of nerves
Stops moved further away after entryRefusing to take the planned loss

A high win rate can hide all of this. If you win 65% of your trades but your average loss is twice your average win, you can still lose money.

How to reduce it

  1. Set the stop and target before you enter, and write them down.
  2. Use orders, not willpower. A resting stop order executes without asking how you feel.
  3. Never move a stop further from your entry. Moving it closer to lock in profit is a different decision.
  4. Ask the "fresh money" question. Would you buy this position today, at this price, with new money? If not, why are you holding it?
  5. Review exits weekly, comparing what you planned with what you did, and put a money figure on the difference.

Example: the hidden cost of early exits

Over a month, Tom takes 20 trades, risking £100 on each with a planned target of £200.

  • He wins 12 and loses 8, a 60% win rate that feels good.
  • But he closes his winners early, at an average of £80 profit, instead of the planned £200.
  • He moves his stop on three losing trades, so his average loss is £140 instead of £100.

His result: 12 × £80 = £960 won, 8 × £140 = £1,120 lost, a net loss of £160.

Had he followed his plan with the same entries, his winners would have averaged £200 and his losers £100: 12 × £200 − 8 × £100 = £1,600. The gap between the plan and what he did, £1,760, is the cost of the disposition effect in one month. These figures are illustrative, but the pattern is the one Odean's data describes.

Yes. Asendify's Trader OS is a one-time-payment Notion template that calculates the money each mistake costs, along with P&L and R-multiple. A self-built spreadsheet is a free alternative.

Who discovered the disposition effect?

Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman named it in 1985. Terrance Odean's 1998 study of 10,000 brokerage accounts is the best-known test of it.

Is the disposition effect the same as loss aversion?

No. Loss aversion is the broader feeling that losses hurt more than gains; the disposition effect is one trading behaviour it helps explain.

Is it ever right to take profits early?

Yes, if it follows a rule you set in advance, such as taking half off at a target. The problem is exiting early out of nerves, not by plan.

Does the disposition effect apply to crypto and forex?

The research focused on shares, but the same pattern of cutting winners and holding losers can show up in any market. Your own journal is the best test.
