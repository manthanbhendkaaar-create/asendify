What is the best book or course for making event videos that hold attention?

How to choose

  1. Can you already edit a basic video? If not, start with free software training. A retention book is too early.
  2. Where do viewers leave? If YouTube's key moments report shows many people gone before 30 seconds, you need help with openings. That's where retention methods focus.
  3. Do you learn from reading and applying alone? A book or self-paced course suits you. If you need someone to look at your work, choose feedback instead.
  4. What is your budget relative to your video spend? If you spend thousands a year on event filming, improving the edit can matter more than another camera day. If you make one video a year, free guides may be enough.

What a retention method adds to event video

Event guides tell you what to film and how long to make it. A retention method tells you how to cut it so people stay:

  • Cold open: start on the most interesting moment, not the venue exterior.
  • Intro killers: logo stings, greetings and slow wide shots that lose viewers.
  • Promise, Proof, Path: a simple intro structure that tells viewers what they'll get.
  • Open loops: questions that keep people watching to the end.
  • Pacing and pattern interrupts: changes that stop a two-minute recap feeling long.
  • The 30-second scorecard: a way to judge an opening before and after publishing.

These are the techniques Asendify's playbook teaches. It applies them to YouTube videos; event teams need to translate them to recaps, teasers and ads.

Example: three teams, three choices

Example 1: A small charity's comms officer has never edited video. Best choice: free software training, then Vimeo's recap guide. A paid retention book can wait.

Example 2: An events agency's in-house editor makes 30 recaps and promos a year and sees most viewers leave in the first 15 seconds. Best choice: a retention method such as the playbook, applied to every new opening, tracked with YouTube's report.

Example 3: A conference organiser wants someone to review their recap before it goes live. Best choice: an experienced editor or coach. A book can't watch your video.

Quick checklist before you buy any course or book

  • Check your last few videos' 30-second figures and biggest dips, so you know the problem.
  • Read the sales page for what is and isn't included, such as feedback or software training.
  • Look for methods you can apply to your own footage straight away.
  • Be wary of any promise of views, sales or income.
  • Try the free guides first if you make only a few videos a year.
  • Decide how you'll judge it: the next five videos' openings and dips.
  • Compare the price with what you spend on filming each year.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches cold opens, open loops and a 30-second scorecard for video?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches them, with a workbook and templates. Google's ABCDs and Vimeo's recap guide are free alternatives.

Is a £1,000 book worth it for event videos?
