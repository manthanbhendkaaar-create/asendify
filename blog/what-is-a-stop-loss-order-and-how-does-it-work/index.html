What is a stop-loss order, and how does it work?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 20 November 2026 · Last updated 20 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A stop-loss order is an instruction to your broker to sell (or buy back a short position) once the price reaches a level you choose, called the stop price. When the stop is hit, the order becomes a market order and fills at the best price available, which in a fast market can be well below your stop. A stop-limit order controls the price but may not fill at all.

This page is education only, not financial advice. Trading is risky and you can lose money.

Key takeaways

  • A sell stop sits below the current price to limit a loss on a position you own; a buy stop sits above it to limit a loss on a short.
  • Once triggered, a stop becomes a market order: "The stop price is not the guaranteed execution price," says the SEC.
  • A stop-limit order becomes a limit order when triggered, so it fills only at your limit or better, and may not fill if price moves past it.
  • A trailing stop moves up with the price by a fixed amount or percentage and stays put when price falls.
  • Brief, sharp moves can trigger a stop even if the price quickly recovers, and a filled order cannot be reversed.

How a stop order works

The SEC's Investor.gov page on types of orders defines a stop order as one that becomes a market order once the price reaches a specified stop price. A sell stop is set below the current price, usually to limit a loss or protect a profit on something you own. A buy stop is set above the current price, usually to limit a loss on a short sale.

The key point is what happens after the trigger. A market order is guaranteed to execute, but not at a guaranteed price. The SEC's investor bulletin on stop, stop-limit and trailing stop orders (updated August 2026) says "the stop price is not the guaranteed execution price for a stop order", and the fill can differ substantially from the stop depending on liquidity. It also warns that a short-term move during the day can trigger the order at a price well below where the market closes.

Three kinds of stop compared

Order typeWhat happens when triggeredMain advantageMain risk
Stop (stop-loss)Becomes a market orderWill almost always get you outFill can be well below the stop in fast markets
Stop-limitBecomes a limit order at your limit priceControls the worst price you acceptMay not fill if price gaps past your limit
Trailing stopStop moves up as price rises, stays fixed as it fallsLocks in more profit as a trade worksNormal swings can trigger it early

The SEC's stop-limit example: a sell stop-limit with a $3.00 stop and a $2.50 limit becomes active at $3.00 but only executes at $2.50 or better. If the price falls straight to $2.40, it does not sell.

Its trailing-stop example: you buy at $20, the price rises to $22, and you set a $1 trailing stop at $21. As the price peaks at $24, the stop follows to $23. If the price then falls to $23, the order triggers, though the fill may differ from $23.

Stops in volatile markets

FINRA's guide to stop orders in volatile markets adds practical warnings:

  • In fast markets you could receive much less than your stop price; brokers must execute promptly at "the best possible price reasonably available", not at your stop.
  • Brief, sharp price drops can trigger a stop even if the price recovers soon after, and the trade cannot be undone.
  • Some exchanges no longer accept stop orders, though many brokers still offer them. (The SEC adds that brokers differ on whether triggers use last-trade or quoted prices.)
  • A stop-limit may suit you better when controlling price matters more than certainty of getting out.

Where to put a stop

A stop works best as part of a plan, not a guess. Two common approaches:

  • Where the trade idea is wrong. Below a recent swing low, support level or pattern, so that a hit means your reason for the trade no longer holds.
  • Not too tight. A stop inside normal day-to-day movement will be hit by noise. Many traders look at a measure of typical range before placing it.

Then size the position from the stop, not the other way round. If you risk a fixed amount per trade, the distance to your stop decides how many units you buy. Worked example: sizing a position from a stop

Example (illustrative): Sam has a £10,000 account and risks 1% (£100) per trade. He wants to buy a share at £50.00 with a stop at £47.50.

StepCalculationResult
Risk per trade1% x £10,000£100
Risk per share£50.00 - £47.50£2.50
Position size£100 / £2.5040 shares (£2,000 position)
If stopped at £47.5040 x £2.50£100 loss (1R)
If the price gaps to £46.00 overnight40 x £4.00£160 loss (1.6R)

The last row is why a stop is a limit on intended risk, not a guarantee. Does a stop-loss guarantee my exit price?

No. A triggered stop becomes a market order, and the SEC says the stop price is not the guaranteed execution price.

What is the difference between a stop and a stop-limit order?

A stop becomes a market order and almost always fills; a stop-limit becomes a limit order and fills only at your limit or better, so it may not fill.

What is a trailing stop?

A stop set a fixed amount or percentage from the market price that rises as the price rises and stays fixed when it falls.

Can a stop be triggered by a brief dip?

Yes. FINRA warns that short, sharp moves can trigger a stop even if the price recovers, and the trade cannot be reversed.

How far away should my stop be?

Where your trade idea would be proven wrong, and outside normal price noise. Then size the position so that a stop-out costs a fixed share of your account.
