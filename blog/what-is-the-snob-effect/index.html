What is the snob effect in economics?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 19 November 2026 · Last updated 19 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The snob effect is when people want something less because other people are buying it too. Economist Harvey Leibenstein defined it in 1950 as demand that "is decreased owing to the fact that others are also consuming the same commodity". It is the opposite of the bandwagon effect, and it explains why exclusive brands limit supply, avoid discounts and sometimes make their logos smaller.

This page is general education about economics and consumer behaviour, not financial advice.

Key takeaways

  • Leibenstein's 1950 paper in the Quarterly Journal of Economics set out three "non-functional" influences on demand: the bandwagon, snob and Veblen effects.
  • Snob effect: demand falls as more people own the thing. Veblen effect: demand rises because the price is higher. Leibenstein said the first depends on others' consumption, the second on price.
  • A 2010 Journal of Marketing study found that among Gucci and Louis Vuitton handbags, the quieter the logo, the higher the price, consistent with the wealthiest buyers avoiding what everyone can recognise.
  • Counterfeits copied loud designs far more than quiet ones, which pushes status-seeking buyers further towards subtle signals.
  • The snob effect is about distinction, not quality: the product can be identical, but fewer owners make it more desirable to some people.

Leibenstein's three effects

In Bandwagon, Snob, and Veblen Effects in the Theory of Consumers' Demand (Quarterly Journal of Economics, vol. 64, 1950, pp. 183-207), Harvey Leibenstein separated three ways other people affect what we want:

EffectLeibenstein's definitionWhat drives itExample
BandwagonDemand "increased due to the fact that others are also consuming the same commodity"How many others own itA trainer brand everyone at school wears
SnobDemand "decreased owing to the fact that others are also consuming the same commodity"How many others own itDropping a brand once it becomes common
VeblenDemand increased "because it bears a higher rather than a lower price"The price itselfA watch bought partly because it costs so much

Leibenstein put the key distinction plainly: the snob effect "is a function of the consumption of others", while the Veblen effect "is a function of price". In practice they often travel together, because high prices keep the number of owners low.

Where it comes from

The roots go back to Thorstein Veblen. In chapter 4 of The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899), he wrote that "conspicuous consumption of valuable goods is a means of reputability to the gentleman of leisure". If owning something is meant to show you are set apart, it loses its point once everyone has it. Leibenstein turned that intuition into a model of how demand curves bend.

Evidence: quiet logos cost more

Young Jee Han, Joseph Nunes and Xavier Drèze tested this in Signaling Status with Luxury Goods: The Role of Brand Prominence (Journal of Marketing, vol. 74, July 2010). They sorted consumers into four groups by wealth and need for status:

GroupWealthNeed for statusPreference
PatriciansHighLowQuiet branding only insiders recognise
ParvenusHighHighLoud logos to separate from the less wealthy
PoseursLowerHighImitate parvenus, often with counterfeits
ProletariansLowerLowLargely indifferent

Their findings fit the snob effect closely:

  • Price falls as logos get louder. Each one-point rise on their 7-point brand-prominence scale went with about $122 lower price for Gucci handbags and about $26 for Louis Vuitton.
  • Counterfeiters copy the loud ones. Across 682 counterfeit listings, copied Louis Vuitton bags scored 5.41 for prominence against 3.79 for uncopied ones.
  • It is not just handbags. Each 1 cm larger Mercedes emblem went with a price about $5,000 lower, and 45% of existing bag styles had been copied at least once.
  • The wealthy avoid the obvious. In a choice test, about 14% of patricians picked the loud bag, against 72% of parvenus and 75% of poseurs.

In other words, as a design becomes widely recognised and copied, the buyers most focused on distinction move away from it. That is the snob effect at work in real prices.

How companies use it

  • Limited runs and waiting lists keep the number of owners low.
  • Avoiding discounts and outlets, so the brand never becomes common.
  • Quiet branding that only insiders recognise.
  • Numbered items, where a low number signals you were early or rare.

The snob effect also has limits. If a product becomes too hard to get, people may give up on it, and a brand that relies only on exclusivity can lose its appeal if the "right" people stop buying.

Example: one jacket, two markets

A small label sells a jacket for £400. In the first year, it sells 2,000, mostly to fashion insiders. In the second year, a celebrity wears it and a high-street chain launches a lookalike. Sales jump to 20,000 as the bandwagon effect kicks in.

But by the third year, the original buyers have moved on: the jacket is now "everywhere", and some insiders say they would not be seen in it. If the label cuts the price to £250 to chase the mass market, it may speed up that exit. If it limits production and launches a new, quieter design, it keeps its original customers but sells fewer jackets.

Both choices are rational. They just serve different buyers: one relies on the bandwagon effect, the other on the snob effect.

What is the difference between the snob effect and the Veblen effect?

Leibenstein said the snob effect depends on how many others consume a good, while the Veblen effect depends on its price.

Is the snob effect the opposite of the bandwagon effect?

Yes. With the bandwagon effect, demand rises as more people buy; with the snob effect, it falls.

Does the snob effect only apply to luxury goods?

No. It can apply to music, holiday spots, restaurants or fashion: anything people use to set themselves apart.

Why do luxury brands avoid big discounts?

Discounts bring in more buyers, which can weaken exclusivity for the existing ones. That is the snob effect in reverse.
