How do you add live captions to a conference live stream on YouTube?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Turn on closed captions in the stream's settings before the event and choose a source. For English, YouTube's automatic live captions are the quickest option, but they need normal latency and vary in accuracy. For a conference with technical terms, names or accents, use a human captioner who sends captions through your encoder (608/708) or YouTube's HTTP caption ingestion URL.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube says "automatic captions for live streams are available in English only", must be turned on per stream, and work only with normal latency.
  • Auto-captions "might misrepresent the spoken content" with accents, noise or overlapping speakers; YouTube encourages professional captions where possible.
  • Human captions can come in through the encoder (embedded 608/708) or a signed HTTP Captions Ingestion URL.
  • YouTube recommends adding a 30- or 60-second broadcast delay before using captions.
  • Live captions don't stay on the replay; new captions are generated afterwards, so check and correct them.

Automatic live captions

YouTube's page on automatic captions says live automatic captions are English only, must be enabled for each stream, and are not available on low or ultra-low latency streams. To use them, turn on Closed captions in the stream settings, choose "Automatic captions" and select English. The same page warns the captions "might misrepresent the spoken content" because of mispronunciations, accents, dialects or background noise, and that poor audio or overlapping speakers can reduce quality or stop captions appearing.

For a conference, that means auto-captions are a fallback, not a plan, especially for panels and Q&A.

Human captions into YouTube

YouTube's live caption requirements describe two routes:

  • HTTP: turn on Closed captions for the event, copy the Captions Ingestion URL (a signed HTTP URL) and give it to your captioner. Each stream entry point takes one caption feed. The page lists supported captioning software.
  • Embedded 608/708: choose "Embedded 608/708" in the stream set-up and have your encoder output EIA-608/CEA-708 captions. YouTube notes it currently supports one caption track.

It also recommends adding a 30-second or 60-second broadcast delay in advanced settings before using captions, which gives the captioner's text time to line up with the picture.

Why captions are worth the effort

The W3C's captions guidance explains that captions provide speech and important non-speech audio for people who are Deaf or hard of hearing, and are used by people who process written information better than audio. Its understanding document for prerecorded captions notes that captions also "identify who is speaking", which matters on a multi-speaker panel. For a conference, captions also help remote viewers watching at work with the sound low, and people following in a second language.

Example: captioning a two-track association conference

Example: a professional body streams two rooms over one day.

  • Two weeks before, they book a remote human captioner for the main room and send the speaker list, session titles and a glossary of acronyms.
  • They schedule the main-room stream, turn on Closed captions with the HTTP option, and send the Captions Ingestion URL to the captioner. They add a 30-second delay.
  • The breakout room stream uses English automatic captions as a lower-cost option, at normal latency.
  • A test stream a week before confirms captions arrive and line up.
  • After the event, the team downloads the replay captions, fixes names and technical terms, and re-uploads corrected caption files for both replays.

Preparing the captioner

  • Send speaker names, job titles and session titles in advance.
  • Send a glossary of acronyms, product names and technical terms.
  • Share slides where possible.
  • Give them a direct audio feed, not a room mic.
  • Agree what happens in breaks (a "captions resume at 11:15" line, or silence).

Test before the day

Run a private or unlisted test stream from the venue with captions switched on, using the same source you'll use on the day. Speak a few names and technical terms from the programme and check how they appear. If you use automatic captions, this test shows quickly whether the room audio is clean enough; if not, improve the audio feed or book a human captioner.

Common mistakes

  • Turning on low latency, which rules out automatic live captions.
  • Relying on auto-captions for a panel with crosstalk.
  • No broadcast delay, so captions lag badly.
  • Sending the captioner nothing in advance.
  • Leaving uncorrected auto-captions on the replay.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches how to edit conference talks so viewers keep watching?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches cold opens and pacing for YouTube uploads such as talk replays. For captioning set-up, YouTube Help and W3C guidance are the free sources.

Does YouTube have automatic live captions?

Yes, in English only, turned on per stream and only at normal latency. Accuracy varies with audio quality and accents.

Can I have live captions in more than one language?

YouTube says it currently supports one caption track for embedded 608/708 captions, and each stream entry point takes one HTTP caption feed.

Do live captions stay on the replay?

No. YouTube generates new captions for the replay, which may differ, so review and correct them.

Why add a broadcast delay?

YouTube recommends a 30- or 60-second delay before using captions so caption text can sync with the video.

Are human captioners worth the cost?

For public conferences with names, jargon and panels, usually yes. Automatic captions are a reasonable fallback for low-stakes streams.
