Should online course videos have background music?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Usually not while you are teaching. In two experiments by Moreno and Mayer, adding background music to a narrated lesson reduced how much learners remembered and how well they applied it, and music plus sound effects was worst. Keep music for the opening seconds, section transitions and promo clips, and leave the explanation itself clear of it.

Key takeaways

  • Moreno and Mayer (2000), Journal of Educational Psychology, tested narrated animations on lightning and car brakes with and without music and sounds (75 learners per experiment). With music, "students remembered significantly less verbal material" and "generated significantly fewer solutions" on transfer questions.
  • The group with both music and sound effects did worst, and the authors advised that designers "should carefully limit the amount of auditory material" in lessons.
  • Mayer's coherence principle, as summarised by Dartmouth: "Learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded, rather than included."
  • Brame's review in CBE-Life Sciences Education calls this "weeding": "the elimination of interesting but extraneous information that does not contribute to the learning goal."
  • The evidence concerns learning from explanations. Music in a promo, trailer or welcome video is a different job.

Why music under narration gets in the way

Learners have limited capacity to take in sound at once. Speech is the main carrier of meaning in most lessons. Music under it adds a second stream that the brain processes whether it wants to or not, especially music with lyrics, strong rhythm or changes in volume. Moreno and Mayer describe the problem as adding "entertaining but irrelevant auditory material".

It also creates practical problems. Many learners watch on laptop speakers or phones, where music and speech blur together. Non-native speakers and people with hearing loss find speech harder to pick out over a music bed. Auto-captions become less accurate when music is loud.

"But silence feels flat"

This is the main reason creators add music. The fix is usually not music, but pace and voice:

  1. Cut pauses and repetition. Dead air feels long because nothing new is happening.
  2. Speak conversationally. Dartmouth's summary of the personalisation principle says learning is better with "a friendly, conversational style".
  3. Change the picture. Move between your face, slides, highlights and screen recordings.
  4. Use short music stings at section breaks rather than a constant bed.
  5. If you must use a bed, keep it very low. Instrumental, no lyrics, steady volume, and dipped well below the voice.

How to check your own mix

Play the lesson on a phone speaker at medium volume and ask someone unfamiliar with the topic to write down three key facts. If they struggle, the music is too loud or should go. Then compare the version with music against one without for a few lessons and look at where learners stop watching. The point is to use music where it helps the viewer, not because silence feels unusual to the person editing.

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers pacing and pattern interrupts with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. The coherence research is free to read.

Does quiet background music hurt learning?

The research tested music under narration and found it reduced recall and transfer. Very quiet music may do less harm, but the safest choice under teaching is none.

Is music fine in a course trailer?

Yes. A trailer helps people decide whether to buy or start; it is not where they learn the content.

What about lo-fi music for study sessions?

That is the learner's choice while studying on their own. Building it into your narration takes the choice away.

Can sound effects help?

Sound effects that carry meaning (a click when a button is pressed) can help. Decorative effects added for energy can distract.
