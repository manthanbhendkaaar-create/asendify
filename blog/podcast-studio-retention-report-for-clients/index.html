What should a podcast production studio put in a retention report for clients?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A useful retention report answers three questions for the client: where did viewers leave, what did we change in the edit, and what will we test next? Use the client's own YouTube Studio numbers, explain each one in a line, and finish with one action per episode. Avoid a wall of charts. The client is paying for decisions, not dashboards. This is a reporting approach drawn from editing practice, so adapt it to what your clients actually ask for.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube Help explains the core reading: a flat line means "viewers are watching that part of your video from start to finish", dips mean "viewers are abandoning or skipping," and spikes mean "more viewers are watching, rewatching, or sharing" (YouTube Help). Quote these definitions in your report so the client can read the graph without you.
  • YouTube also reports an Intro figure, "what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds." It is a clean, comparable number for every episode.
  • You can "see how your video compares to all YouTube videos of similar length" in the same report. That comparison is more fair than comparing a 25-minute episode to a 60-minute one.
  • YouTube's podcast guide says "for any podcast over 20 minutes, chapters are non-negotiable" (YouTube Blog). A chapter-level view is a natural way to explain where the audience goes.

What clients actually want from a report

Clients rarely need every metric. They want to know whether the work is helping and what to do next. A podcast studio report is strongest when it:

  1. Is short enough to read in five minutes.
  2. Uses the same layout each month so changes stand out.
  3. Links every number to a decision.
  4. Is honest about what the data cannot tell you, such as why a specific viewer left.

The one-page structure

1. Summary, in three sentences

Say what changed since the last report, what worked, and what you recommend next. Write it last, but put it first.

2. The episode table

EpisodeLengthIntro (first 30 seconds)Biggest dip (timestamp)Biggest spike (timestamp)Action
Ep 1

Fill this from the client's YouTube Studio. Keep the table to the episodes published in the period. If you cannot pull a number, leave the cell blank and say why.

3. One graph per episode that matters

Choose the best and worst episodes of the period. Show the retention graph with two or three annotations: the hook, the biggest dip and the spike. A reader should understand it in ten seconds.

4. What we changed

List the edit changes you made, in plain language: shorter intro, cut the sponsor read to the middle, added chapters, trimmed the guest introduction. This links the work to the results without claiming credit for things you did not control.

5. What we will test next

One or two tests per month, not ten. For example, "open the next three episodes on the guest's strongest line rather than the host welcome." State what you will look at to judge it.

6. Questions for the client

Things only they can answer: Did a guest promote the episode? Was there a newsletter send? Was the topic seasonal? External factors can explain spikes the edit cannot.

Honest caveats to put in the report

  • The graph shows what, not why. A dip means viewers left; it does not tell you the reason. Say what you think and label it as a hypothesis.
  • Small sample sizes can mislead. One episode is an anecdote. Look for a pattern across three or more.
  • The edit is one input. Titles, thumbnails, topic and promotion also affect who arrives and who stays.
  • Do not promise a result. Report what happened and what you will test.

Report cadence

  • Per episode (optional): a three-line note a few days after publishing: intro figure, biggest dip, one action.
  • Monthly: the one-page report above.
  • Quarterly: a review of tests run and what you learned, so the style guide can change.

How to build the report faster

  1. Create a template with the table and sections above.
  2. Ask the client for viewer access to YouTube Studio so you can pull the numbers yourself.
  3. Keep a running log of edit changes by episode, so the "what we changed" section writes itself.
  4. Keep a library of annotated graphs so patterns are easy to spot over time.
  5. Review the template every quarter and delete anything the client never reads.

Example: a studio report for a business interview show

Example: a studio edits a weekly interview show. In the report, the summary says the intro figure was lower on episodes that opened with the host's welcome and higher on two that opened with a guest quote. The table lists five episodes with dips at the sponsor read, and the action is to move the read to after the first chapter. The test for next month is to open three episodes on the guest's strongest line. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.
