What does a creative strategist do, and how do they work with video ad editors?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

A creative strategist decides what an ad should say and who it should say it to, then turns that into a brief the editor can build from: the audience, the problem, the promise, the hook options and the tests to run. The editor turns the brief into finished variants, with the pacing and visuals that make the first seconds work. Then both read the results and decide what to change. The role has no single definition, and companies split it differently, so treat this as a common pattern rather than a standard.

Key takeaways

  • TikTok's ad guidance gives concrete creative inputs a strategist can brief: "introduce your content proposition in the first 3 seconds," "prioritize your hook in the first 6 seconds," "use captions or text overlays," and feature "creators, employees, or customers" (TikTok Ads Help).
  • Meta's guidance for Reels ads says to "build for vertical," notes that "over 75% of Reels views on Instagram are sound on," and tells advertisers to "put your key messages within the Reels safe zone" (Meta for Developers). A strategist builds those platform rules into the brief.
  • TikTok's blog describes creator tools where you can "paste in your campaign brief and get a curated shortlist" of creators (TikTok for Business), which shows how central the brief is.
  • None of these sources defines the job title. This page describes common practice.

What a creative strategist does

  1. Researches the audience. Who they are, what they worry about, what words they use, what they have tried.
  2. Studies the market. What competitors say, what formats are used and what claims are allowed.
  3. Defines angles. Different reasons to care: price, convenience, fear of missing out, social proof, a specific result the product can honestly support.
  4. Writes briefs. For each ad, the audience, problem, promise, proof, hook options, format, length and call to action.
  5. Plans tests. What to change and what to keep, so results can be read.
  6. Reads results. Looks at the metrics from the ad platform and the business, and turns them into the next round of ideas.
  7. Keeps compliance in mind. Checks claims and disclosure against platform rules.

What the editor does with the brief

  1. Builds the hook options. Three openings of a few seconds each, tested against the same body.
  2. Shapes the pacing. Cuts dead frames, sets the order of proof and benefit and keeps the message in the safe zone.
  3. Adds captions and text. Reviewed and sized for a phone.
  4. Chooses sound. Voice, music and effects.
  5. Exports variants with clear names so results can be matched to changes.
  6. Gives feedback. Tells the strategist what the footage can and cannot support.

What a good brief contains

  • Audience: one sentence.
  • Problem: in the audience's words.
  • Promise: one thing, supportable with proof.
  • Proof: demo, testimonial or fact, with a source.
  • Hook options: three to five opening lines or visuals.
  • Format and length: vertical or square, duration range, platform.
  • Call to action: one.
  • Do not: claims or phrases to avoid.
  • Test plan: what changes between variants.

Working with creators

For creator-style ads, the strategist often briefs the creator and the editor together. Ask the creator for alternative openings in the same session, and get raw footage so the editor can recut for different placements. Check platform and local advertising rules on disclosure. How to read results together

  1. Agree the main metric before launch.
  2. Change one variable per test.
  3. Look at early retention figures alongside conversions, since a strong hook with a weak offer needs a different fix from the reverse.
  4. Keep a log of variants, changes and results.
  5. Avoid reading too much into small differences or short tests.

Example: a subscription snack brand

Example: a strategist finds from reviews and customer interviews that new customers worry about waste. She writes a brief with the promise "choose only what you like," three hook options and a testimonial as proof. The editor builds three 15-second vertical ads with different openings, captions and a clear call to action, naming each file by hook. After a week, they review the results together and write the next brief around the strongest hook. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Common mistakes

  • A brief that lists five messages.
  • No test plan, so results cannot be read.
  • Editors not told the platform rules.
  • Claims with no proof.
  • Changing too many things per variant.

How to run a weekly creative review

A short, regular review keeps the strategist and the editor aligned. Once a week, look at the variants that ran, the one thing that differed between them and the result. Write one line per test: what we changed, what happened, what we will do next. Retire variants that clearly lost, and write the next brief around the winner. Keep a shared document of hooks, angles and results, so that new editors and creators can see what has already been tried.

Warning signs that the brief is the problem

If several editors produce weak first seconds from the same brief, the brief probably lacks a clear promise or a visual idea for the hook. If variants look identical, the brief gave no reason to differ. If results are confusing, the test changed too many things. Fix the brief before blaming the edit. A strategist's most useful habit is to cut the brief down until it states one audience, one problem, one promise and one proof.
