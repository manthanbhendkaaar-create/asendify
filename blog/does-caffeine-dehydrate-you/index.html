Does caffeine dehydrate you?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 30 October 2026 · Last updated 9 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

For most healthy adults, no. Moderate caffeine from coffee and tea does not dehydrate you. A controlled study of regular coffee drinkers found four mugs a day hydrated about as well as the same amount of water, and the NHS counts tea and coffee towards your daily fluids. Caffeine can briefly raise urine output, but the drink itself more than makes up for it.

Key takeaways

  • Moderate coffee and tea do not dehydrate healthy adults and count towards your fluid intake.
  • Caffeine is a mild diuretic, but at moderate amounts the water in the drink outweighs it.
  • Water is still the best everyday drink, especially in heat and during exercise.
  • Stay within 400 mg a day and 200 mg per dose, and protect your sleep with an early cut-off.
  • Skip caffeine when you are ill or dehydrated, and never mix it with alcohol.

This page is general education for healthy adults aged 18 and over. It is not medical advice. If you have a health condition or take regular medicine, ask your doctor or pharmacist. If you have signs of dehydration that do not improve, seek medical help.

Where the "coffee dehydrates you" myth comes from

Caffeine is a mild diuretic, which means it can make your kidneys produce a little more urine for a short while. That is where the idea comes from. The Mayo Clinic puts it plainly: caffeine increases urine production, but most research suggests the fluid in caffeinated drinks balances the diuretic effect of typical caffeine levels. It adds that high doses taken all at once may increase urine output more.

The mistake is to stop there. A mug of coffee is mostly water. Even if you pass a little more urine, you still end up with more fluid than you started with at moderate amounts. That is why the myth falls apart when researchers measure what actually happens in the body.

What the best study found

The clearest test is a 2014 study by Killer, Blannin and Jeukendrup in PLOS ONE. Fifty male regular coffee drinkers, who usually had three to six cups a day, completed two trial periods. In one they drank four 200 ml mugs of coffee a day, providing 4 mg of caffeine per kg of body weight. In the other they drank the same amount of water. Researchers checked body mass, total body water, blood markers and urine.

They found no meaningful difference in hydration between the two. The authors concluded that, in moderation, coffee provides similar hydrating qualities to water in caffeine-habituated men.

Two honest limits: the study was in men who already drank coffee, and it tested moderate amounts. It does not tell you that a litre of energy drink is as good as a litre of water.

The NHS guidance on water and drinks says to aim for 6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day, and that water, lower-fat milk and lower-sugar drinks, including tea and coffee, all count. It adds that some people need more, such as when pregnant, breastfeeding, in a hot environment, active for long periods or ill.

The real limits that matter more than hydration

Caffeine's main risks are not about fluid. They are about dose, timing and who is drinking it.

  • Daily total: the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) says up to 400 mg a day from all sources raises no safety concerns for healthy adults outside pregnancy. The US FDA uses the same 400 mg figure.
  • Single dose: EFSA says up to 200 mg in one go, about 3 mg per kg of body weight, raises no safety concerns.
  • Sleep: a 2023 meta-analysis of 24 studies by Gardiner and colleagues (open-access copy, Leeds Beckett University) estimated a normal coffee should be taken at least 8.8 hours before bed, and a typical pre-workout serve at least 13.2 hours before.

A simple hydration routine for caffeine drinkers

  1. Start the day with a glass of water, then your usual coffee or tea.
  2. Keep a glass of water next to every caffeinated drink. It also slows down how fast you drink.
  3. Drink water separately in heat or during exercise. Sweating, not moderate caffeine, is the bigger fluid loss.
  4. Skip caffeine when you are unwell, feverish or already dehydrated, for example with vomiting or diarrhoea. Your body needs rest and plain fluids.
  5. Do not use energy drinks as your main drink. They carry sugar and large doses, and they are easy to stack.
  6. Never mix caffeine with alcohol. The CDC explains that caffeine can make you feel less affected by alcohol, which can lead to drinking more.

Who should limit caffeine anyway

Hydration aside, some people should avoid caffeine or talk to a doctor first:

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding: the NHS advises no more than 200 mg a day in pregnancy.
  • Children and teenagers: this guidance is for adults only.
  • People with heart conditions, high blood pressure or an irregular heartbeat.
  • People with anxiety or panic disorder, as caffeine can make symptoms worse.
  • Anyone taking regular medicines, as some interact with caffeine. Ask your pharmacist.
  • Never use caffeine powders or highly concentrated liquid caffeine. The FDA warns that tiny amounts can be dangerous.

FAQ

Does coffee count towards my daily water intake?

Yes, at moderate amounts. The NHS counts tea and coffee as fluid, and a controlled study found four mugs a day hydrated regular drinkers about as well as water.

Is caffeine a diuretic?

Yes, a mild one. Caffeine increases urine production, but at typical amounts the water in the drink balances it out, according to the Mayo Clinic. Large doses taken in one go are where the diuretic effect becomes more noticeable.

Why do I need the toilet more after coffee?

Caffeine can briefly increase urine output, and the volume of the drink plays a part too. At moderate amounts, it does not mean you are losing more fluid than you drank.

Do energy drinks dehydrate you?

They contain water, so they add some fluid, but they often carry large caffeine doses and a lot of sugar. Count them in your 400 mg daily total and do not use them in place of water.

Should I avoid coffee before a workout in hot weather?

Not necessarily, if you are used to it and stay within the dose limits. Drink water before, during and after training, and skip caffeine if you already feel unwell or dehydrated.

Is tea more hydrating than coffee?

Both count towards your fluids. Tea usually has less caffeine per cup, about 75 mg against about 100 mg for instant coffee by NHS estimates.
