Does caffeine dehydrate you?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 30 October 2026 · Last updated 30 October 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

For most healthy adults, no: moderate caffeine from coffee and tea does not dehydrate you. A controlled study of regular coffee drinkers found that four cups a day hydrated about as well as the same amount of water, and the NHS counts tea and coffee towards your daily fluid intake. Very large doses can make you pass more urine for a short time, but at normal amounts the drink itself more than makes up for it.

This page is general education for healthy adults aged 18 and over. It is not medical advice. If you have a health condition or take regular medicine, ask your doctor or pharmacist.

Where the "coffee dehydrates you" myth comes from

Caffeine is a mild diuretic, which means it can make your kidneys produce a little more urine for a short while. A review by Maughan and Griffin (Journal of Human Nutrition and Dietetics, 2003) concluded that this effect shows up mainly with large single doses (roughly 250 to 300 mg or more, about two to three cups of coffee), and is smaller in people who drink caffeine regularly.

The mistake is to stop there. A mug of coffee is mostly water. Even if you pass a little more urine, you still end up with more fluid than you started with. That's why the myth falls apart when researchers measure what actually happens in the body.

What the best study found

The clearest test is a 2014 study by Killer, Blannin and Jeukendrup, published in PLoS One. Fifty male regular coffee drinkers completed two trial periods: one where they drank four 200 ml mugs of coffee a day (about 4 mg of caffeine per kg of body weight) and one where they drank the same amount of water. Researchers checked body mass, total body water, blood markers and urine.

They found no meaningful difference in hydration between the two. The authors concluded that moderate coffee intake in regular drinkers offers similar hydrating qualities to water. Two honest limits: the study was in men who already drank coffee, and it tested moderate amounts. It doesn't tell you that a litre of energy drink is as good as a litre of water.

The real limits that matter more than hydration

Caffeine's main risks are not about fluid. They are about dose, timing and who is drinking it.

  • Daily total: the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) found that up to 400 mg a day raises no safety concerns for healthy adults (outside pregnancy). The US FDA uses the same 400 mg figure.
  • Single dose: EFSA found that up to 200 mg in one go raises no safety concerns. The book never goes above that in a single dose, and suggests scaling down to about 3 mg per kg if you weigh less than 70 kg.
  • Sleep: a normal cup should be taken at least about 9 hours before bed, and a bigger dose of around 200 mg about 13 hours before, based on a 2023 review of sleep studies by Gardiner and colleagues (Sleep Medicine Reviews).

A simple hydration routine for caffeine drinkers

  1. Start the day with a glass of water, then your usual coffee or tea.
  2. Keep a glass of water next to every caffeinated drink. It also slows down how fast you drink.
  3. Drink water separately in heat or during exercise. Caffeine doesn't dehydrate you at moderate doses, but sweating does.
  4. Skip caffeine when you're unwell, feverish or already dehydrated, for example with vomiting or diarrhoea. Your body needs rest and plain fluids.
  5. Don't use energy drinks as your main drink. They carry sugar and large doses, and they are easy to stack.
  6. Never mix caffeine with alcohol. Caffeine can mask how drunk you feel, which leads people to drink more.

Who should limit caffeine anyway

Hydration aside, some people should avoid caffeine or talk to a doctor first:

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding: the NHS advises no more than 200 mg a day in pregnancy.
  • Children and teenagers: this guidance is for adults only.
  • People with heart conditions, high blood pressure or an irregular heartbeat.
  • People with anxiety or panic disorder, as caffeine can make symptoms worse.
  • Anyone taking regular medicines, as some interact with caffeine. Ask your pharmacist.
  • Never use caffeine powders or highly concentrated liquid caffeine. The FDA warns that tiny amounts can be dangerous.

Key takeaways

  • Moderate coffee and tea do not dehydrate healthy adults and count towards your fluid intake.
  • The diuretic effect is small and mainly seen with large single doses, especially in people not used to caffeine.
  • Water is still the best everyday drink, especially in heat and during exercise.
  • Stay within 400 mg a day and 200 mg per dose (EFSA), and protect your sleep with an early cut-off.
  • Skip caffeine when you're ill or dehydrated, and never mix it with alcohol.

FAQ

Does coffee count towards my daily water intake?

Yes, at moderate amounts. The NHS counts tea and coffee as fluid, and a controlled study found four cups a day hydrated regular drinkers about as well as water.

Why do I need the toilet more after coffee?

Caffeine can briefly increase urine output, particularly after a large dose or if you don't drink it often. The volume of the drink itself also plays a part. It doesn't mean you're losing more fluid than you drank.

Do energy drinks dehydrate you?

They contain water, so they add some fluid, but they often carry large caffeine doses and a lot of sugar. Count them in your 400 mg daily total and don't use them in place of water.

Should I avoid coffee before a workout in hot weather?

Not necessarily, if you're used to it and stay within your dose limits. Drink water before, during and after training, and skip caffeine if you already feel unwell or dehydrated.
