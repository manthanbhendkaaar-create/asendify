How do I make an online fundraising appeal video people watch all the way to the ask?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 3 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Open on one person and one specific moment, not your logo or a statistic. Online appeal videos keep viewers when there's a story to follow: a named person (with consent), a concrete need, what changes when someone gives, and a clear ask. Keep it to roughly 60 to 90 seconds for social and email, put the ask on screen before most viewers would drop off, and repeat it at the end.

Key takeaways

  • A single identifiable person usually holds attention better than numbers. In Small, Loewenstein and Slovic's research, summarised by Stanford GSB, a personal story of one girl drew more donations than statistics about famine, and adding statistics to the story reduced giving.
  • Make the need concrete: "a warm bed tonight" beats "tackling homelessness".
  • Show the change a gift makes, not just the problem; hopeless videos get skipped.
  • Ask early as well as late: a simple on-screen "Donate" moment in the middle catches people who won't reach the end.
  • In the UK, the Fundraising Regulator's key values are legal, open, honest and respectful.

A 75-second appeal structure

SecondsContentPurpose
0 to 5One person in one moment, with a line of text ("Amira was sleeping in her car in January")Hook through a story
5 to 25What they faced, in their words or a narrator'sMake the need concrete
25 to 35What your charity did: one specific actionShow you can help
35 to 40First ask: "£15 can pay for..." on screen (only if accurate)Catch early leavers
40 to 65Where they are now, honestly toldHope and payoff
65 to 75Clear ask, link, and thanksAct

Editing choices that hold attention

  1. Start mid-story. The first frame should already be part of the story, not a title card.
  2. Real voices. The person speaking (with consent) or a staff member who was there beats a polished voiceover.
  3. Captions for every word. Appeals are often watched muted in feeds and inboxes.
  4. Restrained music. Let it support, not manipulate; overly sad tracks feel like pressure.
  5. One ask, one link. Multiple calls to action (donate, volunteer, share, sign up) dilute each other.
  6. Honest figures. Only say what a gift buys if you can stand behind it; explain if gifts go to general funds.

Example: a food bank winter appeal

Example: a food bank's old appeal opens with its logo and "Last year we distributed 40,000 parcels." The new version opens with a volunteer packing a parcel while a mother, filmed with consent and filmed from behind at her request, says "I didn't know how I'd feed them that week." At 35 seconds, a card reads "£10 helps fill a parcel" (the food bank checked this figure against its costs), and it ends with the donate link. Details are illustrative.

Ethics first

Dignity matters as much as performance. Get informed consent, let people choose how they appear, avoid images that reduce people to their hardship, and don't exaggerate. See how to tell a beneficiary's story without exploiting them.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches storytelling for charity videos?

The Retention Editing Playbook (£1,720 one-time) teaches hooks and story structure for YouTube; it isn't fundraising-specific.

How long should a fundraising appeal video be?

Roughly 60 to 90 seconds for social and email; longer stories can live on YouTube or your website.

Should I use statistics?

Sparingly. Research suggests a single person's story moves people more than numbers, and adding statistics can reduce giving.

Where should the donate ask go?

In the middle as well as at the end, so viewers who leave early still see it.

Can we feature the people we help?

Only with informed consent and in a way that respects their dignity.
