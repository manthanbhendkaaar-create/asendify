Should captions be burned in on faceless voiceover videos?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

For short vertical clips on feeds, burned-in captions are usually worth it, because many people watch without sound and you control the look. For long YouTube videos, upload a proper caption track and burn in only the key words or numbers you want emphasised. In both cases, make the captions accurate and readable, and remember that a faceless video leans heavily on the voice, so a viewer who cannot hear it has nothing else to follow.

Key takeaways

  • The W3C's explanation of captions defines them as "synchronized visual and/or text alternative for both speech and non-speech audio information", conveying not only dialogue but "sound effects, music, laughter, speaker identification and location" (W3C WCAG 2.2). Burned-in text can show this, but cannot be turned off or restyled by the viewer.
  • TikTok's creative guidance says to "use captions or text overlays to provide context to your ad" and recommends "5-10 words per second when using text" (TikTok for Business).
  • Mayer's redundancy principle says "people learn better using on-screen graphics with voiceover", and that adding on-screen text to both can overwhelm (Dartmouth). Full captions on top of busy graphics can add load, so design them carefully.
  • YouTube Help says "video chapters add info and context to each portion of the video" (YouTube Help). Chapters help long videos in ways captions do not.

The W3C's intent behind captions is to give people who cannot hear a text equivalent. A separate track is the more accessible default, because it works with assistive tools and viewer settings. Burned-in captions add design control and guaranteed visibility on top of that.

When to burn in

  • Short vertical clips. Many feeds autoplay muted, and you want text visible with no taps.
  • Voice-only story channels. The captions carry rhythm and emphasis that the visuals alone do not.
  • Keyword emphasis. One or two highlighted words per line help scanning.
  • Platforms with weak caption support. Where caption tracks are limited or hidden, burned-in text is the reliable option.

When to rely on a track

  • Long YouTube videos. Upload a caption file and let viewers choose. Burn in only on-screen text such as numbers and names.
  • Multiple languages. Add tracks, rather than rendering each version.
  • Heavy infographic scenes. Burned-in text may clash with the graphics.
  • Accessibility priority. Offer a track in addition to any burned-in style.

How to make burned-in captions work

  1. Transcribe accurately. Fix names, numbers and homophones. Auto-generated text often needs correction.
  2. Break lines at natural phrases. Two lines maximum, short ones.
  3. Keep pace readable. TikTok's text guidance goes up to 10 words per second; for comfortable reading, stay well under that.
  4. Sync to speech. Text should appear as words are spoken, not noticeably early or late.
  5. Choose a clear font. Large, high contrast, with an outline or backing.
  6. Stay within safe margins. Keep captions clear of platform interface elements and edges.
  7. Place them consistently. One position through the video, moved only when a graphic blocks them.
  8. Highlight sparingly. Colour or bold on key words, not on every line.
  9. Include meaningful non-speech sounds if they matter. The W3C notes captions cover sound effects and speaker identification; for voice-only videos this may be rare, but indicate when the sound carries information.
  10. Check with sound off. The video should still be understandable.

Example: a faceless finance explainer

Example: a faceless channel produces a 45-second vertical clip and a 9-minute landscape video of the same topic. For the clip, the editor burns in short two-line captions in the lower middle with the key number in a different colour. For the long video, the editor uploads a caption track and adds on-screen text only for key figures and names. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result. The channel compares the clip's retention on the platform's own metric and the long video's graph, noting where viewers leave.

Mistakes

  • Word-by-word captions that bounce and flash for minutes. They tire the eyes.
  • Burning in full captions over dense infographics.
  • Captions that differ from the speech.
  • Tiny text.
  • Forgetting to supply a track for the long version.
  • Captions hidden behind interface elements.

How to test

For clips, publish two versions that differ only in captions: no captions versus burned-in. Compare early retention and completion on the platform. For longer videos, compare the retention graph before and after adding a track, though that effect is mostly accessibility and may not show up in retention. Interpret carefully: small differences may be noise.

Checklist

  • Accurate transcript.
  • Short lines, readable pace.
  • Safe margins, high contrast.
  • Burned-in for short feeds; track for long video.
  • Key numbers and names on screen.
  • Works with sound off.

A worked review routine

Once a week, set aside half an hour to review one published video with the graph open. Play the video with the caption track hidden, then with sound off, and write down three notes: where you would have left, what the picture was doing at that moment, and what the voice was doing. Most fixes show up in these notes. If the voice and picture both stay the same for a long stretch, add a change. If a point is repeated, cut one version. If text is hard to read on a phone, enlarge it. Keep each note short and dated, so that after a month you can see which kinds of change helped and which did not, and then decide the one change to try on the next upload.
