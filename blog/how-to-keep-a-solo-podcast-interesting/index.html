How do you keep a solo podcast interesting?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Keep a solo podcast interesting by giving each episode one clear question, opening on a story or a bold claim instead of a welcome, and planning the episode as three or four segments that each end with a reason to keep listening. Use specific examples and real stories rather than general advice, then cut hard in the edit: repeats, tangents and long pauses hurt a solo show more than an interview, because there's no second voice to break them up.

Key takeaways

  • One episode, one question: "Why do most small agencies stall at five clients?" beats "Agency tips, part 12".
  • Open on the strongest moment; Spotify says listeners "have a tendency to drop off early".
  • End each segment on an open question that the next segment answers.
  • Specific stories and examples hold attention better than lists of advice.
  • In the edit, remove false starts, repeats and long fillers; a single voice exposes every slow patch.

Why solo shows are harder

In an interview, the second voice does a lot of work: it changes the sound, asks the questions listeners are thinking and reacts. On your own, all of that has to be built in. Without a plan, solo episodes drift: the host thinks aloud, repeats points and circles the topic.

Plan the episode as segments

SegmentJobExample (episode: "Why most small agencies stall at five clients")
Cold open (30-60 s)A story or claim that raises the question"I hit five clients and earned less than when I had three."
Segment 1Set up the problem with a specific exampleThe month the fifth client signed
Segment 2The cause, explained simplyPricing by the hour, one person doing all delivery
Segment 3What to do, with an examplePackaging the service; the first hire
CloseOne-line takeaway, one call to action, teaser"Next week: the first hire I got wrong."

End segments 1 and 2 with a line that opens the next ("which is when I realised the problem wasn't the clients"). That's an open loop.

Why open loops matter more when you're alone

Carnegie Mellon's George Loewenstein argued that curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge" and that such gaps "produce the feeling of deprivation labeled curiosity" (quoted in PBS NOVA). An interviewer creates gaps naturally by asking questions. Solo, you have to create them deliberately: ask the question out loud, then make the listener wait a little for the answer.

Get to the point fast

Spotify's guide to episode performance warns that "listeners have a tendency to drop off early, so it's crucial to capture people's attention as quickly as possible", and that "getting into the heart of your episode as quickly as you can is a great way to keep up retention" (Spotify for Creators). For a solo show, that means no long greeting, no "before we start" housekeeping, and no "so today I wanted to talk about...". Start on the story.

Sound like a person, not a script

  • Outline, don't script word for word, unless you're a trained reader. Bullet points keep you natural.
  • Talk to one listener. Picture a specific person and explain it to them.
  • Use stories and numbers you can stand behind. "A client called me at 11pm" is more gripping than "communication is important".
  • Vary your pace. Slow down for the key point; speed up through context.

Edit hard

Fillers are a natural part of speech. Psychologists Herbert Clark and Jean Fox Tree found speakers use "uh" and "um" to signal a minor or major delay in speaking (Clark and Fox Tree, 2002, PDF), which helps in conversation but slows a solo recording. Remove strings of fillers, false starts and repeated points, but keep pauses that give a key line weight. On video, cover cuts with punch-ins, text or B-roll.

Example: re-planning a rambling episode

Example (a hypothetical solo episode, to show the method): a 40-minute episode called "Thoughts on pricing" starts with three minutes of greeting and news, wanders through six loosely linked ideas and ends with a long recap.

Re-planned:

  • New title: "Why charging by the hour keeps freelancers broke."
  • Cold open: the story of a project where working faster earned less (45 s).
  • Three segments: the problem (hourly pricing punishes speed), the cause (selling time, not outcomes), the fix (packages), each ending on a question.
  • Cut: the news section, two unrelated ideas (saved for future episodes) and the recap.
  • Close: one takeaway, one call to action, a teaser for next week.

The new episode runs 24 minutes and has a clear reason to listen at every point.

Add variety without a co-host

  • Listener questions: read one out and answer it.
  • Short clips: a 20-second voice note from a client or listener (with permission).
  • Sound and music cues to mark new segments.
  • On video: change framing between segments; add text for key points.

FAQ

Is there a book on making solo podcasts more engaging?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, open loops and pacing and applies them to podcasts.

How long should a solo podcast be?

As long as you can stay focused on one question. Many solo shows work well at 10 to 30 minutes.

Should I script my solo podcast?

Outline the key points and stories; full scripts often sound read unless you're experienced.

How do I stop rambling?

Pick one question per episode and plan three or four segments, each with a clear job.

How can I add variety on my own?

Use listener questions, short clips, music cues and, on video, changes of framing and on-screen text.

Should I edit out mistakes?

Yes: false starts, repeats and long fillers. Keep pauses that give key lines weight.
