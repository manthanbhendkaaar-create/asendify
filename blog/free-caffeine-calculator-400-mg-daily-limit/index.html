Is there a free caffeine calculator that tells me if I'm over the 400 mg daily limit?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 10 November 2026 · Last updated 10 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes. Asendify's free Caffeine Cutoff Calculator adds up the drinks you list and checks the total against the 400 mg daily guidance for healthy adults; it suits people who also want a last-coffee time for sleep. IFIC's Caffeine Calculator and Omni Calculator's caffeine tool also total your intake for free.

Key takeaways

  • Several free calculators add up your caffeine and compare it with a daily limit. None of them knows your health, so treat the result as a guide.
  • For healthy adults, EFSA's guidance is up to 400 mg a day and up to 200 mg in one go. The daily figure drops to 200 mg if you are pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • The biggest source of error is the caffeine figure per drink. Coffee varies widely, so use labels or café figures where you can.
  • Count hidden sources: decaf, chocolate, supplements, pre-workout and some medicines.
  • Never use caffeine powders, and never mix caffeine with alcohol.

This is general information for healthy adults aged 18 and over, not medical advice. If you have symptoms, see a doctor.

What the 400 mg limit actually means

The figure comes from the European Food Safety Authority. Its summary of its caffeine opinion says:

  • Up to 400 mg a day, spread through the day, raises no safety concerns for healthy adults, except pregnant women.
  • Single doses up to 200 mg (about 3 mg per kg of body weight) raise no safety concerns for healthy adults.
  • Up to 200 mg a day from all sources raises no concerns for the unborn child during pregnancy and breastfeeding.
  • A single 100 mg dose may disrupt sleep in some adults, especially close to bedtime.

The US Food and Drug Administration uses the same 400 mg figure. It describes it as about two to three 12-ounce cups of coffee and an amount not generally linked with negative effects for most adults. It also stresses that people vary widely in how sensitive they are to caffeine and how fast they clear it.

So a useful calculator checks two numbers, not one: your daily total and your biggest single dose.

How to get an accurate answer from any calculator

  1. List everything for a normal day. Coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks, pre-workout, chocolate. The FDA notes that caffeine also turns up in some energy or protein bars, chewing gum, supplements and over-the-counter medicines, and that decaf typically still has 2 to 15 mg per 8-ounce cup.
  2. Use real figures where you can. Typical values are averages. The FDA gives a range of 113 to 247 mg for a 12-ounce brewed coffee and 41 to 246 mg for 12 ounces of energy drink. That spread can decide whether you are under or over. Energy drink cans carry caffeine figures on the label, and many coffee chains publish them too.
  3. Match the size. A large takeaway coffee is not "a cup". Our guide to how much caffeine is in a cup of coffee breaks this down by drink.
  4. Check your biggest single serving. If one drink is over 200 mg, split it or choose a smaller size, even if your daily total is fine. Pre-workout is a common culprit; see how much caffeine is in pre-workout.
  5. Look at timing too. EFSA notes that 100 mg close to bedtime can disrupt sleep in some adults. A calculator that gives a cut-off time helps here.

What a calculator can't tell you

A calculator does arithmetic. It does not know your body. The FDA lists signs of too much caffeine, including a fast heart rate or palpitations, high blood pressure, sleep problems, anxiety or jitteriness, an upset stomach, nausea and headache. If you get these below 400 mg, your personal limit is lower.

Some people should avoid caffeine or check with a doctor or pharmacist first:

  • Under-18s. EFSA proposes a lower, weight-based level for children and teenagers (3 mg per kg of body weight a day).
  • People who are pregnant, trying to conceive or breastfeeding (200 mg a day at most).
  • People with heart conditions, high blood pressure, anxiety or sleep problems.
  • Anyone taking medication that may interact with caffeine.

Two rules no calculator overrides

Never use caffeine powders or pure caffeine products. The FDA warns that about 1,200 mg, less than half a teaspoon of pure caffeine, taken quickly can cause toxic effects such as seizures, and that these products have caused serious harm, including death. Our article on why caffeine powders are dangerous explains the risk of measuring them at home.

Never mix caffeine with alcohol. Keep them separate, whatever your daily total says.

FAQ

Is 400 mg of caffeine a day safe for everyone?

No. It is EFSA's guidance for healthy adults. Pregnant or breastfeeding women should stay at or under 200 mg a day, under-18s need much less, and some people feel unwell at lower amounts.

How much caffeine is too much in one go?

EFSA says single doses up to 200 mg raise no safety concerns for healthy adults. Above that, split the dose or choose a smaller drink.

Do caffeine calculators count tea and chocolate?

Most do. IFIC's tool includes teas and dark chocolate, and Asendify's lets you add your own drinks. Check that every source you use is on the list.

Why do calculators give different totals?

They use different average figures per drink. Coffee in particular varies a lot, so real label or café figures give the most accurate answer.

Is decaf caffeine-free?

No. The FDA says decaf typically contains 2 to 15 mg per 8-ounce cup. It adds up if you drink a lot of it.
