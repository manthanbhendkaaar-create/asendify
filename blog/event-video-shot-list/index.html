What shots should I get at an event so the recap video holds attention?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Film for the edit, not for the archive. A recap holds attention when you have a strong opening shot, plenty of audience reactions, the key speaker lines, short attendee interviews with clean audio, close-up details and a few wide shots to show scale. Write a shot list before the day, assign someone to flag big moments, and capture more reactions than you think you need, because reactions are what make every other shot land.

Key takeaways

  • Vimeo's recap guide lists keynote speakers, audience reactions, breakout sessions and Q&A sessions as core shots.
  • Canon's advice is "Plan your shoot, shoot your plan": bring a list of must-capture moments and useful B-roll.
  • Cvent recommends planning crowd shots, speaker entrances, reactions, signage and interviews, and assigning someone onsite to flag standout moments.
  • Audio is part of the shot list: Canon suggests a shotgun mic, a lavalier for interviews and a portable recorder as backup.
  • The opening shot of your edit is usually captured by accident unless someone is watching for it.

Why a shot list is a retention tool

Editors can only cut what was filmed. Most dull recaps are not badly edited; they are under-shot. The editor has two hours of wide shots of a stage and no reactions, no close-ups and no clean interview audio, so the edit drags.

The Retention Editing Playbook starts every video with a cold open, the most interesting moment in the video at 0:00. You cannot cold open on a moment nobody filmed. So the shot list is where retention starts.

Vimeo's recap guide names keynote speakers, audience reactions, breakout sessions and Q&A as the core, and Cvent's event video guide adds speaker entrances, signage and interviews.

Reactions: shoot three times more than you think

A speaker saying something clever is fine. A speaker saying something clever followed by a room laughing is a moment. Ask one camera operator to spend whole sessions facing the audience. Get the front rows, but also people at the back and at the side, so the room feels full in the edit.

Interviews that actually cut well

  • Ask open questions that invite a short story: "What surprised you today?" works better than "Are you enjoying it?"
  • Ask them to include your question in the answer, so the clip works on its own without your voice.
  • Use a lavalier mic. Cvent recommends lavalier mics for testimonials and says "Bad audio will lose your audience." Canon suggests a lavalier for interviews, a shotgun mic for general sound and a portable recorder as backup.
  • Film somewhere quiet with life behind them. A blurred, busy background shows the event without drowning the voice.
  • Get permission. Tell people how the clip will be used. The Irish Data Protection Commission's guidance on event photos treats images of people as personal data and suggests written notices at large events.

Assign a moment-spotter

Cvent advises assigning someone onsite to flag standout moments and coordinate with the crew. This person knows the run of show, knows when the surprise guest walks on, and makes sure a camera is pointed at the audience at that moment, not at the stage only.

Example: shot list for a one-day trade conference

Example: a one-day conference with two stages, 400 attendees and two camera operators.

  • Camera A (stage): each keynote in full from the back, plus close-ups of the headline speaker at three planned moments the speaker has flagged in advance.
  • Camera B (people): audience reactions during each keynote, then roaming: arrivals 8:30-9:00, coffee and networking, workshops, 10 interviews between 12:00 and 2:00, sponsor stands at 3:00.
  • Phone (moment-spotter): vertical clips of anything unexpected for same-day social posts.
  • Audio: sound desk feed recorded for keynotes; lavalier for interviews; one minute of room tone in each space.

At the end of the day the editor has: 3 cold-open candidates, roughly 60 reaction shots, 10 usable interviews and enough detail shots to cut a two-minute recap with no shot held longer than it needs to be.

Shooting for different cuts

If you need a vertical cut for Reels, TikTok or Shorts, keep the subject in the centre of the frame or shoot some moments vertically on a second device. TikTok's creative best practices recommend vertical 9:16 at 720p or higher and keeping key content inside the safe zone. Planning this on the day saves awkward crops later.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches how to find and build a cold open from your footage?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches cold opens, intro killers, open loops and pacing. It is written for YouTube; Vimeo's and Canon's free recap guides are fair alternatives.

How many cameras do I need for an event?

One can work for a small event if it alternates between stage and audience. Two is better: one on the stage, one on the people.

How much footage should I shoot for a 2-minute recap?

There is no fixed ratio. What matters is variety: enough reactions, details and interviews that no shot has to be held too long.

Should I film vertically or horizontally?

Film landscape for the main recap and plan vertical moments for social, either by centring subjects or using a second device.

What is room tone?

A short recording of a space with nobody talking. Editors use it to smooth audio cuts so they don't sound abrupt.

Do I need permission to film attendees?

Images of people are personal data under GDPR. Use clear notices and, at smaller events, consent options such as coloured lanyards.
