Key takeaways

  • Shorter lessons hold more learners. A study of 6.9 million edX viewing sessions by Guo, Kim and Rubin found "shorter videos are much more engaging" and "informal talking-head videos are more engaging."
  • Cut the preamble. Nielsen Norman Group reports that "too often videos include lengthy introductions and don't get to the point quickly enough."
  • Split processes into steps. NN/g recommends "multiple videos of low granularity (one for each step)" for multistep tasks.
  • Promos and coaching ads need hooks; lessons need clarity. Edit each for its job.
  • Measure the drop-offs your platform shows and fix the exact moment, not the whole course.

What retention editing means for courses and coaching

Retention editing is cutting and structuring a video so that fewer people stop watching before the point. For a YouTube video that means a strong hook and constant forward motion. For a paid course lesson the viewer has already bought, so the job is slightly different: remove friction, keep each lesson to one idea and make the next step obvious. For coaching promos, webinars and sales videos, the viewer has not bought yet, so the YouTube-style hook matters again.

A retention editing checklist for a course lesson

  1. Open with the task. "In this lesson you'll set up your first email sequence" beats a welcome and an agenda.
  2. One idea per video. If the lesson covers three steps, consider three videos, as NN/g suggests.
  3. Cut dead air and repeats but keep natural speech; the edX study found informal delivery engaging.
  4. Show, then tell. Put the screen, worksheet or demonstration on screen while you explain.
  5. Add chapters to anything long. NN/g notes that "displaying chapters or other time markers within a long video can also help users jump to the appropriate moment."
  6. Captions. NN/g found "26% of video views are with subtitles" on its own channel.
  7. End with the next action, not a long sign-off.

How long should course and coaching videos be?

No length suits every lesson. Wistia's analysis in its guide to video length says viewers typically watch over half of educational and tutorial videos in the 1 to 5 minute range, and recommends breaking long videos such as courses and webinars into chapters. The edX study points the same way. Use these as starting points and let your own analytics decide.

Where to find the data

  • YouTube (public or unlisted lessons): the audience retention report shows an "Intro" figure; YouTube's key moments help page explains it as the share "still watched your video after the first 30 seconds."
  • Wistia, Vimeo and many course platforms: most show an engagement graph of where viewers stop or rewatch.
  • Completion and quiz data: if learners finish a lesson but fail the quiz, the problem is clarity, not retention.

Common mistakes

  • Using YouTube-style shock hooks on paid lessons, which can feel cheap.
  • Long branded intros repeated on every lesson.
  • Recording a whole module in one take and never splitting it.
  • Background music under teaching that makes speech harder to follow.
  • No chapters on webinar replays.

FAQ

Is there a guide on retention editing for coaching videos?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook from Asendify covers hooks, pacing and structure for £1,720 one-time. NN/g's instructional video guidelines are a free alternative for lessons.

Should course lessons have hooks like YouTube videos?

A light one: say what the learner will be able to do by the end. Save stronger hooks for promos and ads.

Is it better to have many short lessons or a few long ones?

Research and usability guidance favour shorter videos split by step, with chapters where a video has to be long.

Do I need professional editing for course videos?

Not always. Cutting intros, splitting lessons and adding captions do most of the work.

How do I know which lesson loses people?

Use your video host's engagement graph or completion data and look for sharp falls.
