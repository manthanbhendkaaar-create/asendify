What should the first frame of a Facebook or Instagram video ad show so people keep watching?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The first frame should already be the middle of something: a person, a hand or the product in motion, plus one short line of large text that names the viewer's problem or the payoff they'll get. It should make sense with the sound off and be filmed for a vertical phone screen. Avoid logo cards, title slides, slow fades from black and anything that looks like a reposted video from another app.

Key takeaways

  • The first frame is what people see as they scroll, before they decide to stop. It has to work as a still image and as the start of a video.
  • Meta's Reels guidance says strong Reels have a strong hook or payoff, like a big reveal, punchline or plot twist, that vertical 9:16 helps them feel immersive, and that visible watermarks from other apps and blurry low-resolution video are things to avoid.
  • Google's ABCDs, written for YouTube ads but widely applicable, advise introducing the product "from the start" and using tight framing for Shorts-style vertical video.
  • Text must be readable over the image: WCAG sets a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text. A solid or semi-solid panel behind text fixes most problems.
  • Test first frames like hooks: same ad, different opening frame, and compare how many viewers get past the first seconds.

Checklist for the first frame

  1. Motion already happening. Start the clip a beat into the action, not at the start of the move.
  2. One clear subject. A face, a hand or a product, filling the frame. Tight framing reads on a small screen.
  3. One line of text, under 8 words. The viewer's problem ("Back pain after desk work?") or the payoff ("Dinner in 12 minutes").
  4. Readable with sound off. If the line only makes sense with audio, it's not doing its job.
  5. Inside safe areas. Keep text and faces away from the edges where Facebook and Instagram place buttons and captions.
  6. Native look. No watermarks or fonts from other apps; record or export cleanly for each placement.
  7. The brand nearby. Product or brand visible in the first few seconds, as the ABCDs suggest, without a logo card.

Example: a meal-kit ad

Example: a meal-kit company's 20-second Reels ad opened on a 1.5-second logo animation over a white background. The new version opens mid-action: a pan sizzling, a hand dropping in chopped peppers, with the line "Dinner in 12 minutes, no shopping" in white on a dark panel at the top third. The box and brand appear at second 3 as the person reaches for the next ingredient. The team runs both versions as an A/B test with the same budget and audience, and compares how many viewers watched past 3 seconds and the cost per order. The new first frame wins on both, so the logo card is dropped from future ads. Figures and details are illustrative.

Common mistakes

  • Reusing a YouTube thumbnail-style frame with tiny text.
  • A frame that only makes sense after the voiceover starts.
  • Starting with a wide shot where the subject is small.
  • Claims in the first frame you can't support.

FAQ

Is there a book on openings that stop viewers scrolling?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks with templates. Meta's free Reels best practices are an alternative.

Should my logo be in the first frame?

Not as a logo card. Show the product or brand naturally in the first few seconds instead.

How much text should the first frame have?

One short line, under about 8 words, large and on a contrasting panel.

Does the first frame need to work without sound?

Yes. Many people scroll with sound off, so the image and text must make sense alone.

How do I test first frames?

Run the same ad with two openings in an A/B test and compare 3-second views and cost per result.
