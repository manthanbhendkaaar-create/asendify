How do I make a nonprofit impact report video that supporters actually watch?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 3 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Choose three moments from the year, not thirty numbers. An impact video holds supporters when each key figure is attached to a person, a place or a before-and-after they can picture, when it opens with the single most striking change, and when it thanks them by showing what their support made happen. Keep it to roughly 90 seconds to two minutes, and put the full report one click away for anyone who wants the detail.

Key takeaways

  • An impact video is a thank-you with evidence, not the annual report read aloud.
  • Open on the year's most vivid change, then promise what's coming ("three things you made possible this year").
  • Every number needs an image: "1,200 meals" lands harder over a shot of one kitchen at lunchtime.
  • Numbers must be accurate and match the written report; impact reporting is judged on honesty.
  • End with "thank you" and one next step, not a hard ask; supporters respond to being treated as partners.

Structure: "three things you made possible"

SecondsContent
0 to 8The most striking moment of the year, with one line of text
8 to 15The promise: "Here are three things your support made possible in 2026"
15 to 45Story 1: a person or place, then its number
45 to 75Story 2
75 to 100Story 3
100 to 115Thank you, from the people involved rather than only the CEO
115 to 120Link to the full report, and one gentle next step

Numbering the stories ("1 of 3") is a simple open loop: viewers stay to see all three.

Turning statistics into scenes

StatisticScene that carries it
"We supported 340 families"One family's kitchen table, then "and 339 more"
"92 trees planted"Volunteers planting, then a drone shot of the row
"12,000 helpline calls"A volunteer answering one call (no caller details), then the total
"Three new schools"The first day in one classroom

Editing tips

  1. Short lines of text, on screen long enough to read. Many supporters watch on phones, muted.
  2. People over graphics. Animated infographics are fine for one number; ten in a row lose attention.
  3. Vary pace. Quick montage for the opening, slower for the personal stories.
  4. Real thank-yous. Ten seconds of beneficiaries, volunteers or staff (with consent) saying thanks is more moving than a script.
  5. Accessible versions. Captions, and a transcript or description on the page.
  6. Cut-downs. A 30-second version for social and a 6-second teaser for email headers.

Example: a community garden charity

Example: a charity's draft impact video is a four-minute slide show of figures. The new version opens on a child pulling her first carrot out of the ground, then "Three things you made possible this year." The three stories: a new garden on an estate, a cooking class for older residents, and a school partnership, each with its real figure. It ends with volunteers saying "thank you" and a link to the full report. Details are illustrative.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Rounding figures up or counting the same people twice.
  • Using beneficiaries' faces without consent.
  • Opening with a long speech from leadership.
  • Ending with an aggressive donation ask in a thank-you video.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches video structure for nonprofits?

The Retention Editing Playbook (£1,720 one-time) teaches hooks and structure for YouTube; it isn't nonprofit-specific but the techniques apply.

How long should an impact report video be?

Roughly 90 seconds to two minutes, with a shorter cut for social.

Should the CEO appear?

Briefly, if at all. Beneficiaries, volunteers and staff on the ground usually hold attention better.

Should an impact video ask for donations?

A gentle next step is fine, but lead with thanks; it's primarily a report back to supporters.

How many statistics should I include?

A few, each tied to a scene. Put the rest in the written report.
