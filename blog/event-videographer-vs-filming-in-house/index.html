Should I hire an event videographer or film the event in-house on phones?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Hire a videographer when the video has to look polished and the audio must be reliable, such as a recap for sponsors, a keynote recording or a paid ad. Film in-house on phones when speed and volume matter more than polish, such as same-day social clips. Many events do both: a professional crew for the main recap and session recordings, and a social team on phones for vertical clips. Either way, the edit decides whether people keep watching.

Key takeaways

  • Audio is the most common weak point of DIY event video; Cvent warns "Bad audio will lose your audience."
  • Canon's recap guide suggests a shotgun mic, a lavalier for interviews and a portable recorder as backup, a kit many in-house teams don't have.
  • Phones are ideal for fast vertical clips; TikTok recommends 9:16 at 720p or higher.
  • If you hire a freelancer, the contract decides who owns the footage: GOV.UK says the creator is usually the first owner unless agreed in writing.
  • The opening and pacing of the edit matter more to retention than the camera.

What professionals do better

The biggest difference is usually sound. Cvent's event video guide recommends lavalier mics for testimonials and recording room tone, and warns "Bad audio will lose your audience." Canon's five steps for recap videos suggests a shotgun mic for overall sound, a lavalier for interviews and a portable recorder as backup. A crew also brings multiple angles and lighting, which matter for keynote recordings and anything that will run as an ad.

What in-house phones do better

Speed and volume. A social team member with a phone can post a vertical clip of the opening keynote's biggest moment before lunch. TikTok's creative best practices recommend vertical 9:16 at 720p or higher, which any modern phone handles. In-house filming also feels more personal, which suits stories and behind-the-scenes content.

Ownership and rights

If you hire a freelance videographer, GOV.UK's ownership of copyright guidance says the first owner of copyright is usually the creator unless you agree otherwise in writing, while work made by employees in the course of employment generally belongs to the employer. Agree raw-file delivery and usage rights before the event, so you can re-edit later.

How to decide

  1. What will the video be used for? Sponsor reports, ads and keynote recordings need polish and clean audio. Social clips don't.
  2. How fast must it go out? Same day favours in-house.
  3. How many outputs do you need? Lots of short clips favours in-house volume; one hero film favours a crew.
  4. Who will edit? A great shoot with a weak edit still loses viewers. Budget for editing time or skills either way.

Example: a 300-person conference on a tight budget

Example: a professional association with a modest budget runs a one-day conference.

  • They book a freelance videographer for half a day to record the two keynotes with a desk audio feed, plus 10 interviews on lavalier mics. The contract includes raw files and full usage rights.
  • A staff member films vertical clips on a phone with a clip-on mic: arrivals, workshop moments, reactions.
  • That evening, the staff member posts three vertical clips, each opening on the best moment.
  • The following week, the in-house marketer edits a 90-second recap from the videographer's footage, opening on the keynote's biggest laugh.

Result: polished keynote footage and clean interviews where they matter, fast social clips where speed matters, and full rights to re-cut both.

The edit decides retention

Whoever films, the first seconds decide whether viewers stay. The Retention Editing Playbook's cold open rule, start on the most interesting moment, applies to a phone clip and a crew-shot recap alike.

It is written for YouTube, so it suits in-house marketers and editors who cut their own event footage and will adapt the examples. Free alternatives are Cvent's and Canon's event video guides.

Quick checklist when hiring a videographer

  • Write the outputs you need: recap, session recordings, interviews, social clips.
  • Ask how they'll capture audio for talks and interviews.
  • Agree raw-file delivery, format and deadline in writing.
  • Agree copyright ownership or a broad licence covering future and paid use.
  • Share your shot list and run of show in advance.
  • Name an onsite contact who flags big moments.
  • Decide who edits, and when, before the event day.
  • Plan who films vertical clips for same-day social, so the crew can focus on the main footage.

Common mistakes

  • Booking a crew without agreeing who owns the footage.
  • Relying on a phone's built-in mic for interviews in a noisy hall.
  • Expecting the crew to deliver dozens of social clips they weren't briefed on.
  • Leaving editing time out of the plan.
  • Filming everything landscape when most posts will be vertical.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches cold opens and pacing for editing your own footage?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches cold opens, intro killers and pacing. Cvent's free event video guide is a fair alternative.

Can I film a whole event on an iPhone?

Yes, for social content. For keynotes and interviews, add proper microphones and a tripod at minimum.

What's the one piece of kit to buy for DIY event video?

A good wireless lavalier or clip-on mic. Audio is where DIY video usually fails.

Do I own footage my staff film?

Work made by employees in the course of their job generally belongs to the employer.

Should the videographer also edit?

It can save time, but make sure you get raw files too so you can re-cut later.

How early should I book a videographer?

As early as you can, especially for busy event seasons.
