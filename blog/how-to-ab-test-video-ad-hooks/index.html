How do I A/B test video ad hooks properly?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

To A/B test video ad hooks properly, make several versions of the same ad that differ only in the first few seconds, run them to the same audience with the same budget and placements, and compare them first on hook rate (did people stop?) and then on the result you care about (clicks, leads or sales). Change one thing at a time, give each version enough impressions before deciding, and keep the winning hook as the new control for the next round.

Key takeaways

  • Isolate the hook: same body, same call to action, same audience, same budget.
  • Measure early attention with platform metrics: Meta 3-second plays, TikTok 2-second and 6-second views, Google "video played to" 25%.
  • Then judge on results. A hook that stops more people but attracts the wrong ones isn't a winner.
  • TikTok advises putting the hook in the first 6 seconds and the proposition in the first 3.
  • Keep honest hooks only. The UK CAP Code requires evidence for objective claims.

Step 1: write hook variations

Use different types of opening, not small wording tweaks, so the test tells you something:

Hook typeExample first frame and text
Result firstFinished kitchen, text: "Fitted in 3 days"
Specific number"£212 saved on my energy bill" (only if true and typical results are clear)
Contradiction"You don't need a bigger ad budget"
Question"Why do your ads get views but no sales?"
Reveal in progressMid-action shot that only makes sense if you keep watching

The guide to the first 3 seconds of a video ad explains each type.

Step 2: keep everything else the same

Hold constantWhy
Ad body after the hookOtherwise you're testing two things
Audience and placementsDifferent audiences behave differently
Budget and scheduleUneven spend skews results
Call to action and landing pageSo results reflect the hook
LengthShorter ads reach completion metrics more easily

Many ad platforms have built-in experiment or A/B test tools that split audiences so people don't see more than one version; use them where available.

Step 3: measure attention, then results

PlatformEarly-attention metricSource
Meta3-second video plays ÷ impressions (hook rate, not an official metric)Madgicx glossary
TikTok2-second and 6-second video viewsTikTok video play metrics
YouTubeVideo played to 25%; TrueView view rateGoogle Ads view metrics

TikTok's creative best practices say to "prioritize your hook in the first 6 seconds" and "introduce your content proposition in the first 3 seconds", so 2-second and 6-second views line up well with what a hook test is measuring.

Then look at the business result: click-through rate, cost per lead, cost per purchase. Rank on the result, using hook rate to explain why.

Step 4: wait for enough data

Small numbers swing wildly. A hook with 30% hook rate on 400 impressions may be 22% on 10,000. Decide in advance how many impressions or results each version needs before you call it, and don't stop early because one version leads on day one. If the versions are within a point or two of each other, treat it as a tie and test bigger differences.

Step 5: keep the winner, test again

The winning hook becomes the control. Next round, test a new challenger against it, or test the next element (the first line of voiceover, the on-screen text style, the person on screen). Over several rounds, small gains compound.

Worked example

C stops the most people but brings the wrong ones. B and D are close on cost per lead; with similar numbers, the team keeps both, runs a second round with more budget, and drops A and C. For how to read the ratios, see hook rate and hold rate in video ads.

Rules for honest hooks

The UK CAP Code says ads "must not materially mislead", that marketers must hold evidence for objective claims, and that ads must not exaggerate a product's performance. A "£212 saved" hook needs to be true and representative. Misleading hooks may win a test and then cost you in complaints, refunds and trust.

Keeping a hook test log

A simple log turns individual tests into lasting knowledge. Record each test in a spreadsheet with these columns:

ColumnExample
Date and platform3 March, Meta Reels
Ad body (control)"Kitchen demo v2"

Impressions and spend25,000, £150

Why we think it wonShows the outcome buyers want

Look for patterns. After ten or twenty tests, you'll see which hook types tend to win for your audience. That's more useful than any general list of "best hooks".

Re-test old winners. Audiences change and hooks tire. A hook that won six months ago may not win now; re-test it occasionally against new challengers.

Share the log. If you work with an editor or agency, share it so their next edits start from what you've learned. See should I hire a video ad editor or learn myself.

Be patient with results. A hook that wins on hook rate in two days may not win on sales over two weeks. Give the business result time to show before you declare a final winner.

Is there a book that teaches how to write and test video hooks?

The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks and pacing, with templates and a tracker, at £1,000 one-time. Google's ABCDs and TikTok's guide are free alternatives.

How many hooks should I test at once?

Three to five distinct types is manageable for most budgets.

Should I judge hooks on hook rate or sales?

Rank on the business result; use hook rate to understand why.

How long should a hook test run?

Until each version has enough impressions and results to be meaningful, decided before you start.
