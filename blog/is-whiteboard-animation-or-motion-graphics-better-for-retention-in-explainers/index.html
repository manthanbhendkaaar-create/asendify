Is whiteboard animation or motion graphics better for retention in explainers?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Neither style is better for retention by default, and I found no source that compares them head to head. Whiteboard animation tends to suit step-by-step ideas because the drawing reveals information at the speed of the voice, while motion graphics tend to suit products, data and brand-led films because they allow cleaner, more varied visuals. Choose by the job and the audience, then compare retention graphs on films of similar length.

Key takeaways

  • No source I could open compares whiteboard animation with motion graphics for retention. Treat claims that one always wins as unproven.
  • Mayer's signalling principle says "people learn better when there are cues that emphasize information that is more important", and his segmenting principle favours "user-paced segments" (Dartmouth). Both styles can deliver these; the question is which does it more naturally for your topic.
  • His coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded" (same source). Both styles can tempt you into decoration.
  • YouTube lets you "compare your 10 latest videos of similar length" with typical retention (YouTube Help), a fair way to compare two styles on your own channel.
  • Wistia reports "the 1-5 minute range is the sweet spot" for explainers (Wistia). Style matters less than length and structure, and style must fit within that.

Where each tends to fit

Whiteboard fits when:

  • You are teaching a process or concept in steps.
  • The audience values clarity over polish.
  • The budget is limited.
  • The topic is abstract and benefits from a hand-drawn metaphor.

Motion graphics fit when:

  • You need to show a product, interface or data.
  • Brand consistency matters.
  • The film will run in a polished context such as a homepage.
  • You need varied scale, camera moves and transitions.

Mixed approaches fit when:

  • You explain the idea with simple drawn visuals, then show the product in motion graphics.

What really drives retention in either style

  1. The hook. A clear opening problem matters more than the style.
  2. Pace of information. Both styles can feel slow or crowded.
  3. Voiceover quality. Mayer's modality principle favours "voiceover with images or animation" (same source), so the voice carries much of the load.
  4. Visual variety. A long whiteboard video where the same hand draws similar boxes for five minutes can drag; the same holds for motion graphics with identical transitions.
  5. Length. Keep within the range your purpose needs.
  6. Relevance. Every visual should explain something.

How to test fairly

  1. Pick one topic and script. Keep the voiceover the same.
  2. Make two short versions, one in each style, about 60 to 90 seconds each.
  3. Publish in comparable placements. The same channel, the same sort of audience, similar timing.
  4. Compare the first 30 seconds and average view duration. YouTube reports an intro figure and a typical retention comparison.
  5. Change nothing else. Same title style, same thumbnail approach.
  6. Repeat on a second topic. One test is not enough.
  7. Weigh cost. If one style costs much more for similar retention, factor that in.

Example: a freelance payment explainer

Example: a studio produces a 75-second explainer on how a freelance payment protection scheme works. They make one version in whiteboard style and one in motion graphics, using the same script and voice. The whiteboard version reads clearly as a three-step process. The motion graphics version shows a mock app screen and a clean payment flow. They publish both to separate audiences on the client's channel and compare the intro figure and average view duration. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result. The client then chooses the style that suits the next explainer, and may choose a mix.

Mistakes

  • Choosing by trend rather than by job.
  • Using whiteboard for software UI and then struggling to show screens.
  • Using motion graphics with heavy decoration that obscures the point.
  • Repeating the same transition for the whole film.
  • Comparing two videos of different length and topic as if style were the only difference.

Checklist

  • Style chosen for the job.
  • Hook and script strong in either style.
  • Visual variety planned.
  • Length within purpose.
  • Test on two topics before generalising.

A decision shortcut

If you are still undecided, ask four questions. Does the film need to show a real product or data screen? If yes, lean towards motion graphics. Is the idea a process that unfolds in steps and benefits from being drawn as you speak? If yes, lean towards whiteboard. Does the client's brand depend on a tightly controlled look? Motion graphics give more control. Is the budget or timeline tight? Whiteboard is often quicker for simple ideas, though a complex whiteboard film is not automatically cheap. Whichever you choose, write the script and storyboard before committing to a style, and review the storyboard with sound off to see whether each frame explains something. A style is a container; the retention comes from what you put in it.
