What should an editor brief include so the first 3 seconds hold attention?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

An editor brief that protects the first 3 seconds names five things: the exact hook line, what the first frame shows, the on-screen text for muted viewers, when the promise in the hook gets paid off, and the metric you will judge the edit by. Without those, editors guess, and the opening is usually where the guess goes wrong.

Key takeaways

  • Platforms judge the opening hard. TikTok advises advertisers to "introduce your content proposition in the first 3 seconds" and to put the hook in the first 6.
  • YouTube's Shorts report shows "the percentage of times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short", so you can check whether the brief worked.
  • A brief should say what the first frame shows, not just "make it punchy".
  • Write the on-screen text into the brief, because many viewers start muted.
  • Agree the success metric before the edit, so feedback is about data, not taste.

Why the opening needs its own section in the brief

Most briefs describe the whole video: tone, length, music, call to action. The first 3 seconds get one line, if any. Yet that is where most viewers decide to stay or scroll. Google's ABCD guidance for video ads tells advertisers to "get to the heart of the story faster, and use engaging pacing and tight framing to draw viewers in". An editor can only do that if the brief says what "the heart of the story" is.

Editors also work from footage, not from your head. If the strongest shot is buried at minute four of the raw files, they may never find it. The brief should point to it.

What to put in the brief: a template

Brief fieldWhat to writeExample
Hook line (spoken)The exact words, under 12 if possible"We spent £400 testing cheap mics so you don't have to."
First frameThe shot that opens the video, with file name and timecodeA-cam_014.mov 02:13, five mics lined up on a desk
On-screen text3-7 words that work with sound off"5 cheap mics. 1 winner."
Payoff timingWhen the promise is answeredWinner named at 0:45 in a 60-second Short
What to avoidThings that waste the openingNo logo sting, no "hey guys", no slow zoom
AudioMusic start, sound effect on first cutMusic in at frame 1, low under voice
Success metricThe number you will checkShorts "stayed to watch" vs channel average
VersionsHow many opening variants you want2 hooks, same body

Keep it to one page. A long brief gets skimmed, and the opening is the part that suffers.

How to write each field well

  1. Hook line. Write it as you want it heard. If you leave it to the edit, the editor will pick whichever line in the recording sounds most energetic, which isn't always the clearest.
  2. First frame. Choose a shot that shows the subject of the video at once. The viewer should know what the video is about before any words land.
  3. On-screen text. TikTok suggests "5-10 words per second when using text" as an upper limit. For a 3-second opening, that means one short line, not a paragraph.
  4. Payoff timing. The hook makes a promise. The brief says where it is kept. This stops editors from dragging the answer out so long that the viewer gives up.
  5. What to avoid. Name the habits you don't want. Editors often add intros by default.
  6. Success metric. For long YouTube videos, use the intro figure in the retention report, which shows "what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds". For Shorts, use "stayed to watch". For ads, use your platform's early-view metric.

Worked example

Example: a cooking creator briefs a freelance editor on a 9-minute YouTube video, "I tried the 3 most viral pasta recipes".

FieldBrief entry
Hook line"One of these viral pastas is a scam. One I now make every week."
First frameClose-up of the three finished plates side by side (B-cam_041.mov 00:08)
On-screen text"3 viral pastas. 1 is a scam."
Payoff timingScam recipe revealed at 3:30; favourite at 8:00
AvoidChannel intro, kitchen wide shot, "today we're making"
MetricIntro retention vs the creator's last five videos
VersionsVersion A opens on plates; version B opens on the taste test reaction

The creator compares the intro figure in YouTube Studio against their recent average using typical retention. If version B wins, that becomes the default for the next brief. The result applies to that channel; it is a test, not a promise.

Common brief mistakes

  • "Make the hook strong." It tells the editor nothing. Give the line.
  • No first frame. The editor opens on whatever came first in the timeline.
  • Text written by the editor at the end. Text should be planned with the hook, not added as decoration.
  • No payoff time. The hook becomes a broken promise.
  • Judging by feel. Agree the number first so revisions aren't a matter of opinion.

How agencies and teams can reuse the brief

Save the brief as a template in your project tool and add a "last result" line to each field. After ten videos you'll have a record of which openings held viewers for each channel or client. FAQ

Is there a template for briefing an editor on the first 3 seconds?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook includes templates and a cheat sheet for hooks and openings; the table on this page is a free starting point you can copy.

Should the brief include the exact hook words?

Yes. Writing the line yourself removes the biggest guess an editor has to make.

How many hook versions should I ask for?

Two is enough for most creators. Keep the body the same so you can see which opening did the work.

What if the best shot isn't in the footage?

Say so in the brief and ask for the strongest available shot of the subject, or plan a pickup shot before the edit starts.

How do I know if the brief worked?

Compare the early-retention metric (intro retention or "stayed to watch") with your recent average for similar videos.
