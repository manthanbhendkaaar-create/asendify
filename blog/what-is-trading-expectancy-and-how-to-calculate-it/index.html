What is trading expectancy, and how do you calculate it?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 19 November 2026 · Last updated 19 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Trading expectancy is the average amount you can expect to win or lose per trade, based on your past results. The formula is: expectancy = (win rate x average win) - (loss rate x average loss). A positive number means the approach has made money on average; a negative one means it has lost. It matters more than win rate alone: a strategy that wins 70% of the time can still lose money if its losers are much bigger than its winners.

Key takeaways

  • Formula: (average win x win rate) - (average loss x loss rate).
  • Win rate alone tells you little; E*TRADE's example shows a 45% strategy making +$170 a trade and a 70% strategy losing $50 a trade.
  • Expectancy is only as good as the data behind it ("garbage in, garbage out"), so you need enough logged trades.
  • Costs (spread, commission, financing) must be included, or a slightly positive expectancy can turn negative.
  • Expressing results in R (multiples of the amount risked) lets you compare trades of different sizes.

The formula

BabyPips' Forexpedia entry on expectancy defines it as "a statistical measure that estimates the average amount a trader can expect to win or lose per trade". E*TRADE's guide Trade expectations (July 2022) writes the same idea as:

Expectancy = (Average winning trade x Win frequency) - (Average losing trade x Loss frequency)

InputWhat it meansWhere to get it
Win rateShare of trades that made moneyCount winners ÷ total trades
Loss rateShare that lost money1 - win rate
Average winMean profit on winning trades (after costs)Your journal
Average lossMean loss on losing trades (after costs), as a positive numberYour journal

BabyPips' example: a 60% win rate, $100 average win and $50 average loss gives 0.6 x $100 - 0.4 x $50 = $60 - $20 = $40 per trade.

Why win rate misleads

E*TRADE compares two strategies:

StrategyWin rateAverage winAverage lossExpectancy per trade
A45%$500$100(500 x 0.45) - (100 x 0.55) = +$170
B70%$100$400(100 x 0.70) - (400 x 0.30) = -$50

As the article puts it, "Winning percentage is only one part of the potential profitability equation." Strategy B feels better day to day, because it wins most of the time, but over many trades it loses. This is the trap behind cutting winners early and letting losers run; see the disposition effect in trading.

Expectancy in R-multiples

Many traders express results in R, where 1R is the amount risked on a trade (entry to stop-loss). A trade that makes twice what was risked is +2R; one stopped out at the planned level is -1R. Expectancy in R lets you compare a £20-risk trade with a £200-risk trade.

Expectancy (R) = (win rate x average winning R) - (loss rate x average losing R)

Worked example: 40 trades from a journal

Example (illustrative): Priya logs 40 CFD trades, risking £50 (1R) on each.

Trades40
Winners16 (40%)

Average winner+£110 (+2.2R) after costs

Expectancy (£)0.40 x £110 - 0.60 x £52 = £44.00 - £31.20 = +£12.80 per trade
Expectancy (R)0.40 x 2.2 - 0.60 x 1.04 = 0.88 - 0.624 = +0.256R

Now she checks costs more carefully and finds she left out overnight financing of about £8 a trade on average. Adjusted expectancy: £12.80 - £8.00 = +£4.80 per trade, or about +0.1R. Still positive, but thin. A small change, such as wider spreads or a few more impulsive trades, could push it below zero. And 40 trades is a small sample; see how many trades you need to know if a strategy works.

Why costs and leverage matter so much

The UK regulator's policy statement on CFDs, PS19/18, estimated that limiting leverage and adding other protections would save retail clients £267m to £451m a year, and found that ESMA's temporary leverage limits "reduced client losses by £77m between August 2018 and October 2018". Higher leverage does not change your expectancy per unit of risk, but it makes each losing trade bigger relative to your account. With a thin or negative expectancy, that speeds up losses.

What is a good expectancy in trading?

Any positive expectancy after all costs, measured over enough trades, means the approach has made money on average. How large it needs to be depends on your costs and how often you trade.

Can a strategy with a low win rate be profitable?

Yes. In E*TRADE's example, a 45% win rate made +$170 a trade because average wins were five times average losses.

How many trades do I need to calculate expectancy?

More is better; a few dozen trades give only a rough estimate. E*TRADE warns that the maths is only useful with enough reliable data.

Should I include costs in expectancy?

Yes. Use profits and losses after spread, commission and financing, or your expectancy will look better than it is.

What is the difference between expectancy and R-multiple?

R-multiple is the result of one trade measured against the amount risked. Expectancy is the average result across many trades, in money or in R.
