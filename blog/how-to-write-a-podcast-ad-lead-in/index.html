How do you write a podcast ad lead-in so listeners don't skip or leave?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Write a podcast ad lead-in by opening a specific question just before the break ("what she found in that email changed the whole company, right after this"), then keeping the read short, honest and in your own voice, and answering the question straight after. Place ads at natural breaks rather than in the first minute or mid-story. Spotify says a steep, sudden drop at an ad slot is the sign that the lead-in needs work.

Key takeaways

  • Spotify: retention dips "probably occur at the same time as your advertisements" and often "normalize back" afterwards.
  • Spotify: "Steep and sudden changes in retention are the best evidence for deciding if your ad slot needs a new lead-in."
  • A good lead-in is an open loop: a specific question the listener wants answered after the break.
  • Avoid ads in the first minute; Spotify warns listeners tend to drop off early.
  • Make the read relevant and honest; follow your platform's and country's rules on disclosing ads.

What the data shows

Spotify's guide to understanding episode performance is direct about ads. Dips in the retention curve "probably occur at the same time as your advertisements", and "these lapses in listenership or viewership don't necessarily mean that people left your podcast", because "the retention curve normalizes back to where it was before the ad." But it adds: "Steep and sudden changes in retention are the best evidence for deciding if your ad slot needs a new lead-in" (Spotify for Creators).

On YouTube video podcasts, the key-moments view marks dips where "viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). Look at whether the curve recovers after the read. If it does, people skipped. If it doesn't, they left.

Why an open question works before a break

Carnegie Mellon's George Loewenstein argued that curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge" (quoted in PBS NOVA). A lead-in that names a specific gap ("what the bank said on the phone") gives listeners a reason to wait through the read. A generic "we'll be right back" gives them none.

Lead-in formulas

  1. The cliffhanger: "And then the bank called. What they said, right after this."
  2. The tease of the best part: "Next, she explains the one pricing change she says made the biggest difference. First, a quick word from..." (only tease what the episode really contains).
  3. The question to the listener: "Before we get to why the deal fell apart, here's who's supporting the show."
  4. The bridge: connect the sponsor to the topic honestly ("Speaking of tracking every invoice...") when it genuinely fits.

After the read, return with the answer in the first sentence: "So, the bank. They wanted the loan repaid in 30 days."

Make the read itself better

  • Keep it short and in your normal voice.
  • Be specific and honest: what you actually use or like about the product, if you do.
  • Disclose clearly that it's an ad or sponsorship, following the rules where you and your audience are (in the UK, for example, advertising rules require ads to be obviously identifiable).
  • Avoid sudden changes in sound (louder music, a different room) that cue people to skip.
  • Use chapters on YouTube wisely; viewers can jump past reads, which is better than leaving.

Host-read or pre-recorded ads?

  • Host-read ads sound like part of the show and let you write a lead-in that flows from the conversation.
  • Pre-recorded or dynamically inserted ads are easier to sell and swap, but the change in voice and sound is an obvious cue to skip.
  • Either way, the lead-in is yours: you control the question that comes before the break and the answer that comes after it.
  • Check the placement of inserted ads against your edit, so a slot doesn't land in the middle of a story.

Example: fixing a read that loses listeners

Example (a hypothetical episode, to show the method): a 50-minute interview has a 90-second ad read at 0:45, right after the welcome. Spotify's curve drops sharply there and never recovers; YouTube shows a dip at the same point.

Changes for the next episode:

  • Start with a 30-second cold open from the guest's best story.
  • Move the read to 18:00, at the end of the first story.
  • New lead-in: "That got her the first client. Losing them three months later is the strangest part. Right after this."
  • After the read: "So, three months later, the client sent a one-line email..."

Then compare the curve at the ad slot with previous episodes: a dip that recovers is fine; a cliff means the lead-in or the placement still needs work.

FAQ

Is there a book on keeping listeners through podcast ad breaks?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops and re-hooks and applies them to podcasts.

Where should ads go in a podcast?

At natural breaks, after you've delivered some value, not in the first minute or mid-story.

Do listeners leave during ads?

Many skip. Spotify says curves often recover after the ad; a steep drop that doesn't recover is the warning sign.

How long should a podcast ad read be?

As short as the sponsor allows. Shorter, honest reads in your own voice tend to hold attention better.

Should I tell listeners it's an ad?

Yes. Make ads clearly identifiable and follow the disclosure rules that apply to you.

What is a good ad lead-in?

A specific open question that the content right after the ad answers.
