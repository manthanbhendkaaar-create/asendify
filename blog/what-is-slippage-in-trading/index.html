What is slippage in trading, and how do you reduce it?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 20 November 2026 · Last updated 20 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Slippage is the difference between the price you expected when you placed an order and the price you actually got. It happens because a market order fills at the best price available at that moment, not the last price you saw, and in fast or thin markets those can differ. Stop orders are exposed too, because they turn into market orders. Limit orders prevent slippage on price but may not fill at all.

This page is education only, not financial advice. Trading is risky and you can lose money.

Key takeaways

  • The SEC: "the last-traded price is not necessarily the price at which a market order will be executed."
  • FINRA: with a market order, "you might not get the price you saw or were originally quoted, especially in fast-moving markets."
  • A large market order can fill in pieces at different prices when there is not enough volume at the best price.
  • Limit orders give you the limit price or better, but there is a chance they never execute.
  • Orders placed outside regular hours can be hit by big price moves when the market reopens.

Why slippage happens

The SEC's investor bulletin on order types (updated August 2026) explains that a market order buys or sells at the best available price and usually executes immediately, but the price is not guaranteed: "the last-traded price is not necessarily the price at which a market order will be executed". The execution price depends on demand and on how much is available to trade at that moment.

FINRA's guide to order types lists the causes: delayed quotes, the time it takes to execute, and volatility. It notes that "in highly volatile markets, millions of shares can trade in microseconds causing price swings", and that no order type eliminates market risk.

The SEC's partial-fill example

Investor.gov's trading basics guide gives a simple example. You place a market order to buy 1,000 shares of XYZ when the best offer is $3.00. If only 500 shares are available at $3.00, those fill at $3.00 and the other 500 fill at a higher price. If other orders reach the market first, your whole order may fill above $3.00.

FINRA notes that limit orders are often used when price matters more than speed, and that market orders get the highest execution priority, followed by limit orders. Our guide to stop-loss orders explains the trade-off for exits in more detail.

Ways to reduce slippage

  • Use limit orders for entries when price matters more than speed.
  • Avoid trading around major news if your strategy does not need it.
  • Trade liquid markets and sizes that the market can absorb at the best price.
  • Be careful outside regular hours. FINRA warns that orders placed before or after the session can be hit by big moves when it reopens.
  • Check your firm's rules. The SEC notes that order types and their rules vary between brokers, even when the names are similar.
  • Measure it. Record intended price and actual fill on every trade.

Is slippage always bad?

No. Prices can move in your favour between placing and filling an order. In fast markets, though, stop orders tend to fill at worse prices.

Do limit orders have slippage?

Not on price: a limit order fills at your limit or better. The risk is that it does not fill at all.

Why did my stop fill below my stop price?

A triggered stop becomes a market order and fills at the best available price, which can be lower in a fast market or after a gap.

Is slippage the same as the spread?

No. The spread is the gap between the buying and selling price at a moment in time. Slippage is the gap between the price you expected and the price you got.

How do I measure slippage?

Record the price you intended on each order and the actual fill, then total the difference across trades.
