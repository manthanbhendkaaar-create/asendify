Where should mid-roll ads go in a long gaming highlight video?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Put mid-roll ads at natural breaks between plays, not in the middle of a fight, a clutch moment or a sentence. A highlight video must be at least 8 minutes and monetised to qualify, and YouTube says slots at natural breakpoints, such as a pause in audio or a visual transition, are more likely to serve ads than slots that interrupt speech or action.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube says "on monetized videos that are 8 minutes or longer, you can turn on ads during the middle of the video" (YouTube Help).
  • Slots "placed at natural breakpoints, such as a pause in audio or transition in visual" are more likely to serve; slots that are "mid-sentence or mid-action" can be disruptive, per the same page.
  • You can place slots manually or let YouTube find breakpoints automatically, and combine both.
  • A flat retention line means viewers watch that part start to finish; dips mean they abandon or skip (YouTube Help). Check the graph around each slot.

What a highlight video gives you to work with

A gaming highlight video is a string of short peaks: a kill, a save, a funny failure. Between the peaks there is usually a natural gap, such as a respawn, a loading screen, a menu, a replay or a change of game. Those gaps are your ad candidates. The peaks are not.

The danger in a highlight edit is that the clips are cut tightly, so there are no quiet moments. That leaves two choices. You can build a deliberate break into the edit, or you can accept fewer ad slots. Cutting ads into the middle of a clip is the choice most likely to lose the viewer, because the viewer was in the middle of a payoff.

How to plan a highlight edit around ads

  1. Decide the length by content. If you have 14 minutes of strong clips, there is room for slots. If you have 7, publish 7. Stretching a video to reach 8 minutes with weak clips will lower retention.
  2. Group clips into acts. For example, "opening rounds", "mid-game swing", "final push". Each act ends in a natural pause.
  3. Add a short breather at act ends. One or two seconds of a calm shot, a score screen or a title card gives the break a clear edge without padding.
  4. Mark slots in YouTube Studio under ad settings. Manual placement lets you choose the exact second. Preview each one.
  5. Put your best clip before the first slot. Viewers who see the payoff early are more likely to stay through the break.
  6. Keep audio simple around slots. End the music or commentary cleanly rather than cutting it mid-word.
  7. Review the retention graph after publishing. Look at the viewing curve around each slot and compare it with similar-length videos.

Should you use automatic placement?

Automatic placement is quick and finds breakpoints for you, and the YouTube page says automatic slots can be used together with manual slots. For a highlight video, treat automatic placement as a starting point. Check the proposed positions in the preview and delete any that land in the middle of a play. A tight highlight edit may have fewer true breaks than the system expects. That is a reason to check, not a reason to avoid automatic placement.

How many slots?

YouTube does not give a magic number for the best count. The sensible approach is to start with fewer slots than you think you need, watch the retention graph, and add one more only if viewers keep watching through the earlier ones. A 10-minute highlight might begin with one or two slots; a 20-minute compilation might begin with three or four. These are planning starting points from editing practice, not research findings, and your own graph is the test.

Example: a 14-minute battle royale compilation

Example: a creator edits 14 minutes of clips from one season. The first draft has four slots set automatically at 3:10, 5:45, 8:20 and 11:30. In the preview, the 5:45 slot lands mid-fight and the 11:30 slot lands in the middle of the commentator's sentence. The creator moves 5:45 to 6:05, after the replay of the fight finishes, and moves 11:30 to 11:50, where a score screen appears. She also adds a one-second title card at 8:20, "Round 3", to give that slot a clear boundary. After two weeks, she checks the retention graph and compares it with her last five videos of similar length. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Chapters and mid-rolls together

Chapters give viewers a map and give you natural boundaries. YouTube's chapter rules need a first timestamp of 00:00, at least three timestamps and chapters of at least 10 seconds (YouTube Help). Using the chapter boundaries as ad boundaries is one way to keep the structure consistent: the viewer expects a break where a new chapter starts.

Do I need 8 minutes for mid-roll ads?

On YouTube, yes: monetised videos of 8 minutes or longer can turn on mid-roll ads.

Is automatic placement safe for highlights?

Use it as a starting point, then preview each slot and move any that land mid-action.

Should I make a video longer to fit more ads?

No. Padding weakens the video. Publish the length the clips deserve.

Where do I see if an ad slot hurt retention?

In the audience retention graph in YouTube Studio, around the slot's time.
