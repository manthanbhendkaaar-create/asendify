How do I make client video ads accessible without losing attention?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Make client video ads accessible by adding accurate captions, keeping on-screen text large and high-contrast, avoiding rapid flashing, keeping speech clear above music, and making sure the message works without sound and without relying on colour alone. These changes rarely cost attention; most make the ad easier for everyone to follow in a busy feed, which is good for retention as well as fairness.

Key takeaways

  • Captions should be accurate and synchronised; the W3C warns that auto-captions don't meet accessibility needs unless fully accurate.
  • WCAG asks for no more than three flashes in any one second; saturated red flashing is riskier.
  • WCAG's contrast minimum is 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text.
  • In UK advertising, the ASA points advertisers to Ofcom's flashing-images guidance and recommends testing with the Harding test or an equivalent.
  • Accessible choices (clear text, sound-off design) usually support retention rather than fight it.

Why accessibility belongs in the edit

Agencies often treat accessibility as a compliance box at the end. It is better handled in the edit, because the same choices that help disabled viewers help everyone scrolling with the sound off on a small screen in bright light. A caption that is accurate, a headline that is big enough to read, and a pace that lets text be read all serve both goals.

Captions

The W3C's guidance on captions describes them as "a text version of the speech and non-speech audio information needed to understand the content", synchronised with the audio. It warns that "automatically-generated captions do not meet user needs or accessibility requirements" unless they are fully accurate. For ads, burned-in captions are common because many feeds autoplay muted; check every word, especially brand names and prices, and include important sounds ("[doorbell rings]") if they carry meaning.

Contrast and readable text

WCAG's contrast minimum for text and images of text is a ratio of at least 4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text (18 point, or 14 point bold). The guideline was written for web content, not video, but it is a useful benchmark for headline text in an ad. Add a solid or semi-transparent background behind text over busy footage. Keep text on screen long enough to read; TikTok's creative guidance suggests 5 to 10 words per second.

Flashing and strobing

Fast cuts and flashes are tempting in a hook, but they carry real risk. WCAG's three flashes rule says content should not flash "more than three times in any one second period", and notes that "people are even more sensitive to red flashing than to other colors".

For UK ads, the ASA's guidance on flashing images in advertising points advertisers to the CAP and BCAP Codes and to Ofcom's guidance note, and says broadcast ads should pass "a Harding Flash and Pattern Analyser (FPA) Test or other suitably robust equivalent test". It upheld complaints about a 2012 TV ad with a repeatedly flashing word. For online ads, it advises testing if you have concerns.

Audio

Keep the voice clearly above any music, avoid sudden loud effects, and make sure the key message is also on screen for people who can't hear it.

Does accessibility hurt retention?

Usually not. The main changes are:

  • Captions: they help sound-off viewers follow the ad, which is most of a feed.
  • Bigger, clearer text: easier to read in a scroll.
  • Fewer flashes: you lose a strobe effect, but you can still open on movement, a face or a surprising result.
  • Clear audio: fewer people leave because they can't make out the words.

If a client's concept relies on rapid flashing, offer alternatives that create energy without it: quick cuts between steady shots, punch-in zooms, or a bold reveal.

Worked example

Example: an agency's 15-second ad for a client's festival tickets opens with a strobe-style flash of the lineup, white text over moving crowd footage, and a voice-over drowned by a bass-heavy track.

The accessibility pass changes three things. The strobe opening becomes a fast push-in on the headliner's name, with no flashing. The text gets a dark semi-transparent panel behind it and is enlarged so it reads on a phone. The music is ducked under the voice-over, and captions are corrected ("Saturday" had been auto-captioned wrongly). The hook still opens on the biggest name in the first second, and the ad is now safer, clearer and easier to follow muted.

FAQ

Is there a checklist for making video ads accessible?

Yes: the seven-area table above covers captions, contrast, text timing, flashing, audio, colour and sound-off design. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook supplies a pre-export checklist you can add these lines to.

Do video ads legally need captions?

Requirements vary by platform, country and sector. Captions are good practice regardless, as many viewers watch muted.

How do I test an ad for flashing?

The ASA mentions the Harding Flash and Pattern Analyser test or an equivalent. Many post-production houses offer this.

Are auto-captions good enough?

Only if you correct them. The W3C says auto-captions don't meet accessibility needs unless fully accurate.

Does accessible design make ads boring?

No. You can keep fast pacing and bold hooks; you just avoid rapid flashing and unreadable text.
