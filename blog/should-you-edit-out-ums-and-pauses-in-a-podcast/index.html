Should you edit out ums and pauses in a podcast?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes, cut most ums, false starts and long dead air, but not all of them. Remove fillers that slow the conversation or make a guest sound unsure, and tighten pauses that go nowhere. Keep the pauses and hesitations that carry meaning: a breath before a hard answer, a beat after a joke, a moment of thought before a reveal. Over-cleaned audio sounds robotic and can make speech harder to follow.

Key takeaways

  • Fillers aren't random noise: research by Clark and Fox Tree found speakers use "uh" to signal a minor delay and "um" a major one.
  • Cut fillers that pile up, repeat or make a point hard to follow; keep the ones that sound natural and carry timing.
  • Dead air and false starts are the biggest drag on pacing; remove them first.
  • Tools such as Descript can remove filler words "in a click", but always listen back.
  • Edit hardest in the first few minutes, where Spotify says listeners are most likely to leave.

What fillers actually do

"Um" and "uh" are part of how people talk. In a 2002 paper in the journal Cognition, psychologists Herbert Clark and Jean Fox Tree argued that speakers use them to "announce that they are initiating what they expect to be a minor (uh), or major (um), delay in speaking" (Clark and Fox Tree, 2002, PDF). In other words, a filler tells the listener "wait, more is coming".

That's useful in live conversation. In an edited podcast, where the listener can't interrupt, a stream of fillers mostly slows things down. But a few left in place keep speech sounding human and keep natural timing.

What to cut, what to keep

How much should you remove?

A practical rule: cut until the conversation moves at the pace of an engaged chat, then stop. Signs you've gone too far:

  • Sentences run together with no breath between them.
  • Speakers sound like they're answering before the question ends.
  • The rhythm feels mechanical, with every gap the same length.
  • Emotional moments lose their weight because the pause before them is gone.

Different shows want different levels. A tight news briefing can be heavily cleaned. A relaxed, conversational show can keep more texture. Interviews with nervous guests often benefit most, because a lot of fillers can make a smart guest sound unsure of what they're saying.

Start with the first five minutes

Spotify's guide to episode performance warns that "listeners have a tendency to drop off early, so it's crucial to capture people's attention as quickly as possible" (Spotify for Creators). That makes the opening the most valuable place to edit tightly. Remove every hesitation, warm-up and false start in the first few minutes, even if you allow more texture later.

On YouTube, sudden dips in the retention graph show where "viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). If dips line up with rambling answers, that's where to tighten.

Tools that help

  • Descript describes itself as an editor that "lets you edit by editing text", and says you can "cut filler words and retakes in a click" (Descript). It's fast for removing fillers across a long transcript.
  • Most editors (Premiere Pro, Resolve, Audition, Reaper and others) let you find silences and fillers by waveform, then ripple-delete.
  • Automatic tools are a first pass. Listen back: they sometimes cut words that sound like fillers, leave clicks at the edit, or remove pauses that mattered.

On video, every cut is visible unless you hide it. Cover tightened sections by switching camera angle, punching in, or laying B-roll over the join; otherwise the picture jumps.

Example: cleaning a 10-minute answer

Example (a hypothetical guest answer, to show the method): a guest's answer about how they found their first customers runs 10 minutes in the raw recording.

  • 1 minute 20 seconds of false starts and "so, basically, um, the thing is" at the beginning: cut to the first clear sentence.
  • 14 fillers scattered through: remove 11, keep three that sit before a thoughtful point.
  • Two long pauses while the guest searched for a name: cut.
  • One 2-second pause before the guest says the business nearly closed: keep. It's the emotional beat.
  • A repeated explanation of the same tactic: keep the clearer version.

The answer now runs about 7 minutes, says the same thing, and the important moment still lands.

Does cutting fillers make you sound fake?

It can, if you overdo it. Listeners hear when every breath is gone and every gap is identical. The goal is not perfect speech; it's speech without friction. Lower breaths rather than deleting them, vary pause lengths, and leave a little texture so the conversation still sounds like two people talking.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches how tight to edit a podcast?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers pacing, removing silence and dead air, and applies it to podcasts and clips.

Should I remove every um?

No. Remove strings and distracting ones; keep a few that sound natural and carry timing.

Should I delete breaths?

Usually lower them rather than delete them. Removing every breath makes speech sound unnatural.

Will listeners notice edits?

They notice bad ones: clipped words, clicks, or a picture that jumps. Use crossfades in audio and angle changes or B-roll in video.

How long does it take to edit fillers out?

It depends on the recording and the tools. Text-based editors speed it up a lot, but always leave time to listen back.

Is it OK to tidy a guest's answer?

Yes, as long as you keep their meaning. Never edit someone so they appear to say something they didn't.
