Should I use a teleprompter for founder talking-head videos, or speak from bullet points?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

For most founder and talking-head videos, speak from a few bullet points and record several takes: it sounds like you, and natural, energetic delivery holds viewers better than careful reading. Use a teleprompter when the exact words matter (a legal line, a product launch announcement, a tightly timed ad), and if you do, write the script the way you talk, break it into short chunks, and let the operator or app follow your pace. Y Combinator's advice for its founder videos sums up the default: "Do not recite a written script: Use bullet points instead."

Key takeaways

  • Default to bullet points for founder updates, LinkedIn videos, YouTube talking heads and prospecting videos.
  • Use a teleprompter when wording must be exact or timing is tight.
  • Reading is usually visible: eyes tracking across lines, flat intonation, no pauses.
  • Teleprompter scripts must be written for the ear: short sentences, contractions, your own phrasing.
  • Speed should follow the speaker, not the other way round.
  • Either way, the edit (cutting pauses, restarts and weak openings) does a lot of the work.

Why reading loses viewers

  • The eyes give it away. Even with a beam-splitter teleprompter in front of the lens, eyes move side to side if lines are long.
  • Written sentences sound written. "Our platform enables organisations to streamline..." is not how anyone talks.
  • No natural pauses or emphasis. Readers stress the wrong words because they're decoding, not meaning.
  • Lower energy. Concentrating on the text leaves less attention for performing.

If you speak from bullet points

  1. Write three to six bullets, each a single idea in a few words ("Problem: customers wait 3 days for quotes").
  2. Put them just below or beside the lens, in large text.
  3. Say the opening line out loud a few times until it's natural; that line matters most.
  4. Record each bullet as its own short take if you lose your way; the editor joins them.
  5. Do the whole thing two or three times. Later takes are usually looser.

In the edit, cut restarts, long pauses and filler, and use jump cuts or a punch-in between sections. Viewers accept jump cuts in talking-head videos.

If you use a teleprompter

  • Write for the ear. Read the script aloud before filming and rewrite anything you stumble on.
  • Use short lines. Fewer words per line means less visible eye movement; PremiumBeat's advice is to take in "full lines at a time".
  • Break it into chunks. Pauses between paragraphs give natural breathing points and edit points.
  • Let the speed follow you. A human operator or a voice-tracking app that scrolls at your pace beats a fixed speed.
  • Mark emphasis and pauses in the script (bold a word, add a line break).
  • Glance away occasionally as you would in conversation, and ad-lib small phrases if it feels right.
  • Rehearse until it's familiar, so the prompter is a safety net rather than something you read word by word.

Example: a founder's product update

Example: a founder records a 4-minute product update from a full script on a laptop teleprompter app. The video sounds stiff: long sentences, little variation, eyes clearly tracking.

For the next update she writes five bullets on a card under the camera: the headline change, why customers asked for it, a 30-second screen demo, what's next, and how to give feedback. She records each bullet twice, keeps the better take, and the editor cuts the result to 2 minutes 40 seconds. The opening line ("You can now send quotes from your phone") is the only sentence she memorised. Founder and product are illustrative.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches founders to make talking-head videos people watch?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks and pacing for £1,720 one-time. Practising with bullet points and watching your takes back is a free alternative.

Do professional presenters use teleprompters?

Many do, especially in news and corporate video, after a lot of practice. For most founders, bullet points are easier to make sound natural.

Can viewers tell I'm reading?

Often, yes: eye movement and flat delivery are common giveaways. Short lines and rehearsal reduce it.

What's the best place for bullet points?

Just below or beside the camera lens, in large text, so your eyes stay close to the lens.

Should I memorise my script instead?

Memorise the opening line. For the rest, bullets usually sound more natural than a memorised script.
