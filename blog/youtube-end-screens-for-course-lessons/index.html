How do I use YouTube end screens to move learners to the next course lesson?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Use the end screen as a handoff, not a goodbye. Finish the lesson's teaching about 10 to 15 seconds early, say what the next lesson solves in one sentence, and place a video or playlist element for that lesson on screen while you say it. Learners who finish a lesson are already motivated; your job is to remove the choice about what to watch next.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube says "End screens can be added to the last 5–20 seconds of a video" and the video "has to be at least 25 seconds long" to have one (YouTube Help).
  • You can add "up to four elements" on a standard 16:9 video; types include video or playlist, subscribe, channel and link. Links to external websites require YouTube Partner Program membership (same page).
  • End screens are not available on videos set as made for kids (same page).
  • YouTube's retention report says a flat line means viewers are watching "from start to finish", and dips show where they leave (YouTube Help). Check the final seconds of each lesson, because that is where an end screen succeeds or fails.
  • Mayer's segmenting principle says "People learn better when the information is presented in user-paced segments" (Dartmouth). A course split into lessons with a clear next step respects that.

What an end screen can and cannot do

An end screen is a set of clickable elements placed over the last seconds of a video. For course lessons the useful ones are the video element (the next lesson), the playlist element (the whole course) and, for monetised channels in the Partner Program, a link element to a signup or resource page. The subscribe element is useful for the first lesson, less so for lesson seven when the learner is already deep into the series.

YouTube's rule is specific: the end screen lives in the last 5 to 20 seconds, and you get at most four elements on a 16:9 video. That is a design constraint worth embracing. If four things compete for attention, the learner picks none. For lessons, two is plenty: next lesson and playlist.

Step 1: Finish the teaching before the end-screen window

If the lesson's last useful sentence lands in the final second, the end screen sits on top of content. Instead, end the content roughly 10 to 15 seconds before the video ends and use that tail for the handoff. Edit the tail with a calm, static or slow background so the elements are readable.

A tail that works has three beats:

  1. One-line recap. "That was how to set up a basic budget sheet."
  2. One-line promise. "Next, we add formulas so the sheet updates itself."
  3. One action. "The next lesson is on screen now."

Say the action in plain words. Learners respond to a clear instruction more than to a vague "see you next time".

Step 2: Make the next lesson feel inevitable

The best handoff creates a small open loop. Do not tease something you will not deliver; instead state a real question the next lesson answers. "Your sheet works, but what happens when a month has five Fridays? That is next." This is the same idea as an open loop in any retention-edited video: a question raised and promised an answer.

Match the promise to the next lesson's actual opening. If lesson 4 ends promising formulas and lesson 5 opens with a ten-minute recap of lesson 3, the learner feels the mismatch and leaves.

Step 3: Layout the screen so nothing clashes

ElementBest use in a coursePlacement note
Video element (next lesson)Primary actionLarge, central, so the learner sees it first
Playlist elementWhole-course view for learners who lost their placeSecondary, smaller
SubscribeEarly lessons onlyUse sparingly later in the series
LinkWorksheet, signup or resource pageNeeds Partner Program; test on mobile
ChannelRarely needed inside a courseSkip unless it adds something

Keep your own graphics out of the areas where the elements will sit. A lower-third, logo or caption in the same spot makes both unreadable. Check on a phone: elements shift with screen size and aspect ratio, so view the final lesson on mobile before you publish.

Step 4: Check the numbers in YouTube Studio

Open the retention graph for a lesson and look at the last 30 seconds. If viewers drop sharply well before the end, they have stopped caring before the handoff, and an end screen cannot help. The fix is upstream: tighten the content or move the handoff earlier. If the graph is steady until the end and then falls off, the handoff is the problem: the recap is too long or the next step is unclear.

YouTube's own advice is to "review your dips to better understand why audiences lost interest in a particular segment." Use that on the final segment, then change one thing per lesson and compare across lessons of similar length.

Example: a five-lesson course on spreadsheets

Example: a creator publishes a free five-lesson spreadsheet course on YouTube. Lesson 1 ends on its last teaching sentence at the final second, and the end screen shows four elements: subscribe, two random videos, and a link. Lesson 2 is edited differently: teaching ends at 12 seconds before the finish, the narrator gives a one-line recap and promise, and the end screen shows only the next-lesson video and the course playlist. The creator then compares the retention curves for the last 30 seconds and the end-screen click behaviour across the lessons. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result, but it shows what to compare: same course, one change.

Step 5: Use the same logic outside the end screen

An end screen is only one handoff. Pair it with:

  • A pinned comment linking the next lesson.
  • A description line with the next lesson link first.
  • Chapters so learners can jump back. YouTube requires at least three timestamps, the first at 00:00 and each at least 10 seconds long (YouTube Help).
  • A playlist order that matches your course order, so autoplay does the work when learners do not click.

If your course is also sold or hosted off YouTube, be clear in the video what is free and what is not. A learner who clicks a link expecting a free worksheet and lands on a sales page loses trust quickly.

Common mistakes

  • Putting teaching under the elements. The elements cover the content.
  • Too many choices. Four elements for a learner who needs one next step.
  • A vague goodbye. "Thanks for watching" with no next step.
  • A mismatched promise. The next lesson does not open on what you teased.
  • Forgetting made-for-kids limits. End screens are unavailable on those videos.
  • Not testing on mobile. Layout shifts on small screens.

FAQ

How long should the end screen be on a lesson?

YouTube allows end screens in the last 5 to 20 seconds. For a lesson, 10 to 15 seconds is usually enough for a recap, a promise and the click.

How many elements should I use?

You can use up to four on a 16:9 video, but two (next lesson and playlist) is a cleaner choice for most course lessons.

Can I link to my course site from an end screen?

Link elements to external websites require YouTube Partner Program membership. Without it, use the description and a pinned comment.

Do end screens work on short videos?

The video must be at least 25 seconds long to have an end screen.
