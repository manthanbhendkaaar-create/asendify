How do I show slides and my face together in a course lesson?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Do not pick one layout for the whole lesson. Open with your face full-screen so the learner connects with you, switch to slides with a small face window while you explain, go slides-only when a diagram needs the full frame, and return to your face to close. The aim is that the learner always knows where to look.

Key takeaways

  • Mayer's personalization principle says "learning is more effective when teachers use a friendly, conversational style, rather than a formal style" (Dartmouth). Showing your face is one way to carry that tone.
  • Spatial contiguity: "learning is more effective when corresponding images and text are placed close together, rather than far apart" (same page).
  • Redundancy: "graphics, voiceover, and on-screen text can be overwhelming for learners" (same page), so slides should not copy your narration word for word.
  • Coherence: "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded" (same page).
  • YouTube's retention report treats dips as moments where "viewers are abandoning or skipping" (YouTube Help); use it to see which layout holds people.

The trade-off: connection versus clarity

Your face brings warmth, tone and a sense of a person. Slides bring structure, diagrams and written terms. A layout that is all face loses the visuals; a layout that is all slides can feel like a recorded document. The decision is not either-or; it is which one the learner needs right now.

A useful test: if the viewer would be lost without seeing the slide, the slide dominates. If the point is a personal explanation, a story or a transition, your face dominates.

Step 1: Design three standard layouts

Build these once in your editor and reuse them across lessons. Consistency helps learners relax because the screen behaves predictably.

Place the face window in the corner that does not cover important slide content, and keep it in the same corner throughout the lesson.

Step 2: Plan the transitions

Switch layouts when the purpose changes, not when you feel like variety.

  • Open on A. Say what the lesson solves in a sentence or two.
  • Move to B when you introduce the first slide.
  • Move to C when a slide needs the full frame, for example a diagram with small labels.
  • Return to B for the explanation of that diagram.
  • Return to A for a story, a warning or the wrap-up.

Keep the switch crisp: a quick cut or a short dissolve. Slow or fancy transitions eat time and distract.

Step 3: Build slides for video, not for a room

Slides designed for a classroom projector are often too dense for a phone or laptop video.

  1. One idea per slide. If it needs two sentences of explanation, consider two slides.
  2. Short text. A few words or a short list. The redundancy principle warns that voiceover, graphics and identical on-screen text can overwhelm, so do not read the slide aloud word for word.
  3. Large type. Aim for readable on a phone.
  4. Keep labels beside their parts. Spatial contiguity says matching text and images should sit close together.
  5. Reveal step by step. Build a diagram or list one item at a time, in step with your voice.
  6. Leave room for the face window. Keep a clear area for the corner window and for captions.

Step 4: Light and frame your face for the small window

In layout B the face window is small, so details matter.

  • Frame from mid-chest up with eyes at about the upper third of the frame.
  • Light your face from the front with a window or soft lamp, with no strong light behind you.
  • Look at the lens when you are talking to the learner, and at your slides only when you are pointing something out.
  • Keep the background plain so it does not compete with the slide.

Step 5: Keep audio consistent across layouts

Switching layouts should not change how you sound. Use the same microphone and distance. A learner who hears an echo in one layout and not in another will notice the cut. Record the narration and face in the same session where possible.

Step 6: Use signalling to direct the eye

Mayer's signalling principle says "people learn better when there are cues that emphasize information that is more important." In a layout with a face and a slide, the learner has two places to look. Direct them: a highlight on the item you are discussing, an arrow, or a short zoom. When you move to a new item, move the cue.

Step 7: Check the retention graph

After publishing, look at the graph for each layout segment. If viewers drop when the layout changes, the transition may be jarring. If they drop during long slides-only segments, add your face or a visual change. If they drop during long face-only segments, add a slide or a diagram. Change one thing at a time and compare against a similar lesson.

Example: a ten-minute lesson on setting prices

Example: a course creator records a lesson on pricing a service. The lesson opens on the face full-screen for 20 seconds with the problem. Then it moves to a slide titled with the first of three approaches, with a small face window bottom-right. A diagram of three price tiers goes slides-only while the narration explains the labels, then returns to slide-plus-face. The final 20 seconds returns to the face for a recap and the next lesson. Step 8: Accessibility and captions

Add accurate captions. WCAG says captions should "convey not only the content of spoken dialogue" but also meaningful sounds (W3C). Place captions where they do not cover the face window or important slide content; if your slide design leaves a clear band at the bottom, the problem disappears.

Common mistakes

  • One layout for everything. It either hides your face or hides the slide.
  • Reading the slide aloud. It adds load, not clarity.
  • A face window that covers slide content. Move it.
  • Dense slides. Split them.
  • Switching layouts for variety. Switch for a purpose.
  • Changing audio quality between layouts. Keep the sound consistent.

FAQ

Should my face be on screen the whole lesson?

Not necessarily. Use it for connection moments and keep a small window during explanation. Hide it when a diagram needs the full frame.

Where should the face window go?

In a corner that does not cover slide content, and keep it in the same corner throughout.

How much text should be on a slide?

A few words or a short list. Do not read the slide aloud word for word.

Do I need captions on course lessons?

Captions help people who are deaf or hard of hearing and anyone watching without sound. Check them for accuracy.
