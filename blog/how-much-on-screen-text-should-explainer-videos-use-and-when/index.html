How much on-screen text should an explainer video use, and when?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

An explainer video should use short on-screen text, such as key words, labels, numbers and step titles, placed next to the visual it describes and shown at the moment the narrator says it. Avoid putting full sentences on screen that repeat the voiceover word for word. Learning research finds that adding duplicate text to narrated graphics usually does not help, while short, well-placed labels do.

Key takeaways

  • Mayer's redundancy principle states "people do not learn more deeply when on-screen text is added to narrated graphics" (8 of 12 tests). The effect can shrink or reverse with short or reworded text, slow pacing or viewers watching in a second language.
  • The same summary reports strong support for spatial contiguity (words placed near the graphics they describe, 9 of 9 tests) and temporal contiguity (graphics and narration at the same time, 8 of 8 tests).
  • The signaling principle, cues that highlight structure, was supported in 26 of 28 tests, which backs step titles and key-word highlights.
  • Reading has limits. Netflix's timed text guide uses up to 20 characters per second for adult subtitles, so long text that flashes past cannot be read.
  • These are learning studies, not YouTube retention studies. Use YouTube's key moments report to see where your text-heavy sections lose viewers.

Why less text often holds attention better

When the narration and the screen say the same long sentence, viewers try to read and listen at once, and the two compete. Short text works differently: it anchors the main idea without asking for much reading. Placing a label right next to the thing it names means viewers do not have to search the frame, and showing it as the narrator says it ties the word and the picture together.

There are good exceptions. Viewers watching in a second language, or with the sound off, benefit from more text. That is where captions come in: a caption track is separate from your on-screen graphics and can be turned on by those who need it.

A practical text plan for an explainer

  1. Script first, then mark key words that deserve to appear on screen, around one per idea.
  2. Write labels as short as possible, a few words at most.
  3. Place each label next to its visual, not in a separate text area.
  4. Time each word to the narration, appearing as it is spoken.
  5. Use step titles to mark structure on longer explainers.
  6. Add a caption track for accessibility and sound-off viewing.
  7. Check text on a phone, because small labels disappear at that size.

A quick test for every text element

For each word on screen, ask three questions. Does it say something the picture and narration do not already make clear? Is it next to the thing it refers to? Can a viewer read it in the time it is shown? If the answer to any is no, shorten it, move it or cut it.

Short-form explainers are different

In vertical, sound-off feeds, more text is normal because many viewers do not hear the narration. Even then, keep each caption short, keep it inside the app's safe zone and highlight the key word in each line rather than adding extra text boxes.

FAQ

Is there a guide on on-screen text and pacing for explainer videos?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers on-screen text, pacing and hooks with templates and a 30-day plan. Mayer's research summaries are a free companion.

Should an explainer video show the full script as text?

Usually not. Research finds duplicate text on narrated graphics rarely helps; use key words and add a caption track instead.

When is more on-screen text a good idea?

For sound-off feeds, second-language viewers and videos without narration. Keep each line short.

Where should labels go?

Right next to the part of the visual they describe, not in a separate text area.

How long should text stay on screen?

Long enough to read comfortably. Subtitle guides use about 20 characters per second as an upper reading speed for adults.
