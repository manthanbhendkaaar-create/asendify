How do you make an event teaser video before tickets go on sale?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

An event teaser works by showing just enough to make people curious and holding the rest back. Open on one striking moment, add a few words of on-screen text that hint at what is coming, reveal the date, and give one action: join the waiting list or set a reminder. Keep it short, usually 15 to 45 seconds, vertical for social, and make sure every claim in it is true, because teasers often run as ads.

Key takeaways

  • Cvent describes teasers as "short, high-energy clips" such as speaker prep, host-city footage or past attendee energy, made to build interest before registration.
  • Wistia groups promos and teasers under one minute.
  • A teaser is an open loop: it raises a question the event answers.
  • TikTok advises introducing your proposition within the first 3 seconds and the hook within 6.
  • Any scarcity claim ("only 50 tickets") must be true; the ASA has ruled a misleading "Only 14 tickets remaining" claim against an advertiser.

What a teaser is for

Cvent's guide to event videos puts it simply: "Before anyone registers or clicks on a calendar invite, they need a reason to care." The teaser's job is not to explain the event. It is to make someone want to know more, so they click, sign up for alerts or save the date.

That makes a teaser a pure open loop, a technique the Retention Editing Playbook uses inside videos: raise a question, delay the answer, and give the viewer a reason to keep going. In a teaser, the answer is the event itself.

A 30-second teaser structure

TimeBeatExample
0-3 sCold open: the most striking shotLast year's headline act walking into a roaring crowd
3-8 sThe tease: a line of text that raises a question"Last year was the warm-up."
8-22 sThree fast glimpses, never the full pictureSilhouette of a new stage, a speaker's hands on a prop, a blurred line-up poster
22-27 sThe reveal: date and place"Manchester. 14-15 June."
27-30 sThe action"Join the waiting list. Link in bio."

The cold open rule from the playbook applies: start on the most interesting thing you have. TikTok's creative best practices say to introduce your proposition within the first 3 seconds and prioritise the hook in the first 6.

What to film if it is your first event

No past footage? You still have options:

  • Speaker prep: a headline speaker recording a 10-second "I'll be there, and I'm going to show you something I've never shown before" (only if it's true).
  • The venue: dramatic shots of the empty space being built, lit or set up.
  • The host city: Cvent suggests host-city footage as a teaser ingredient.
  • The process: the team designing badges, testing the stage, printing posters.
  • Text-led motion graphics: a strong line, animated well, can carry a teaser on its own.

Avoid stock footage that looks like it is from your event. Viewers who attend will notice, and advertising rules require ads not to mislead.

Tease, don't confuse

The line between intriguing and confusing is thin. Three tests:

  1. Can a stranger tell what kind of event it is? Conference, festival, launch, gala. If not, add a word.
  2. Is there one clear date or "coming soon" moment? People need to know when to care.
  3. Is there one action? Waiting list, reminder or tickets. Not all three.

Honest scarcity and urgency

Teasers often lean on urgency: "Limited early-bird tickets." That is fine if it is true. The ASA's guidance on availability claims says marketers must make a reasonable estimate of demand (CAP Code rule 3.27) and must not falsely claim an offer is available only for a very limited time (rule 3.30). In one case it found "Only 14 tickets remaining" misleading because consumers would think most tickets were already sold. Use real numbers and real deadlines.

Example: a teaser for a second-year festival

Example: a small food and music festival launches tickets for its second year on 1 March.

  • Two weeks before (15 seconds, vertical): opens on last year's fireworks over the main stage, text "Year two is bigger.", a 1-second glimpse of a new tent, "1 March" and "Set a reminder".
  • One week before (25 seconds): opens on a chef's flame burst, three silhouetted shots of new stalls, "Early birds: first 500 tickets at £35" (the real allocation and price), "Waiting list in bio".
  • Launch day (20 seconds): "Tickets are live" in the first 2 seconds, then the best three shots from last year and the link.

Each teaser answers a little more of the question, so people who saw the first one have a reason to watch the next.

Measure and refine

If you run teasers as YouTube ads, remember viewers can skip after five seconds, according to Google Ads Help. Compare how many people watch past those first seconds for two or three different openings, keep the winner and cut a new version.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches open loops for short videos?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches open loops, cold opens and pacing for YouTube and Shorts. TikTok's free creative guide is a fair alternative.

How long should an event teaser be?

Usually 15 to 45 seconds. Wistia groups teasers with promos under one minute.

Should a teaser reveal the line-up?

Usually not all of it. Tease one name or a silhouette, and save the full reveal for launch day.

When should I post a teaser?

One to three weeks before tickets go on sale, with a final version on launch day.

Can I say tickets are limited?

Only if it is true, with a reasonable estimate of demand. The ASA has ruled against misleading ticket scarcity claims.

Do teasers need sound?

Use sound, but make sure the message works with it off, using on-screen text for the key line and date.
