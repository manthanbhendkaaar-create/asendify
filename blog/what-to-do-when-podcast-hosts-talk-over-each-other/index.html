What should a podcast editor do when two hosts talk over each other?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 7 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Keep the overlap that sounds like a real conversation, and fix the overlap that hides a point. Work on separate tracks per speaker, lower or trim the less important voice, and shift clips apart when two sentences collide. Loud or muddy collisions should be cut, because they cost you listeners and viewers.

Key takeaways

  • Descript's guide says to "let your speakers sound human, not like perfect robots", and also that loud overlap is "not worth startling your audience in the middle of their listen" (Descript).
  • Separate tracks matter most: "You can't do anything when people are in the same room talking on the same microphone" (Descript). Record one track per person where you can.
  • The Podcast Host suggests keeping the more important track at full level and fading or lowering the other rather than deleting it outright (The Podcast Host).
  • YouTube's retention report says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). A tangled passage is a likely place for one.

Why talking over each other is an editing decision, not a mistake

Crosstalk is what The Podcast Host calls "the parts of a multitrack session where all of the participants are speaking at the same time". Some of it is the best part of a conversation: a laugh, a quick "exactly!", an interruption that shows two people care about the point. Some of it is damage: two sentences arrive at once and the listener catches neither.

The editor's job is to sort one from the other. A video podcast adds a second question, because the viewer can see who is speaking. If the cut stays on the wrong face while the other host is mid-sentence, the overlap feels worse than it sounds.

The Podcast Host adds a useful check: listen to tone, because the same "right, right, right" can be enthusiastic or bored. It also says to watch the waveform, where bigger shapes tend to show energy and smaller ones a lack of it.

Step-by-step: a clean crosstalk pass

  1. Check how it was recorded. If you have a file per speaker, the fix is easy. If both voices share one microphone, you can only cut or leave, so decide earlier in the edit how much you will tolerate.
  2. Group the tracks. Descript's advice is to group tracks before editing so a cut on one applies to the others and everything stays in sync.
  3. Do the crosstalk pass separately from the content pass. The Podcast Host notes this keeps decisions about overlap apart from decisions about what the episode says. For a 60-minute interview with two tracks, the author says the pass "usually takes me about 20 minutes".
  4. Mark each overlap as keep, lower or fix. Use the table above. Be quick; you are sorting, not polishing yet.
  5. For backchannel sounds, trim the quieter track. Descript calls this "your best bet" for words such as "uh huh".
  6. For two real sentences colliding, move clips. Cut at the end of the first speaker's sentence and the start of the second's, then shift the later clips so they no longer overlap. Descript's example moves clips by two seconds and warns that being off by "a hundredth of a second" can sound strange, so keep tracks grouped.
  7. Lower, do not erase, the less important voice. The Podcast Host's advice is to fade out a laugh briefly, then lower it, rather than silencing it. Completely removing it can sound odd.
  8. Fill the gaps. If you cut a cough or a bump, lay in a little room tone so the silence does not sound like a hole.
  9. Choose the picture to match. In a video podcast, cut to the host who is carrying the point. Where both speak, a two-shot or split view avoids a flicker between faces.
  10. Listen on phone speakers and headphones. Overlap that is tolerable on studio monitors can be muddy on a small speaker.
  11. Review the retention graph after publishing and look for dips at the places you edited.

What about captions?

If you caption the episode, overlap needs a decision too. The W3C says captions should "identify who is speaking and include non-speech information conveyed through sound, including meaningful sound effects" (W3C). Where two people speak at once, caption the line that matters and label the speaker, rather than stacking both on screen. If a laugh matters to the moment, say so in the caption.

Example: a 40-minute two-host episode

Example: two hosts record on separate tracks over a video call. In the first draft, the editor leaves everything as spoken: there are around a dozen places where the hosts start the same sentence together, plus a run of repeated "yeah, yeah" from one host during the other's long answer. The second draft sorts each into the table's categories. The "yeah" run is trimmed from the quieter track. Three real collisions are fixed by moving the second host's sentence two to three seconds later. One laugh is kept at lower level under the main voice. A clean overlap during a joke is left alone because it sounds natural. The editor posts the second draft and checks the retention graph for dips near those moments. When to leave it alone

Not every overlap needs a fix. If the audience can follow both lines, or the interruption is the point, over-editing makes the show sound staged. A good test is to ask whether the cut would change what the listener understands or feels. If not, leave it.

If the overlap is part of a tense disagreement, keep enough of it that the exchange stays honest. Do not trim a speaker's words in a way that changes their meaning. When in doubt, ask the host.

If you record the next episode differently

You will save the most time at the recording stage. Ask each host to record their own track, to leave a short beat before replying and to keep acknowledgements like "mm-hmm" quiet or silent when the other person is making a point. A short note before recording about not talking over each other helps more than a long edit later.

Should I remove every interruption?

No. Short, warm overlap keeps the conversation human. Cut or move the overlap that hides a point or startles the listener.

Can I fix overlap if we recorded on one microphone?

Only by cutting or leaving it. Descript notes you cannot separate voices on one shared microphone, so record separate tracks next time.

How long does a crosstalk pass take?

The Podcast Host says about 20 minutes for a 60-minute, two-track interview. Yours will vary.

Do captions need to show both speakers at once?

No. Caption the line that matters and identify the speaker, as the W3C guidance on captions suggests.
