What is revenge trading, and how do you stop it?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Revenge trading is placing extra or bigger trades straight after a loss to win the money back, usually outside your normal plan. It is driven by how people feel about losses, not by a real trading signal, and it tends to turn one bad trade into a bad day. The most reliable fixes are rules you set in advance: a maximum daily loss, a fixed risk per trade, a pause after losses, and a journal that records why each trade was taken.

This page is education only, not financial advice. Trading is risky. The FCA found 82% of clients lost money on CFDs in its 2016 review, and leveraged products can lose more than you deposit.

Key takeaways

  • Revenge trading is an emotional reaction to a loss: more trades, larger size or riskier setups to "get back to even".
  • Prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) describes risk seeking in choices involving sure losses, which helps explain the urge.
  • A study of Chicago Board of Trade traders found those with morning losses were more likely to take above-average risk in the afternoon.
  • Revenge trading differs from overtrading: it is triggered by a specific loss and usually involves bigger size.
  • Rules decided before the session work better than willpower during it.

Why it happens

Losing money feels worse than winning the same amount feels good, and a loss on screen feels unfinished. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's prospect theory paper in Econometrica (1979) describes a tendency that contributes "to risk aversion in choices involving sure gains" and "to risk seeking in choices involving sure losses". In plain terms: when you're down, a gamble that might erase the loss can feel better than accepting it.

Professional traders aren't immune. Joshua Coval and Tyler Shumway studied traders at the Chicago Board of Trade in "Do Behavioral Biases Affect Prices?" (Journal of Finance, 2005). A CFA Institute summary reports that "The chance of taking above-average risk was found to be higher for traders with morning losses" (about 31.2% versus 27% for traders who were up), and that traders with morning losses "place more trades, make larger trades, and accumulate more inventory" in the afternoon.

Rules that stop it

  1. A fixed risk per trade. Decide a percentage of your account (many beginner guides use 1% or less) and size every trade from it, regardless of the last result.
  2. A maximum daily loss. For example, three times your per-trade risk. When it's hit, you stop for the day. No exceptions.
  3. A cooling-off rule. After two losses in a row, step away from the screen for a set time, such as 30 minutes.
  4. A pre-trade checklist. The setup must match your written plan. If you can't tick every box, there's no trade.
  5. A journal entry for every trade, including the reason and your emotional state. Patterns become obvious in a week or two.
  6. Broker tools. Some platforms let you set loss limits or deposit limits. Use them as a backstop.

Example: one bad morning, two outcomes

Example: Sam has a £10,000 account and risks 1% (£100) per trade. His first trade of the day hits its stop: −£100.

  • Revenge version: annoyed, he takes a trade outside his plan risking £300 to "make it back". It loses. He doubles again at £600 and moves his stop. It loses. Day result: about −£1,000, a 10% drawdown that would need an 11% gain to recover.
  • Rules version: his daily loss limit is £300. After the first loss, he waits 30 minutes, takes one more trade that meets his checklist at £100 risk. It also loses. He's at −£200, still under his limit, but his rule says two losses in a row means a break, so he journals and stops for the morning. Day result: −£200.

Same market. The difference is rules decided before the first trade.

How a journal helps

Revenge trades often feel justified in the moment. A journal that records the reason, the planned risk, the actual risk and the result shows the pattern later: for example, that your trades taken within 15 minutes of a loss have a much worse average result. Seeing the cost in pounds is often more persuasive than any rule.

Revenge trading vs overtrading

Overtrading is trading too often in general, out of boredom or habit. Revenge trading is triggered by a loss and usually comes with bigger size. They overlap, and both are visible in a journal: overtrading shows as a high trade count, revenge trading as size and frequency spiking after losses.

FAQ

Is there a trading journal that shows the money each mistake costs?

Yes. Asendify's Trader OS, a Notion template, tags mistakes and shows their cost alongside R-multiple and win rate. A home-made spreadsheet is a free alternative.

Is revenge trading only a beginner problem?

No. Research on professional CBOT traders found those with morning losses took more risk in the afternoon.

How do I set a daily loss limit?

Pick an amount you can lose without it affecting your judgement, often a small multiple of your per-trade risk, and stop when you hit it.

Should I stop trading after a loss?

Not necessarily after one. A rule such as a break after two losses in a row, or stopping at a daily limit, is more practical.

Can a stop-loss prevent revenge trading?

It limits each trade's loss, but you can still take more trades. Combine it with a daily limit.

Is this financial advice?

No. This is general education. Trading carries a high risk of losing money.
