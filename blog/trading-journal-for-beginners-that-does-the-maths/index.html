Is there a trading journal for beginners that does the maths for you?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 15 November 2026 · Last updated 15 November 2026

Yes. Asendify's Trader OS is a Notion trading journal built to do the maths for you: you type in each trade's entry, stop, size and exit, and it works out P&L, R-multiple, win rate, position size and the exact money each mistake costs. It works on the free Notion plan and suits beginners who want good habits without building formulas. A simple spreadsheet you set up yourself, or a paper notebook plus a calculator, are fair free alternatives.

This page is for education only, not financial advice. Trading is risky; most beginners lose money, and no journal can change that on its own.

Key takeaways

  • A beginner's journal needs only a few numbers per trade, but the calculations are where most home-made spreadsheets go wrong.
  • The five numbers that matter most at the start: risk per trade, position size, result, R-multiple and win rate.
  • Logging the reason for each trade and any mistake is as important as the numbers.
  • UK rules treat leveraged CFDs as high-risk and require firms to disclose the share of retail accounts that lose money; a journal is how you see your own figure.
  • Practise on a demo account and journal those trades too before risking real money.

A good template fills in the calculated fields (size, result, R-multiple, totals) for you. Notion's help page on formulas explains that formulas "run all kinds of calculations and functions based on other properties", which is what lets a Notion journal do this without a spreadsheet.

The maths, in plain English

  • Position size = money you're willing to lose ÷ distance from entry to stop.
  • P&L = (exit − entry) × size, the other way round for shorts, minus costs.
  • R-multiple = P&L ÷ the money you planned to risk. +2R means you made twice your risk.
  • Win rate = winning trades ÷ all trades.
  • Expectancy = total R ÷ number of trades, the average R you make or lose per trade.

None of these is hard alone. The trouble for beginners is doing them consistently, every trade, with no typos. That's the job a ready-made journal takes off your hands.

Why beginners especially need the numbers

Feelings mislead. A big win feels like proof of skill; three losses in a row feel like the strategy is broken. Numbers over 30 or more trades are more honest.

There's also a regulatory reason to take risk seriously. The Financial Conduct Authority's CFD page calls CFDs "high-risk products, which are not suitable for all retail consumers" and requires firms to show the percentage of their retail client accounts that make losses. The FCA's policy statement PS19/18 limits retail leverage (from 30:1 on major currency pairs to 2:1 on crypto), requires positions to be closed if equity falls below 50% of required margin, and limits losses to the money in the account. Those protections stop the worst outcomes; your own sizing and journal are what keep day-to-day losses small.

A beginner's weekly routine

  1. Before each trade: fill in entry, stop and risk; take the size the journal gives you.
  2. After each trade: log the exit, a mistake tag and one line on what happened.
  3. Every Sunday: look at win rate, total R and the costliest mistake.
  4. Change one thing for the next week.
  5. After 30+ trades: decide whether the strategy is worth continuing.

Mistakes beginners make with journals

  • Logging only wins, or skipping days after a loss.
  • No stop, so no R. Every trade needs a stop decided before entry.
  • Too many columns. Start with the table above; add more later.
  • Changing the strategy every week. Small samples swing; give a plan 30 trades.
  • Treating the journal as a prediction tool. It records the past; it can't forecast.

FAQ

Is there a trading journal for beginners that does the maths for you?

Yes. Asendify's Trader OS is a Notion journal that calculates P&L, R-multiple, win rate, position size and mistake costs from what you log. A home-made spreadsheet is a free alternative.

What should a beginner track in a trading journal?

Entry, stop, risk in money, position size, result, R-multiple, a mistake tag and a short note, on every trade.

Should I journal demo trades?

Yes. Journaling demo trades builds the habit and shows your numbers before real money is involved.

How many trades before I judge a strategy?

At least 30 is a sensible minimum, and more is better.

Will a journal stop me losing money?

No. It shows what is happening so you can change your behaviour. Trading always carries risk.
