How do I make the first 3 seconds of a video ad stop people scrolling?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 21 November 2026 · Last updated 21 November 2026

To stop people scrolling, the first 3 seconds of a video ad must start on the most interesting thing in it: a face mid-sentence, a surprising number on screen, or the product doing something, with on-screen text that states the one outcome the viewer gets. No logo sting, no greeting, no slow wide shot. Google's own guidance for YouTube ads is to "get to the heart of the story faster", and the same rule holds in Facebook and Instagram feeds.

Why the first 3 seconds matter so much in ads

In a feed, nobody chose to watch your ad. They were scrolling past it on the way to something else, so the opening has one job: earn the next few seconds. On YouTube, skippable in-stream ads can be skipped after five seconds, according to Google Ads Help, so your first 3 seconds are most of the time you are guaranteed.

Google's ABCDs of effective video ads put Attention first and list three habits: jump in, support the story with audio and supers (on-screen text), and keep visuals bright and high-contrast. Google says that applying the ABCDs can deliver as much as a 30% lift in short-term sales likelihood, citing a 2021 Google/Kantar study. That is Google's figure for its own framework, not a promise for any single ad.

The cold open rule, applied to ads

The Retention Editing Playbook's rule for YouTube intros is the cold open: the video starts on the most interesting thing in it. "Not a logo. Not a greeting. Not a wide shot of the host settling in." If the best moment happens later, move it to 0:00. In an ad, that usually means one of these first frames:

  • The result. The finished room, the fixed graph, the before-and-after. Show the end first, explain after.
  • The specific number. A concrete, checkable figure on screen. Only use a real one you can back up.
  • The contradiction. One line that disagrees with what your buyer believes ("Longer ads aren't your problem. Your first line is.").
  • The reveal in progress. Open mid-action, so the viewer has to stay to understand what they are seeing.
  • Their exact question. The words your buyer would type into Google, on screen, in their language.

Rotate between these across ads so the opening doesn't become predictable to your audience.

A 3-second structure that works

Break the first 3 seconds into three tiny beats:

  1. 0.0 to 1.0 s: the visual hook. Movement or a face from the first frame. Something is already happening.
  2. 1.0 to 2.0 s: the text promise. On-screen text, three to seven words, naming the outcome or the problem. Many people watch feeds with the sound off, so the text has to carry the message without audio.
  3. 2.0 to 3.0 s: the open loop. A line that raises a question the ad will answer ("Here's the one change that fixed it."). An open loop gives the viewer a reason to keep watching.

Then keep the promise quickly. The playbook's intro structure is Promise (what they get), Proof (show it is real), Path (how you'll get there, ending on a loop). In a 15-second ad those beats compress to roughly 3, 5 and 4 seconds, with the call to action at the end.

Seven things to cut from the first 3 seconds

These come straight from the playbook's list of intro killers, adapted for ads:

  • A logo animation or sting of any length
  • "Hey guys", "welcome", or your company name spoken first
  • Any sentence that describes the ad instead of starting it ("In this video...")
  • Music that starts before anything happens on screen
  • A wide, static shot with no on-screen text for more than a second or two
  • A promise vaguer than the headline ("better marketing")
  • Small, low-contrast text that can't be read on a phone

Note the tension with branding: Google advises showing your brand "early and throughout". You can do both by putting the product or a subtle brand mark inside the hook frame, rather than in a separate logo intro.

Test hooks, not whole ads

The cheapest way to improve an ad is to keep the body and swap the first 3 seconds. Make three to five versions that differ only in the opening (result, number, contradiction, question), run them with the same audience and budget, and compare how many people keep watching past the first few seconds and how many click. Ads Manager and Google Ads both report video watch metrics; judge on enough impressions to be meaningful, not the first few hundred.

When one opening wins, ask why. Usually it is the one that most clearly matched what the viewer already wanted.

How the Retention Editing Playbook helps

Key takeaways

  • Start on the most interesting moment; move it to 0:00 if you have to.
  • Use on-screen text so the hook works with the sound off.
  • Open a loop by the third second so there is a reason to stay.
  • Cut logo intros, greetings and slow wide shots.
  • Test new openings on the same ad body, one variable at a time.

FAQ

How long is the hook in a video ad?

Treat the first 3 seconds as the hook. On YouTube, skippable ads can be skipped after five seconds, so the opening has to earn attention before the skip button appears.

Should my logo appear in the first 3 seconds?

Google recommends branding early, but a separate logo intro wastes the hook. Show the product or a small brand mark inside an interesting first frame instead.

What is the best first line for a video ad?

There is no single best line. Strong openings show the result, state a specific real number, contradict a common belief, or repeat the viewer's exact question. Test several against each other.
