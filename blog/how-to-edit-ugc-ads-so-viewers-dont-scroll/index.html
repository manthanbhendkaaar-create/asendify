How do I edit UGC-style ads so viewers don't scroll past?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Edit a UGC-style ad so viewers don't scroll past by opening on the creator's strongest line mid-sentence, cutting every pause and repeat, showing the product within the first few seconds, adding short on-screen text that works with sound off, and ending on one clear call to action. Keep the creator's natural style; over-polishing removes the reason UGC works. And keep it honest: paid creator content must be clearly labelled as an ad.

Key takeaways

  • Start on the best line, not the creator's "hi guys". Move it to 0:00 if you have to.
  • TikTok's guidance favours a DIY, not overly polished style featuring creators, employees or customers.
  • Keep text short: TikTok recommends 5 to 10 words per second on screen.
  • Cut breaths, false starts and repeated points; jump cuts are normal in this format.
  • In the UK, both the brand and the creator are responsible for making a paid ad obviously identifiable.

Why UGC ads lose viewers

Raw creator footage usually arrives as a long, friendly ramble. The best line might be at 0:41. The product might not appear until halfway. The creator says "so, um, I've been using this for a while" before anything happens. In a feed, that is three seconds of nothing, and most people are gone.

The fix is not to make it look like a TV commercial. TikTok's creative best practices recommend featuring creators, employees or customers and favouring a DIY, not overly polished style. The editing job is to keep that natural feel while removing everything that delays the point.

The hook

Scrub the raw footage for three candidate openings: a surprising statement, a strong reaction, or the problem in the creator's own words. TikTok advises putting the hook in the first 6 seconds and introducing the proposition in the first 3. Research reported by Harvard Business School's Working Knowledge found surprise was most effective at capturing attention in online video ads and joy best at holding it, which is why a genuine reaction often beats a scripted line.

Pacing

Cut every pause, "um" and repeated sentence. Jump cuts are expected in creator content, so you don't need B-roll to hide every cut. Add a punch-in (a slight zoom) when the creator moves to a new point, so each cut feels intentional.

Text and captions

Most people scroll with the sound low or off. Add captions and a short headline for the hook. TikTok recommends displaying 5 to 10 words per second when you use text, and keeping content within the UI safe zone. Check auto-captions line by line; brand names are often misspelt.

Product and proof

Get the product on screen early and in use. A creator holding a box is weaker than a creator using it. Keep proof specific ("my skin felt less tight after a week" is a personal experience; "cures eczema" is a claim the brand would need evidence for).

Keep it legal and honest

UGC ads sit close to reviews, so the rules matter.

  • Label it. The ASA's guidance on recognising ads on social media says paid content should carry a prominent "ad" label upfront, and that both brands and influencers are responsible for making ads obviously identifiable.
  • Genuine testimonials only. The ASA's testimonials guidance says advertisers must hold documentary evidence that a testimonial is genuine, must have permission to use it, and must not let claims within it mislead.
  • No hidden incentives. Since April 2025, fake reviews and concealed incentivised reviews are a banned practice under UK consumer law, as set out in the government's online reviews and endorsements guidance for businesses.

Never cut a creator's words so they say something they didn't mean, and never script a "customer" review for an actor while implying it is real.

Worked example

Example: a creator sends 2 minutes 40 seconds of footage reviewing a reusable water bottle for a client. Her first line is "Hey everyone, so today I want to talk about..." The best moment is at 1:52, when she drops the bottle down the stairs, picks it up and says "Not a dent. I was not expecting that."

The editor opens on the drop and the line, with the text "Dropped it down the stairs". Second 3: her problem line ("I've cracked three bottles this year"). Seconds 6 to 12: close-ups of the bottle in her bag and at the gym. Seconds 12 to 17: the clip of her filling it with ice and it still being cold at the end of the day, which the brand's product page supports. Last 3 seconds: "Link below" said and on an end card. The "Ad" label sits on the post. The final cut is 20 seconds, and nothing she said has been changed in meaning.

FAQ

Is there a guide to editing UGC ads for retention?

Yes. Start with TikTok's free creative best practices, and use a system such as Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook if you want shared hook and pacing rules for a team.

How long should a UGC ad be?

Long enough to make one point well. Many run 15 to 30 seconds, but test lengths rather than following a rule; TikTok's in-feed ads can run much longer.

Should I add music to UGC ads?

Light music under speech can help, but keep the creator's voice clear and use only properly licensed tracks.

Can I edit what a creator says?

You can shorten and reorder, but don't change the meaning or create claims they didn't make. Get the creator's approval on the final cut.

Do UGC ads need an "ad" label?

If the creator was paid or given an incentive, or the brand controls the content, yes. The ASA expects a prominent label upfront.
