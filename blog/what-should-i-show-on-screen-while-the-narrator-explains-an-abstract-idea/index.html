What should I show on screen while the narrator explains an abstract idea?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Show something concrete that the narration points at: one everyday example, a simple diagram that builds step by step, or a side-by-side comparison. Keep any on-screen words to a few key terms instead of repeating the narration. An abstract idea is hard because the viewer has nothing to hold; your visuals give them something to hold.

Key takeaways

  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded, rather than included" (Dartmouth).
  • The signalling principle says "people learn better when there are cues that emphasize information that is more important" (same page).
  • The redundancy principle warns that "graphics, voiceover, and on-screen text can be overwhelming for learners" (same page).
  • Spatial contiguity says "learning is more effective when corresponding images and text are placed close together, rather than far apart" (same page).
  • YouTube's retention report says dips "mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video", so a dip during an abstract passage is a prompt to change the visual (YouTube Help).

Why abstract ideas need a visual anchor

Words like "opportunity cost", "entropy" or "network effects" name things nobody can see. A narrator can define them, but the viewer is still staring at a screen, and if the screen shows a stock clip of a person typing, it carries no information. Attention drifts because the eyes have nothing to do with the ears' work.

The answer is not more decoration. It is a visual that does a job: it makes one part of the idea visible, at the moment the narrator talks about it.

Step 1: Translate the idea into one concrete case

Before choosing visuals, write the abstract idea as a single concrete example the viewer already knows. Examples:

Abstract ideaConcrete case to show
Opportunity costTwo plates of food; taking one means leaving the other
CompoundingA small stack of coins that grows as the years tick by
Network effectsOne phone with no contacts, then a phone with many
Supply and demandA market stall where the price on the sign changes as the queue grows
InflationThe same basket of items with a price tag that changes
Feedback loopA thermostat and a room temperature graph

These are teaching illustrations, not data. If you use a real number, source it and date it.

Step 2: Choose the visual type that fits the idea

Not every idea needs the same device. Match the structure.

  1. Process or cause and effect → a diagram that builds one arrow at a time.
  2. Comparison → two panels side by side with one difference highlighted.
  3. Change over time → a simple chart or a sequence of frames, with the axis labelled.
  4. Scale or size → a familiar object next to the thing being described.
  5. Relationship → a labelled map or sketch of how pieces connect.
  6. Quantity → a small number of icons, not a crowded grid.

Whichever you choose, one idea per shot. If the visual needs three seconds of reading before it makes sense, it is too complicated for the pace of narration.

Step 3: Build the visual in step with the voice

The biggest mistake is showing the finished diagram while the narrator is still on the first sentence. The viewer reads ahead and stops listening. Instead:

  • Introduce each element as the narrator names it. A box appears when the word is said.
  • Use the signalling principle. Highlight, circle or colour the element being discussed; fade it back once the point has been made.
  • Keep text next to what it labels. Spatial contiguity says text and its image should sit close together.
  • Avoid repeating the narration as text. The redundancy principle warns that combining graphics, voiceover and on-screen text can overwhelm. Use a short label or a key term, not a transcript. Captions for accessibility are a separate matter and serve viewers who cannot hear or choose not to; keep them in the standard caption position.

Step 4: Remove what does not help

Coherence is the principle that does the most work for editors. Ask of each shot: does it help the viewer understand this sentence? If not, cut it or replace it.

  • Remove decorative stock footage that only illustrates a general mood.
  • Remove background music that competes with a difficult sentence. If music stays, keep it quiet.
  • Remove animated flourishes that move for no reason.
  • Remove extra labels that repeat what the narrator said.

This is not a call for minimalism. A good abstract explainer has a lot going on, but every element earns its place.

Step 5: Add a re-orienting beat every so often

On long abstract passages, give the eye a change at natural points: a new visual, a zoom, a different diagram, or a summary frame. This is the standard pattern interrupt in retention editing. Do it when the idea changes, not at a fixed timer, so the change helps the viewer follow the structure.

Step 6: Check the retention graph

After publishing, find the abstract passage on the audience retention chart. YouTube describes dips as moments where "viewers are abandoning or skipping" and suggests reviewing them to understand why audiences lost interest. If the abstract section shows a dip, try one change: a more concrete example, a simpler diagram, or a shorter explanation. Compare on the next video with a similar structure.

Example: explaining opportunity cost

Example: an explainer script says, "Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you give up when you make a choice." Version A shows a stock clip of a person at a desk with the definition typed across the screen. Version B shows two coffee cups on a table; when the narrator says "the best alternative", one cup glows and the other fades; a small label reads "what you give up". Then a second example appears: an hour of overtime versus an hour with family. Version B uses fewer words on screen, ties each visual to a phrase, and uses two examples. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

ElementVersion AVersion B
VisualStock clipTwo objects, one highlighted
On-screen textFull definitionTwo-word label
Link to narrationNoneHighlight on key phrase
ExamplesOne abstractTwo concrete

Step 7: Keep claims honest

If your abstract idea touches money, health or science, do not make the visual stronger than the evidence. A tidy graph can imply a causal link that the research does not support. Label sources, show scale truthfully and avoid cropped axes that exaggerate a trend. Honest visuals build trust, which is the base of retention over a whole channel.

Common mistakes

  • Showing the whole diagram at once. The viewer reads ahead.
  • Repeating the narration as text. It adds load, not clarity.
  • Stock footage that adds nothing. It looks like content but teaches nothing.
  • Visuals that appear late. Show the image when the word is said.
  • Too many ideas per shot. One shot, one idea.
  • Using abstract visuals for abstract ideas. Use concrete ones.

FAQ

Should the screen show the same words the narrator says?

Usually not. The redundancy principle warns that voiceover, graphics and identical on-screen text can overwhelm. Use short labels instead.

Is stock footage fine for abstract ideas?

Only if it shows something relevant. Decorative footage fails the coherence test.

How often should the visual change?

Change it when the idea changes, not on a timer.

How do I know the visual is working?

Look at the retention graph for that passage and compare dips across similar videos.
