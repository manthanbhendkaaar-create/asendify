How do I use text on screen in a LinkedIn video?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Use on-screen text for three things: a short hook line in the first seconds, labels for the key terms and numbers you speak about, and a single clear line at the end. Keep each piece large, brief and inside the safe margins, make sure it appears as the point is spoken, and do not paste the whole script onto the screen. Pair it with a proper caption track if your audience may be watching without sound.

Key takeaways

  • Mayer's redundancy principle says "people learn better using on-screen graphics with voiceover", and that adding on-screen text on top of both can overwhelm learners (Dartmouth). Text should add to the voice, not echo it.
  • His signalling principle says "people learn better when there are cues that emphasize information that is more important" (same source). Short labels on key points are cues.
  • The W3C describes captions as a "synchronized visual and/or text alternative for both speech and non-speech audio information" (W3C WCAG 2.2). Captions and on-screen text labels do different jobs.
  • TikTok's guidance suggests "5-10 words per second when using text" (TikTok for Business). It is written for short ads, not LinkedIn, but is a useful ceiling for reading comfort.
  • LinkedIn's help pages could not be opened by my tools, so this page makes no claim about LinkedIn's own text or caption guidance. Check the platform's current options and your analytics.

Text, captions or both?

TypeJobBest for
CaptionsShow what is said, for sound-off viewers and accessibilityTalking-head posts, interviews, tips
On-screen textAdd what the voice does not: hook, labels, numbers, actionAll video types
BothCover speech and emphasise pointsMost professional video

On a professional feed, many people watch in public or at work without sound, so captions matter. But captions alone can look flat; selective on-screen text adds structure.

Where to use on-screen text

  1. The hook line (first 1 to 3 seconds). A few words that state the topic or question, so a viewer who scrolls past sees what it is about.
  2. Key terms and labels. When you introduce a framework, name or step, show the label.
  3. Numbers and short lists. A figure or three-item list is easier to take in visually.
  4. Speaker identification. Name and role in a lower third for the first appearance.
  5. The final line. One clear takeaway or next step, held long enough to read.

Design rules

  1. Few words per piece. Roughly three to eight words for a hook or label is a practice guide.
  2. Large size. Legible on a phone without zooming.
  3. High contrast. Use a solid or semi-transparent background if the footage is busy.
  4. Safe margins. Keep text away from the edges and from the area covered by the platform's controls and the captions.
  5. Consistent style. One font, one or two colours and one animation style.
  6. One piece at a time. Do not stack lines or overlap with captions.
  7. Timing. Text should arrive as the idea is spoken and leave when it ends; hold long enough to read.
  8. Check for accuracy. Names, numbers and spellings must be right; errors on a professional feed cost credibility.
  9. Do not hide the speaker's face. Place text above or below.
  10. Test on a phone. Check on a small screen, in the actual app.

Example: a founder's 60-second tip

Example: a founder records a 60-second tip about hiring. Version one has captions word by word in large text, a title card with her company logo for the first three seconds and several animated stickers. Version two opens with a hook line, "Hiring mistake #1", while she is already speaking, uses captions in the lower middle, shows a short label when she names the "three-question test", displays "3 questions" as a list as she says it, and ends with one line: "Save this for your next hire". This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result. She compares views, viewers and watch time for both videos in her analytics.

Mixing captions with other text

If you use burned-in captions and on-screen labels together, plan the layout:

  • Captions in the lower middle, labels in the upper third.
  • Move captions up if a lower-third name tag appears.
  • Avoid animation on both at the same time.

If you also upload a caption file where the platform supports it, check how it interacts with burned-in text so words do not appear twice. Whether the platform lets you upload a separate caption file is for you to confirm in its current interface.

Measuring the effect

LinkedIn's API documentation, which I could open, defines a video view as a play of "at least 3 seconds" and lists time watched separately (Microsoft Learn). To test text, make two versions that differ only in text treatment, publish them at comparable times, and compare views, viewers and watch time per view as a share of video length. Do not conclude from one pair; repeat on another topic.

Mistakes

  • Whole script on screen.
  • Tiny or low-contrast text.
  • Text over the speaker's face.
  • Captions and labels colliding.
  • Typos in names or numbers.
  • Holding text too briefly to read.
  • Fancy animation that distracts from the message.

Checklist

  • Hook line in the first seconds.
  • Labels for key terms and numbers.
  • One closing line.
  • Large, high contrast, inside margins.
  • Captions and labels do not overlap.
  • Tested on a phone with sound off.

Keeping a simple log

Whatever you change, write it down. A table with the date, the video, the single change you made, the metric you watched and what happened takes two minutes to update and saves a great deal of guessing later. Include a column for what else was different, such as a bigger topic, a holiday week or a different upload time, because those confound results. After five or six entries you will see which changes helped repeatedly and which only looked good once. Treat any single result as a hint rather than proof, and only promote a change to your standard routine once it has held up on more than one video.
