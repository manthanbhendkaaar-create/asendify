How do I give feedback on a video ad edit that an editor can act on?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Give feedback on a video ad edit that an editor can act on by making every note timecoded, linked to the brief, focused on the problem the viewer would feel rather than a vague taste, and sorted into must-fix and nice-to-have. Watch the cut twice first, once as a viewer scrolling past and once muted. Consolidate everyone's notes into one list before sending, so the editor never has to choose between two people.

Key takeaways

  • "It doesn't pop" is not feedback. "At 0:02 the offer isn't clear yet" is.
  • Link each note to the brief: the viewer, the promise or the call to action.
  • Describe the problem; let the editor propose the fix unless you have a specific one.
  • One consolidated list per round. Conflicting notes from different people cost a whole round.
  • Only 30% of agencies say they receive clear, constructive feedback, so this is a common gap.

Why feedback is where ads lose time

The gap between what clients think they give and what agencies receive is wide. In the BetterIdeas Project by the IPA, WFA, BetterBriefs and Flood + Partners, reported by Marketing Week, 52% of marketers believed they give clear, constructive feedback but only 30% of agencies agreed. The earlier BetterBriefs study found that 69% of marketers and 73% of agencies agree rebriefs happen too often.

Inside an agency, the same pattern plays out between account managers and editors. Vague notes lead to guesses, guesses lead to another round, and the ad launches late with less time to test.

How to watch a cut before giving notes

  1. Watch once at full speed, as a viewer. Don't pause. Note the moment you would have scrolled away.
  2. Watch once muted. Most feeds play with the sound off; if the message disappears without audio, that is note number one.
  3. Check the first 3 seconds against the brief. TikTok's creative best practices recommend the proposition in the first 3 seconds and the hook in the first 6.
  4. Check the close. Is the call to action both said and shown? Google's ABCDs of effective video ads recommend reinforcing an on-screen call to action with voice-over.
  5. Only then go frame by frame for typos, colour, logos and safe zones.

The right-hand version tells the editor where, what and why. It also makes it easier to push back politely if the client asks for something that would hurt the ad.

Use timecoded comments

Email lists of notes drift out of sync with the cut. Review tools tie each note to the exact moment. In Frame.io, for example, a comment is posted with a marker at its timecode, can cover a range of frames using in and out points, and can include arrows, boxes or freehand drawing on the frame. Whatever tool you use, the rule is the same: one note, one timecode, one problem.

Sort notes by priority

  • Must fix: anything that breaks the brief, the law or the brand rules. Wrong price, missing label, offer unclear, call to action missing.
  • Should fix: anything that probably costs attention. A slow beat, a weak proof shot, text on screen too briefly.
  • Nice to have: taste. A different music bed, a colour tweak.

Label each note. Editors can then do the must-fixes first and ask about the rest.

Consolidate before you send

If the client's marketing lead, founder and legal team all comment, the account manager should merge the notes into one list, resolve conflicts with the client, and send one version. Two contradictory notes ("make it shorter" and "add the second testimonial") leave the editor guessing and waste a round.

Worked example

Example: a client reviews a 15-second ad for a cycle-to-work scheme and sends: "Love it but it needs more energy, and can we make it feel more premium? Also the end is a bit flat."

The account manager rewrites it before passing it on:

  1. Must fix, 0:13-0:15: the call to action "Check your eligibility" is spoken but not on screen. Add an end card with the same words.
  2. Should fix, 0:00-0:01: the opening shot is a static office. "More energy" here means starting on the bike in motion; swap with the 0:05 shot.
  3. Should fix, 0:06-0:09: the music bed is quieter than the voice-over drop at 0:06. Even out the mix.
  4. Nice to have, throughout: "more premium" could mean slower, cleaner text animation. Editor to suggest one option.

The editor now has four jobs, in order, each with a timecode and a reason. The revision comes back in one round.

FAQ

Is there a framework for giving feedback on video ads?

Yes: watch twice, check the first 3 seconds and the close against the brief, then give timecoded notes sorted by priority. A shared system such as Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook gives everyone the same terms.

Should I tell the editor how to fix it?

Describe the problem first. If you have a specific fix, add it as a suggestion, but let the editor offer a better one.

How many people should review an ad?

As few as possible, with one person responsible for merging notes. More reviewers usually mean more conflicting notes.

What if the client's feedback will hurt retention?

Explain the risk with the brief and the data, offer to test both versions, and let the client decide.

How many rounds of feedback are normal?

Agree a number at the start, often two. Clear, consolidated notes are the best way to stay within it.
