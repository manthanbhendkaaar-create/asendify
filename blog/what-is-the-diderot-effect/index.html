What is the Diderot effect, and how do you stop one purchase leading to many?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The Diderot effect is the pull to keep buying after one new, better possession makes the things around it look out of place. It is named after the French writer Denis Diderot, whose 1769 essay describes how a gift of a fine scarlet dressing gown led him to replace almost everything in his study, and left him less content. The anthropologist Grant McCracken brought the idea into consumer research in 1988. The fix is mostly about noticing the chain before it starts and deciding in advance what a purchase is allowed to change.

This page is general education, not financial advice. Only spend money you can genuinely spare on anything bought mainly for status.

Key takeaways

  • Diderot wrote "Regrets on Parting with My Old Dressing Gown" in 1769; the Goethe-Institut notes it followed Catherine the Great buying his library in 1765.
  • In the essay, the new robe made his old desk, chair and wall hangings look shabby, so he replaced them one by one. He writes: "All is now discordant. No more coordination, no more unity, no more beauty."
  • McCracken discusses the effect in his 1988 book on culture and consumption, as summarised in the SAGE Encyclopedia of Consumer Culture: people tend to keep their possessions consistent with each other, so one upgrade can trigger others.
  • McCracken also points out the more common response is the reverse: people often reject a new item because it would not fit with what they already own.
  • Status purchases are especially prone to it, because their point is how they look next to everything else.

The original story

Diderot had been short of money for much of his life. After Catherine the Great bought his library and paid him to look after it, he received a luxurious scarlet dressing gown. His old gown had been shabby but comfortable, and it suited the cluttered study he worked in.

The new one did not. Next to it, his straw chair, worn tapestry and cheap shelving looked wrong, so he replaced them. Then the new furniture needed better objects around it. By the end, he describes himself as a slave to the new robe and says, in the line most often quoted from the essay, "Poverty has its freedoms; opulence has its obstacles." The essay is a regret, not a boast.

How the effect works

McCracken's point, as the SAGE entry explains, is that we don't value goods one at a time. We build "consumption constellations": sets of things that go together and say something consistent about who we are. A new item that is far above the level of the rest breaks the set, and there are only two ways to restore harmony:

ResponseWhat happensExample
Upgrade the restYou buy to match the new itemNew sofa, then new rug, lamps and curtains
Reject the newcomerYou don't buy it, or stop using itLeaving an expensive watch in a drawer because nothing else you wear matches it
Change identityThe set, and how you see yourself, shifts upmarketA promotion, then a new car, then a new postcode

The first route is the one that empties bank accounts. The second is more common and is why many people never make the jump in the first place.

None of these is wrong if you can afford it and enjoy it. The problem is that the chain is rarely planned or budgeted for, and each step feels small because it is compared with the item that started it.

How to stop one purchase leading to many

  1. Price the whole set, not the item. Before buying, ask what else you would want to change if you owned it, and add that to the price.
  2. Decide the boundary in advance. Write down what the purchase is allowed to change ("just the coat, nothing else this season").
  3. Use a waiting rule. Wait a set period before any follow-on purchase and see if the feeling of mismatch fades. Often it does.
  4. Replace one for one. If something new comes in, something old goes out, which keeps the total from creeping up.
  5. Prefer purchases with no set to match. Experiences, gifts and one-off items that sit on their own don't create a constellation that needs completing.
  6. Notice the identity story. If the chain is really about becoming "the kind of person who owns X", decide whether you want that identity at that price.

FAQ

Is there a status purchase that doesn't lead to more spending?

A one-off item with nothing to match, such as Asendify's £6,000 Club certificate, doesn't create a set to complete. Saving the money is the cheapest alternative.

Who named the Diderot effect?

It is named after Denis Diderot's 1769 essay. Anthropologist Grant McCracken developed the idea in consumer research in 1988.

Is the Diderot effect the same as lifestyle creep?

They overlap. Lifestyle creep is spending rising with income; the Diderot effect is one specific trigger, where a single new item makes the rest feel inadequate.

How do I avoid the Diderot effect?

Price the whole set before buying, set a boundary for what the purchase may change, wait before follow-on buys and replace one for one.

Is the Diderot effect always bad?

No. Upgrading a set of things can be a considered choice. It becomes a problem when the chain is unplanned and unaffordable.
