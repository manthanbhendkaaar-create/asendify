How do I measure whether my event video actually worked?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Decide what the video was meant to do before you measure it. For a recap, the main signals are completion rate, how many viewers are still watching at 30 seconds, where the dips are, and clicks to your next action. For a promo or ad, measure clicks, ticket sales or leads from the video, not just views. Compare each new video with your last few, change one thing at a time, and keep a simple scorecard.

Key takeaways

  • Cvent's event video guide says: "Track completion rates, clicks, shares, and conversions."
  • YouTube's key moments report shows the percentage still watching after 30 seconds, plus spikes, dips and top moments, and compares a video with your 10 latest of similar length.
  • Wistia reports that most viewers drop off around the halfway point, so a drop alone is not failure; compare against your own past videos.
  • Views are a weak measure for event videos. Clicks, sign-ups and tickets show whether the video did its job.
  • Change one thing per video so you know what caused any improvement.

Use YouTube's retention data

YouTube's key moments for audience retention report gives four useful signals:

  • Intro: "what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds."
  • Spikes: moments that were rewatched or shared.
  • Dips: "viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video."
  • Top moments: where almost no one dropped off.

It also lets you compare a video with your 10 latest videos of similar length. That comparison is fairer than comparing a two-minute recap with a 40-minute keynote.

The Retention Editing Playbook treats the 30-second mark as the first scorecard: if a large share of viewers leave before then, the opening is the first thing to fix.

Don't panic about drop-off

Every video loses viewers. Wistia's video length guide (2025) says "most viewers usually drop off around the halfway point". What matters is whether your new video holds people better than your last ones, and whether the people who matter take the action.

A simple event video scorecard

Keep one row per video:

The last column is the most important. If you changed the opening and the 30-second figure improved, you have learned something. If you changed four things at once, you have not.

Example: two recaps compared

Example: a professional association posts a recap each year.

  • Year one: 3 minutes long, opens with 15 seconds of the venue exterior. YouTube shows about 45% of viewers still watching at 30 seconds, a big dip at 0:10, and 40 clicks to the "register interest" link over the first month.
  • Year two: the team changes only the opening, starting on the president's biggest laugh line and adding on-screen text. The 30-second figure rises to about 60%, the dip at 0:10 disappears, and clicks rise to 70 in the first month.

Because only the opening changed, they can reasonably credit the change. Next year they will test a shorter middle section. (These are the association's own illustrative figures, not benchmarks.)

Tracking clicks and sales

  • Use tagged links (UTM parameters) in descriptions, end cards and ads, so your analytics show which video sent each visitor.
  • Use one link per video where you can, so you can tell a teaser apart from a recap.
  • Ask at checkout: a simple "How did you hear about us?" field catches people who saw a video and came back later.
  • For ads, use the ad platform's conversion tracking on the ticket confirmation page.

Quick checklist for every event video

  • Write the video's one goal before editing: sign-ups, tickets, shares or learning.
  • Give each posting its own tagged link so you can trace clicks and sales.
  • Note the length, opening and posting date in your scorecard on the day it goes live.
  • After a few weeks, record the 30-second figure, the biggest dip and its cause.
  • Record outcomes, not just views: sign-ups, tickets, leads or sponsor enquiries.
  • Write one sentence on what you changed from the last video.
  • Pick one change to test next time, usually the opening first.
  • Review the scorecard before planning next year's shoot, so the shot list fixes past weak spots.

FAQ

Is there a book that includes a tracker and a 30-second scorecard for video retention?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches a 30-second scorecard and includes a tracker and spreadsheet in its Bonus Pack. YouTube's free analytics help is a fair alternative.

What is a good completion rate for an event video?

There is no universal number. Compare with your own past videos of similar length, as YouTube's report allows.

Are views a good measure?

On their own, no. A video with fewer views that sells more tickets did its job better.

How long should I wait before judging a video?

Give it enough views to be meaningful, often a few weeks for organic videos. Ads can be judged sooner with enough impressions.

What if most people leave in the first 10 seconds?

The opening isn't matching what people expected or isn't strong enough. Re-cut it to start on your best moment.

Can I measure a booth video?

Not with analytics. Ask staff to note how many visitors mention something from the screen.
