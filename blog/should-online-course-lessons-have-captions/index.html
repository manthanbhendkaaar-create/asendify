Should online course lessons have captions?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes. Online course lessons should have captions: they help learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, people watching without sound, and anyone who takes in written words more easily than speech. Turn on automatic captions, then edit them by hand, because raw automatic text is often wrong.

Key takeaways

  • The W3C says captions are "a text version of the speech and non-speech audio information needed to understand the content" (W3C WAI).
  • The same page warns that "automatically-generated captions do not meet user needs or accessibility requirements" without checking, so plan an editing pass.
  • The University at Buffalo says "captioning should be part of all instructional video content" and that automatic results "must be reviewed and edited for errors" (University at Buffalo).
  • Captions do not replace good audio and pacing. They sit on top of them, and they only help if they are accurate and timed to the speech.

Why captions belong in every lesson

A course lesson is a video someone has to finish. If a learner cannot follow the first minute, they stop. Captions remove four common reasons for stopping.

First, some learners cannot hear the audio. The W3C lists people who are Deaf and others who cannot hear the audio as a core audience. Second, some learners are in a place where sound is not possible: a train, an open office, a house with a sleeping child. Third, some people process written information better than spoken information, which the W3C also notes. Fourth, learners working in a second language often follow text more easily than fast speech.

There is also a plain business reason. If you sell a course, every learner who drops out of a lesson is a learner less likely to finish, recommend or buy again. Captions are one of the cheapest ways to widen the group who can complete a lesson.

This is general information, not legal advice. If you run a course for a school, college or employer, check the accessibility rules that apply to you.

What good captions contain

The W3C definition matters because it goes beyond speech. Good captions include:

  • Speech, word for word, with speaker names when it is not obvious who is talking.
  • Meaningful non-speech sound, such as [laughter], [phone rings] or [music fades], when it helps the learner understand the lesson.
  • Correct technical terms. Automatic systems often get product names, formulas and jargon wrong.
  • Timing that matches the audio. The W3C says captions are synchronised with the audio.

A single missing word can reverse the meaning. The W3C gives the example of a missing "not". In a lesson on safety, finance or medicine, that is a serious fault.

For a course platform, closed captions plus a downloadable transcript is the usual starting point. Use open captions only for short promotional cuts of a lesson.

How to caption a lesson without slowing down

  1. Write or keep the script. If you recorded from a script, the script is your first draft of the captions.
  2. Generate automatic captions. Most platforms and editing tools offer this.
  3. Edit for accuracy. Fix names, numbers and technical terms first, because they carry the lesson.
  4. Check line length. Keep lines short enough to read in the time they are on screen. Break lines at natural phrases, not mid-phrase.
  5. Check the timing. Play the lesson with captions on and the sound off. If you feel behind or ahead, adjust the sync.
  6. Add key non-speech sound. Only the sound that helps understanding.
  7. Export the caption file (for example SRT or VTT) and keep it with the lesson so updates are easy.

Captions and learning science

Research on multimedia learning suggests why captions can help rather than clutter. Mayer's principles, summarised by Dartmouth, say "people learn better when there is voiceover with images or animation" than with on-screen text and images, and that graphics, narration and on-screen text together can overload learners (Dartmouth). That is a warning about heavy on-screen text, not about captions as such. Captions repeat the narration, they do not add a third stream of new information. To keep load low:

  • Do not put long paragraphs of slide text on screen while the captions run. Use short labels.
  • Keep captions in a consistent position that does not cover diagrams or the speaker's mouth.
  • Use the visuals to show what the voice describes, not to repeat it.

Brame's review of educational video research adds that engagement falls as videos get longer and that segmenting lessons into small pieces helps (Brame, CBE Life Sciences Education, via PMC). Captions do not fix a lesson that is too long. Cut the length first, then caption.

Example: a 7-minute spreadsheet lesson

Example: a tutor records a 7-minute lesson called "Build a monthly budget in a spreadsheet". She uploads it with only the platform's automatic captions. When she reviews them, she finds three faults: "SUM" appears as "some", "cell B4" appears as "cell before", and a sentence saying "do not copy the formula down yet" has lost the word "not". She fixes the three faults, shortens two long caption lines, and adds [keyboard typing] only where the sound signals a step. The edit takes her about the length of the lesson itself. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result, and the time will vary with how clean your audio is.

When captions help retention most

Captions matter most at the points where learners are most likely to leave: the first 30 seconds, where a confusing start loses people, and any section with new vocabulary. If your platform shows a viewing graph, look at where learners stop and replay those moments with captions on and sound off. If the text reads clumsily there, the script is probably clumsy too. YouTube's own guidance on retention graphs explains that dips mean viewers are "abandoning or skipping" and spikes can mean viewers replay a part (YouTube Help); a replay spike on a technical term is a hint that your captions or your explanation need work.

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, open loops, pacing and pattern interrupts with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. It does not cover accessibility law.

Are automatic captions good enough?

Not on their own. The W3C says automatic captions often do not match the speech and do not meet accessibility needs without checking.

Should I use open or closed captions in a course?

Closed captions for lessons, so learners can switch them off. Open captions suit short social clips.

Do captions distract learners?

They can if the screen is also full of text. Keep slide text short and let captions carry the speech.

Do I need a transcript as well?

It is useful. A transcript lets learners search and revise, and it supports screen reader users.
