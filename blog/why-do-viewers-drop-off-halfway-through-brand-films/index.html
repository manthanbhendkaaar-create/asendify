Why do viewers drop off halfway through brand films?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Viewers usually leave a brand film halfway through because the story stops moving: the question that hooked them has been answered or forgotten, and the middle repeats the same mood, interviews or product shots without adding anything new. A mid-video drop is rarely about length alone. Fix it by giving the middle its own question, a turn in the story, or new information, and by cutting repetition.

Key takeaways

  • Magliano and Zacks (2011) found breaks in action had the biggest effect on how viewers split a film into events. Viewers track story progress; a middle with no new action feels like one long, unchanging event.
  • YouTube's key moments report marks dips where viewers stopped or skipped, and suggests moving compelling content earlier when the best moments come late.
  • Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014) found "Shorter videos are much more engaging". A long middle is the easiest place to cut.
  • Google's ABCDs ask brands to "hook and sustain attention with an immersive story", not just hook it.
  • A study of 150 popular films summarised by the Association for Psychological Science found modern film rhythms track natural patterns of attention. Rhythm needs variation, especially in the middle.

Why the middle is the hardest part

Openings get the most attention in planning: hook, cold open, logo placement. Endings get a call to action. The middle often gets whatever footage is left. Yet the middle is where viewers decide whether the rest is worth their time.

Magliano and Zacks's research helps explain why. Viewers break a film into events, and the biggest boundaries come from changes in action: someone starts doing something new, a new problem appears, a goal is reached. If the middle of your film is several minutes of the same kind of footage, the viewer experiences one long event with nothing new to anticipate. That is when they start thinking about leaving.

Brand films face an extra problem: they often avoid conflict. Without a problem, an obstacle or a moment of doubt, there's no tension to resolve, so there's no reason to stay for the second half.

How to fix the middle

  1. Map the questions. Write down what the viewer is wondering at each minute. If there's a stretch with no open question, that's your drop.
  2. Add a turn. A setback, a surprising number, a customer's honest doubt, or a "but then" moment.
  3. Cut repetition hard. One strong interview line beats three similar ones.
  4. Move a strong moment into the middle. If your best footage is all at the start or end, redistribute it.
  5. Use a re-hook. A short line like "That's when it nearly fell apart" previews the next section.
  6. Check length against content. If the story is told in three minutes, a five-minute film will sag.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on story structure and open loops for brand films?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops, hooks and pacing with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. Screenwriting books such as McKee's Story are an alternative for structure.

Is a mid-video drop always bad?

Some decline is normal. A steeper slope in one section is the signal to fix.

Should brand films be shorter?

Often. Cut until the story is tight; length should follow content.

What is a re-hook?

A short line or moment that previews what's coming next and gives viewers a new reason to stay.

Do brand films need conflict?

They need some tension: a problem, a risk or a doubt. Without it, there's little reason to keep watching.
