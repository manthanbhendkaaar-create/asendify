How do you keep a co-hosted podcast from drifting off topic?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Keep a co-hosted podcast on track by agreeing the episode's central question and a short running order before you record, giving each host a clear role, and using spoken signposts to move between segments. Let good tangents run briefly, but bring the conversation back to the question, and cut the tangents that don't pay off in the edit.

Key takeaways

  • Two hosts who get on well can talk for hours. That's the strength and the risk.
  • Decide one central question per episode and write it down where both of you can see it.
  • Give each host a role: one drives the structure, the other brings colour, stories and pushback.
  • Use signposts ("Let's get to the second mistake") so listeners always know where they are.
  • In the edit, keep tangents that are funny or revealing, and cut ones that go nowhere.

Why co-hosted shows drift

Chemistry is why people listen to two-host shows, but chemistry without structure becomes chat. Listeners who came for the topic start to drift away when ten minutes pass without it. Spotify's guide to episode performance says "getting into the heart of your episode as quickly as you can is a great way to keep up retention", and suggests listening back to understand why people drop off where the graph falls (Spotify for Creators).

Structure helps most. NPR's show editor checklist begins with "What's the focus, the central question you are trying to answer?", and after the edit asks whether the conversation answers that question and whether any slow sections should be cut (NPR Training). Spotify's production guide quotes a producer who suggests thinking about a story "as a series of questions you are creating in the mind of the listener" (Spotify for Creators). A running order is simply that list of questions, in order.

A method for every episode

  1. Write the central question. "Is it worth buying a used electric car in 2026?" Not "electric cars".
  2. Agree three to five segments. Each segment answers part of the question. Share them in a doc both hosts can see while recording.
  3. Assign the driver. One person is responsible for moving on. The other is free to riff.
  4. Plan one open loop. Tease something specific early ("I'll tell you what the dealer said about the battery later") and pay it off.
  5. Use signposts. "So that's the price. Now the battery question." Signposts also give the editor clean places to cut.
  6. Agree a tangent rule. For example: a tangent can run until it stops being funny or useful, then the driver brings it back with a question tied to the topic.
  7. Mark tangents while recording. A quick note of the time ("23:40 holiday story") saves hours in the edit.
  8. Edit for the question. Keep what answers it or makes listeners like you more. Cut the rest.

Example: a 35-minute two-host episode

Example (a hypothetical show, to show the method): two friends host a show about personal finance for renters.

  • Central question: "Should renters pay off student loans early or save for a deposit?"
  • Driver: Host A, who researched the topic. Sceptic: Host B.
  • 0:00 cold open: Host B: "I've been overpaying my loan for two years. Apparently that might have been a mistake." (open loop)
  • Segment 1 (2 to 9 min): how the loan works, in plain terms. Signpost: "So that's how it's repaid. Now: what does overpaying actually do?"
  • Segment 2 (9 to 17 min): overpaying vs saving, with a worked example. Host B pushes back.
  • Tangent (17 to 22 min): a funny story about a landlord. In the edit, 90 seconds stay because it's genuinely funny; 3.5 minutes go.
  • Segment 3 (20 to 30 min after edit): Host B's own situation, paying off the cold open.
  • Wrap: each host gives one sentence of advice, framed as general information rather than financial advice.

Mistakes to avoid

  • No prep from either host. If nobody owns the structure, nobody moves the episode on.
  • Talking over each other. It's hard to follow and hard to edit. Agree a small hand signal for "I want in".
  • Inside jokes without context. New listeners feel shut out. Give one line of context or cut it.
  • Cutting all the chemistry. Over-editing turns a friendly show into a lecture. Keep the moments that show why you're fun to listen to.
  • Long catch-up at the start. Save "how was your week" for after the hook, or cut it.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches podcast structure for co-hosts?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops, re-hooks and pacing for podcasts, clips and YouTube.

Should co-hosts script their episodes?

Usually not word for word. A running order with the central question and segments keeps the chemistry and adds direction.

Who should be the driver?

Whoever prepared the topic, or rotate the role each episode so both hosts share the work.

Are tangents always bad?

No. Funny or revealing tangents make people like the show. Cut the ones that go nowhere.

How do we stop talking over each other?

Record on separate tracks if you can, agree a hand signal, and leave a beat before replying.

Should the banter go at the start?

Put the hook first. A short bit of banter can follow, but keep it brief.
