How do coaches make video ads that hold attention?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Open on the problem your ideal client is stuck with, said in their words, within the first three seconds. Then give one genuinely useful insight that shows you understand it, add real proof (a genuine client testimonial used with permission, or a short demonstration of how you work), and finish with one low-commitment next step such as a free call or a guide. Coaching ads lose people when they open on the coach's biography or make big promises with nothing behind them.

Key takeaways

  • The first seconds decide the ad. TikTok's creative best practices say to "introduce your content proposition in the first 3 seconds" and prioritise the hook "in the first 6 seconds".
  • Google's ABCD guidance recommends you "jump in", "feature people" and use "humour, surprise and intrigue" to connect.
  • Testimonials must be real and used with permission. The UK's Advertising Standards Authority says marketers "must hold documentary evidence" that a testimonial is genuine and must not use one "without permission".
  • One ad, one audience, one problem. A coach who helps "everyone" holds no one.
  • Test several hooks with the same body rather than several completely different ads.

Why coaching ads struggle to hold attention

Coaching is a trust purchase. The viewer is asking two questions at once: "Is this about my problem?" and "Can I trust this person?" Most weak coaching ads answer neither in the opening. They start with a logo, a name and a list of qualifications, or with a broad promise like "transform your life". The viewer scrolls on before the coach ever says anything useful.

Strong coaching ads reverse the order: the problem first, the insight second, the coach's credibility third, and the ask last.

Hooks coaches can use

  • The client's own words: "I know what to do, I just don't do it."
  • A specific situation: "If your team goes quiet every time you ask for ideas..."
  • A myth to correct: "Waking up at 5am isn't why you're tired."
  • A quick win: "One sentence to stop over-explaining in meetings."
  • A question they ask themselves: "Why do I keep saying yes to things I don't want to do?"

Avoid hooks that promise outcomes you can't stand behind ("double your income in 30 days"). They may break advertising rules, they attract the wrong clients, and viewers have learned to distrust them. If you are unsure, see whether you can use a shocking or curiosity hook in an ad.

Production choices that help

  1. Film vertically for social placements (TikTok lists 9:16 among its basics) and keep a horizontal version for YouTube.
  2. Talk to one person. Look into the lens, use "you", keep sentences short.
  3. Caption everything. TikTok recommends "captions or text overlays to provide context", and many people watch muted.
  4. Show yourself working, if clients agree: a whiteboard, a session setting (with no client identifiable without consent), a workshop.
  5. Keep it simple. A phone, good light and clear sound beat a polished studio video that looks like a corporate advert.

Proof without breaking the rules

Real proof makes coaching ads work, but it is also where coaches get into trouble. Under the ASA guidance, testimonials must be genuine, you must hold contact details for the person, the claims in them must not mislead, and you need permission to use them. Avoid implying typical results from one exceptional client. For life, health or money coaching, be extra careful about outcome claims; general information is not a promise of results.

Common mistakes

  • Opening on your name, logo or qualifications.
  • Talking about the coaching process ("my 6-step framework") before the viewer cares.
  • Using a client story without written consent.
  • Asking for a big commitment (a high-ticket programme) in a cold ad.
  • Running one ad for months until it stops working.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches hooks for coaching video ads?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook from Asendify covers hooks and pacing for £1,720 one-time. TikTok's and Google's free creative guides are alternatives.

How long should a coaching video ad be?

Long enough to name the problem, give one insight and an ask; often 15 to 45 seconds for social. Test shorter and longer cuts.

Should I use client testimonials?

Yes, if they are genuine, you have permission and the claims don't mislead, as the ASA requires.

Should I show my face?

Usually yes. Coaching is a trust purchase, and seeing and hearing you helps viewers decide.

What should the call to action be?

A low-commitment step, such as a free call, guide or workshop, works better in a cold ad than a direct programme sale.
