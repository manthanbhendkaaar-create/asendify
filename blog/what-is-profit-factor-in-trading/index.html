What is profit factor in trading, and what is a good one?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Profit factor is your gross profit divided by your gross loss over a set of trades. A figure above 1.0 means the winners made more than the losers lost; below 1.0 means the strategy lost money. It's a quick health check for a strategy, but it hides how bumpy the ride was, so read it next to your number of trades, drawdown and average risk per trade.

This page is education only, not financial advice. Trading is risky: ESMA reported that 74-89% of retail accounts typically lose money on CFDs.

Key takeaways

  • Profit factor = gross profit ÷ gross loss (losses taken as a positive number).
  • TradingView: "A value greater than 1.0 indicates a profitable strategy."
  • TradeStation's strategy reports include commissions and, where set, slippage in the figure, so include costs in yours too.
  • A profit factor from 15 trades says very little; from several hundred it says more.
  • Two strategies with the same profit factor can have very different drawdowns.

How to calculate it

TradeStation's help files define it plainly: "This value is calculated by dividing Gross Profit by Gross Loss" (TradeStation). TradingView describes it as "dividing the absolute value of all winning trades by the absolute value of all losing trades" (TradingView).

  1. Add up every winning trade after costs: that's gross profit.
  2. Add up every losing trade after costs, ignoring the minus sign: that's gross loss.
  3. Divide gross profit by gross loss.
Profit factorWhat it means
Below 1.0Losing strategy: losses outweigh wins
1.0Break-even before any costs you left out
1.0 to about 1.3Thin edge; costs or a bad run can wipe it out
About 1.3 to 2.0A meaningful edge if the sample is large
Well above 2.0Check for too few trades, curve fitting or missing costs

The bands above are rules of thumb, not standards; there's no official "good" number.

Worked example

Example: Alex reviews his last 40 trades.

TradesTotal
Winners16+£4,800
Losers24−£3,600
Net40+£1,200

Profit factor = £4,800 ÷ £3,600 = 1.33. He won only 40% of trades but his average winner (£300) was twice his average loser (£150), so the strategy made money.

Now he adds £5 commission per trade he'd left out: £200 in total. Gross profit falls to £4,720 and gross loss rises to £3,720, so profit factor drops to 1.27. Costs matter more when the edge is thin.

How profit factor relates to win rate and expectancy

Profit factor combines win rate and the size of wins vs losses into one number. Expectancy says the same thing per trade: the average amount you make or lose each time. A strategy with a 35% win rate can have a profit factor of 2 if its winners are big; one with a 70% win rate can be below 1 if the occasional loss is huge.

Strategy B feels better day to day and still loses money.

What profit factor doesn't tell you

  • Sample size. Ten trades can produce almost any number by luck.
  • Drawdown. A profit factor of 1.5 can still include a 30% fall along the way.
  • Position size changes. If you doubled size after losses, the figure mixes skill with luck.
  • Market changes. A backtest profit factor from a trending year may not hold in a choppy one.
  • Leverage risk. The FCA requires CFD firms to close positions when funds fall to 50% of margin and to give negative balance protection; a good profit factor doesn't protect you from a gap or a margin call.

ESMA's 2018 product intervention announcement reported that "74-89% of retail accounts typically lose money" on CFDs, which is why tracking the honest figure, costs included, matters.

How to use it in your journal

  • Calculate it monthly and over your full history, after costs.
  • Split it by setup: your breakout trades may be 1.6 while your reversal trades are 0.8.
  • Pair it with maximum drawdown and expectancy.
  • Only change a strategy after a meaningful number of trades, not after one bad week.

The "largest single win" check matters: if one lucky trade supplies most of your gross profit, remove it and recalculate. If profit factor falls below 1 without it, you don't yet have evidence of an edge, just one good day.

What is a good profit factor?

Above 1.0 is profitable; many traders treat roughly 1.3 to 2.0 over a large sample as a meaningful edge. There's no official standard.

Should profit factor include commissions?

Yes. TradeStation includes commissions, and slippage where set, in its calculation.

How many trades do I need?

There's no fixed number, but tens of trades is weak evidence and hundreds is better.

Can profit factor be infinite?

If you have no losing trades, the formula divides by zero. That only means the sample is too small.

Is this financial advice?

No. This is general education. Trading carries a high risk of losing money.
