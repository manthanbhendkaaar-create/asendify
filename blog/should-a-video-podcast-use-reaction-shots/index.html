Key takeaways

  • Spotify for Creators advises recording multiple angles and alternating shots to "focus on who's speaking" or to capture a facial expression, and says "there isn't a golden rule for shot length" (Spotify for Creators).
  • Google says video supports connection through "body language and facial expressions" and that retention graphs let you "pinpoint the exact second" interest rises or falls (Google).
  • Descript suggests using close-ups and multiple camera angles to hide audio edits in an interview (Descript), which is a practical second use for reaction shots.
  • YouTube's retention report says "spikes appear when more viewers are watching, rewatching, or sharing those parts of your video" (YouTube Help). A strong reaction can be one of them.

What a reaction shot does in a video podcast

A video podcast is mostly people talking, so the picture has to earn its place. A reaction shot shows the listener while someone else speaks. It tells the viewer how to take the line: a raised eyebrow after a bold claim, a laugh before the punchline lands, a long breath after a hard story. Google's podcast guidance says video supports intimacy through "body language and facial expressions", and a reaction shot is the simplest way to show those.

It also does practical work. Cutting to the listener lets you hide an audio edit in the speaker's line, and gives the eye a change without a graphic. The risk is overuse: a cut to the listener every few seconds becomes a tic, and viewers start to feel the edit rather than the conversation.

Step-by-step: use reaction shots well

  1. Record coverage. Spotify's advice is to record from multiple angles, such as a wide shot and close-ups, so you have options. Give each person their own camera if you can.
  2. Mark real reactions as you watch. Note the moments where a face changes: a laugh, a frown, a long look. Do not add them everywhere.
  3. Decide the default shot. Most of the time, show whoever is speaking. Treat the reaction as the exception.
  4. Cut on the reaction, not before it. Bring in the listener shot just as the reaction starts, so the viewer sees it happen.
  5. Keep it long enough to read. There is no rule for shot length, as Spotify's guide says, but a face needs a moment to register. Too short and it looks like a glitch.
  6. Return on a natural beat. Cut back to the speaker when they take a breath or start a new sentence, not mid-word.
  7. Keep the audio continuous. The speaker's voice should carry across the cut. Do not cut the audio with the picture unless you mean to.
  8. Use a wide shot to reset. After a run of close-ups, a wide shot shows the room and both people, so viewers know where everyone is.
  9. Avoid mismatched reactions. If the listener is smiling while the speaker describes a loss, the cut will look wrong. Check the context of the listener's expression.
  10. Use them to cover edits. Where you have trimmed a pause or removed a stumble, a reaction shot can hide the cut, as Descript suggests with close-ups and multiple angles.
  11. Review on a phone. Faces are small on a phone; a tight close-up often reads better than a wide shot.
  12. Check the graph. Compare retention in sections with many reaction cuts and sections with few.

Hosts as listeners: what makes a good reaction

A good reaction shot is a real one. If a guest says something surprising and the host genuinely reacts, use it. If the host is waiting to speak, thinking of the next question, or reading notes, it may not help. Where you can, ask hosts to listen actively and keep their eyes on the speaker. That is a recording decision, but it gives the editor better material.

Do not create reactions that did not happen, for example by reusing a laugh from another moment. That can mislead viewers and is unfair to the host. Using a different angle of the same moment is fine.

Reaction shots and the rest of the picture

Reaction shots are one tool among several. Mayer's signalling principle says "people learn better when there are cues that emphasize information that is more important" (Dartmouth). It is about learning, but a well-timed reaction works as a cue in a similar way, pointing to the line that matters. Mayer's coherence principle, that "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded", is the check against overuse: if a reaction shot adds nothing, cut it.

If you also use on-screen graphics, avoid stacking everything at once. A reaction shot, a lower third and a pop-up all at the same moment compete for attention.

Example: a 30-minute interview podcast

Example: a two-camera video podcast has a wide shot and a close-up of each person. In the first edit, the editor cuts to the listener every time the speaker finishes a sentence, giving 90 cuts in 30 minutes, many of them under two seconds. In the second edit, the editor stays on the speaker by default and uses 25 reaction shots, each held for three to five seconds, at moments marked while watching: a surprising claim, two laughs, one long pause after a hard story. Four of them cover trimmed pauses in the speaker's audio. A wide shot appears after each run of close-ups. The editor then compares retention across the two versions on similar episodes. Remote and single-camera shows

If you record remotely, each guest has their own video, so reaction shots are easy but the picture quality can vary. Choose reactions where the person is clearly visible and looking at the screen. If you only have one camera on one person, you cannot show a reaction without a cutaway, such as a short clip, an image or a graphic. In that case, use a different cue: a caption, a sound or a small zoom.

When to leave reaction shots out

Leave them out when they feel forced, when the listener is not engaged on camera, or when the conversation is technical and the viewer needs to follow the speaker's hands or screen. A simple, steady frame is a legitimate choice. Not every show needs cutting.

How often should I cut to the listener?

Only when their face adds meaning. Stay on the speaker by default and use reactions as punctuation.
