Do quizzes inside course videos keep learners watching and remembering?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes, short quizzes placed between segments of a course video help learners stay focused and remember more. In a Harvard study, students quizzed after each part of a 21-minute lecture reported mind wandering about half as often, took more notes and scored higher on the final test than students who were not quizzed. Keep quizzes brief, low-stakes and about what was just taught.

Key takeaways

  • Szpunar, Khan and Schacter (2013), PNAS, split a 21-minute statistics lecture into four segments of about 5.5 minutes, with a 2-minute quiz after each for one group.
  • Mind wandering was reported on 19% of random checks in the quizzed group, against 39% for a group that restudied the material and 41% for a group that did neither.
  • The quizzed group scored 90% on the final cumulative test, against 76% (restudy) and 68% (not tested), and reported less anxiety about the test.
  • The authors say interpolated testing "encourages task-relevant note-taking activities, and improves learning."
  • Brame's review of educational video lists "Packaging video with interactive questions" among the main ways to raise learning from video.
  • In the edX study by Guo and colleagues, shorter videos led to more attempts at the practice problems that followed them.

Why a quiz works better than a recap

A recap tells the learner the answer again. A quiz asks them to pull it from memory. In the Harvard study, one group saw the same questions with the answers already filled in, a "restudy" condition. They did no better than learners who saw nothing, and they still let their minds wander. Being asked was what made the difference.

The quiz also changes how people watch the next segment. Once learners expect a question, they pay attention and take notes. That is the "keep watching" effect course creators care about.

Option between segmentsEffort for learnerEffect in the Harvard study
Nothing (keep watching)None41% mind wandering; 68% final score
Recap with answers shownLow39% mind wandering; 76% final score
Short quiz on that segmentMedium19% mind wandering; 90% final score

How to add quizzes to course videos

  1. Split the lesson into segments of roughly 5-7 minutes. Each segment should teach one idea you can ask about.
  2. Write 2-4 questions per segment. Ask learners to recall or apply, not just to recognise a phrase they heard seconds ago.
  3. Keep them low-stakes. No grades, or very light ones. The aim is practice, not judgement.
  4. Give quick feedback. Show the correct answer and a one-line reason straight after.
  5. Place them where attention dips. If your platform's retention data shows a drop at minute six, a question just before it is a good test.
  6. Ask a short-answer question now and then. Typing a sentence takes more effort than clicking, and that effort helps memory.

A note on the evidence

The Harvard study used a single 21-minute lecture with undergraduates in a lab-like setting. Real courses are longer, learners are busier and many watch on phones. The direction of the finding fits a wide body of research on retrieval practice, but the exact percentages will not carry over to your course. Test it: add quizzes to one module, keep another as it is, and compare.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on keeping learners engaged between course video sections?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers pacing, open loops and pattern interrupts with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. The research on interpolated quizzes is free to read.

How many questions should I add per video?

Two to four short questions after each segment of about five to seven minutes is a reasonable starting point.

Do quizzes annoy learners?

Short, low-stakes quizzes with instant feedback usually don't. In the Harvard study, quizzed students reported less anxiety about the final test.

Can I add quizzes to YouTube videos?

Not inside the player. Use end screens or links to a quiz page, pinned comments or community polls instead.

Is a summary slide as good as a quiz?

No. In the study, seeing the answers again did not reduce mind wandering; being asked to recall them did.
