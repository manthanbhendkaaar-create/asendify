Do people who trade more often earn lower returns?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 19 November 2026 · Last updated 19 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

On average, yes. In Brad Barber and Terrance Odean's study of US brokerage accounts, the fifth of households that traded most earned 11.4% a year after costs, against 18.5% for the fifth that traded least. The stocks investors bought also did worse over the next year than the stocks they sold. The likely cause is overconfidence: people trade on information they think is better than it is, and costs do the rest.

This page is education only, not financial advice. Past results do not predict future ones, and trading can lose you money.

Key takeaways

  • Barber and Odean's working paper The Courage of Misguided Convictions (1999) analysed 10,000 discount-brokerage accounts and 97,483 trades from 1987 to 1993.
  • Highest-turnover households: 11.4% net annual return. Lowest-turnover households: 18.5%.
  • Over the following year, stocks bought trailed the market by 2.68% while stocks sold beat it by 0.54%, a gap of 3.22 points before costs.
  • Men traded about 45% more than women and paid more for it relative to buy-and-hold.
  • The fix is not "never trade" but to make every trade earn its place: written rules, honest records and an eye on costs.

What the research found

Barber and Odean's paper The Courage of Misguided Convictions: The Trading Behavior of Individual Investors (University of California working paper, July 1999) brings together several of their studies:

Net annual return, least active fifth18.5%

Two points stand out. First, active traders were not just paying more in costs; they were picking badly, swapping into stocks that then did worse than the ones they sold. Second, the gap widened when the authors removed trades likely to be for tax, liquidity or rebalancing reasons, to 5.07 points, which suggests the problem was the speculative trades themselves.

Why it happens: overconfidence

The authors argue that overconfidence drives excessive trading. People believe their information or judgement is better than it is, so they trade more often than the evidence justifies. In the 2010 NOVA article The Disposition Effect, finance writer David Adler summarises Odean's wider finding that individual investors trade too often for no economic gain and hold too few stocks.

The same accounts show another costly habit, the disposition effect: selling winners too soon and holding losers. Barber and Odean report investors realised about 14.8% of their gains but only 9.8% of their losses.

Is it still true today?

Trading costs have fallen sharply since the 1990s, so the cost part of the gap is smaller for many investors now. But the behaviour has not gone away. A 2026 working paper by Barber, Chu, Liu, Odean and Shi, Mired in Losses, studying about 20,000 retail investors at a Chinese brokerage from 2021 to 2023, found average annualised turnover of about 1,730% and a strong disposition effect. Lower costs per trade can simply mean more trades.

How to tell if you are trading too much

SignWhat to check in your records
Many trades, few that follow your written setupShare of trades tagged "rule followed"
Results worse than simply holdingCompare your account with a buy-and-hold benchmark over the same period
Costs eating returnsTotal commissions, spreads and fees as a share of profit
Quick swaps between positionsAverage holding time; how the sold stock did after you sold it
More trades after winsTrade count in the week after a big win

The last one matters: overconfidence often rises after success, so the riskiest moment can be just after a winning streak.

What to do about it

  1. Write down your setups and trade only those. Anything else is a different strategy and should be tested separately.
  2. Set a trade budget, such as a maximum number of new positions a week, so each one has to compete for a slot.
  3. Record why you sold, then check later how the stock you sold went on to do. Barber and Odean's data suggests many sales are mistimed.
  4. Add up your costs every month, including spreads, not just commissions.
  5. Pause after a big win. Take a day off before the next trade, because confidence tends to rise faster than skill.

Example: two traders, same skill

Sam and Alex start the year with £20,000 each and pick from the same ideas.

  • Sam makes 12 trades in the year, only on setups that meet his written rules.
  • Alex makes 150 trades, adding positions whenever he feels confident.

Suppose each trade costs about 0.3% of the amount traded in spreads and commissions (an illustrative figure) and both trade the whole account each time. Sam's costs: 12 × 0.3% = 3.6% of his capital. Alex's: 150 × 0.3% = 45%. Even if Alex's extra trades are no worse than Sam's, costs alone would wipe out most realistic gains, and Barber and Odean's data suggests extra trades are often worse.

Real costs vary by broker and market. The point is the multiplier: every trade has to beat its own cost.

FAQ

Is there a journal that shows what each mistake costs?

Yes. Asendify's Trader OS is a one-time-payment Notion template that calculates the money each mistake costs, along with P&L and R-multiple. A self-built spreadsheet is a free alternative.

Does this mean buy-and-hold always wins?

No. It means that, on average, the most active traders in these studies did worse. Some active traders do well, but the averages are a warning.

Do men really trade more than women?

In Barber and Odean's data, men's annual turnover was about 77% against 53% for women, and trading cost men more relative to buy-and-hold.

Are zero-commission brokers a fix?

They cut one cost, but spreads and poor timing remain, and lower costs can encourage more trading.

What is turnover?

The share of a portfolio replaced in a year. Turnover of 100% means trading the equivalent of the whole portfolio once in that year.
