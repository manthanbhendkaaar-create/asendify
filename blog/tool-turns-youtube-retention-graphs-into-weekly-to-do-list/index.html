Is there a tool that turns my YouTube retention graphs into a weekly to-do list?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 16 November 2026 · Last updated 16 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes. Some tools read your YouTube retention graphs and turn them into a short weekly list of edits to make. Asendify's Retention Doctor sends a Monday plan with the three fixes that would keep the most viewers, ticked off as you apply them; it suits creators on one established channel, while a free spreadsheet built from YouTube Studio data does a similar job by hand.

Key takeaways

  • A retention graph tells you where people left; a to-do list tells you what to change in the next video.
  • Limit the list to three items a week. More than that and nothing gets done properly.
  • Rank fixes by how many viewers each drop cost, not by how easy it looks.
  • Track whether each fix worked by comparing the same numbers on the next few uploads.
  • You can build the same system free in a spreadsheet; a tool mainly saves the weekly reading time.

Why a graph alone rarely changes anything

YouTube Studio shows a retention curve for every video and highlights intros, dips, spikes and top moments when a video is long enough and has enough views. YouTube's help page on audience retention key moments explains what each means. The problem is the step after. A curve with five dips across four videos does not tell you which change matters most this week, so most creators look, nod and upload the next video the same way.

A to-do list fixes that by forcing three decisions: which drops matter, what causes them, and what you will do differently.

How to turn retention graphs into a weekly list

  1. Collect the numbers. For each video from the last two to four weeks, note the share still watching at 30 seconds, the biggest dip (timestamp and size) and average percentage viewed. YouTube's Analytics API metrics list these as audienceWatchRatio, relativeRetentionPerformance and averageViewPercentage if you want to pull them automatically.
  2. Name the cause of each big dip. Watch the 15 seconds before it. Common causes: slow intro, a tangent, a sponsor read before any payoff, a repeated point, an ending that signals "we're done".
  3. Group repeats. If three videos lose people at the intro, that is one item, not three.
  4. Rank by viewers lost. On the same video, a 10% dip at 0:20 costs more viewers than a 4% dip at 7:00, because more people are still watching early on.
  5. Pick three. Write each as an instruction for the next script or edit.
  6. Check next week. Did the drop shrink on the videos where you applied the fix? Keep it, or try another fix.

Common drop-off causes and the fix to write down

Most dips come from a handful of causes. Writing the fix as an instruction makes it easy to apply on the next edit.

Where the drop happensLikely causeFix to write on the list

These are starting points, not rules. If the same dip appears after you apply a fix, rewatch the section again; the real cause may be pacing, audio or a confusing visual. YouTube itself notes that a spike can mean a section was unclear and needed rewatching, so treat spikes as something to check too, not only a win.

Worked example

Example: a creator uploads two 12-minute videos a week. Over the last four uploads:

  • 30-second hold: 55%, 60%, 52%, 58%.
  • Biggest dips: 0:20 on two videos (intro recap), 3:40 on one (sponsor read), 9:50 on one (a "to wrap up" line).
  • Average percentage viewed: 31% to 36%.

Their Monday list:

  1. Start with the result of the video in the first sentence; drop the recap of last week's episode (two videos, largest loss).
  2. Move the sponsor read after the first useful tip.
  3. Replace "to wrap up" with a final tip or a reason to keep watching.

Two weeks later they compare the next four uploads. If the 30-second hold is now above 60% on most of them, fix one stays and the next item moves up.

What makes a fix list worth following

  • It is specific. "Improve your intro" is not a fix. "Say the outcome in sentence one" is.
  • It is short. Three items a week is a pace you can keep with a normal upload schedule.
  • It is measured. Each fix is checked against the same numbers on later videos.
  • It respects small samples. One video is noise; look for the same change across three or more.

YouTube's guide to impressions and watch time is worth reading alongside this, because a better intro only helps if people click in the first place.

FAQ

Is there a tool that turns my YouTube retention graphs into a weekly to-do list?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Doctor sends a Monday three-fix plan from your real retention data. A spreadsheet built from YouTube Studio is the free alternative.

How many fixes should I work on at once?

Three a week is enough for most creators. More than that makes it hard to tell which change worked.

How do I know if a fix worked?

Compare the same numbers, such as 30-second hold or the size of a dip, across at least three new videos against the three before the change.

Does this work for Shorts?

Partly. For Shorts, focus on the first two to three seconds and whether people loop. Very low-view Shorts may not show detailed retention data.

Do I need to connect my channel to do this?

No. You can read the numbers in YouTube Studio and keep the list yourself. Tools that connect usually ask for read-only access, which cannot change your videos.
