Do riser sound effects help keep viewers watching?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A riser, a sound that climbs in pitch, volume or density, can help keep viewers watching by building tension that promises a payoff a few seconds later. Music research shows rising loudness and pitch increase felt tension, and tension builds toward an expected release. It only works if something worth waiting for arrives; a riser into nothing teaches viewers to ignore it.

Key takeaways

  • Farbood (2012), in Music Perception, found loudness and pitch height had the clearest effect on perceived musical tension, with note density adding a weaker effect. A riser combines all three.
  • Turrell and colleagues (2025), also in Music Perception, found listeners' tension rose during build-ups before expected drops in dance music and fell when the expectation was fulfilled.
  • McCarthy and Olsen (2017) found rising-intensity sounds were located faster and more accurately than falling ones, consistent with the idea that "looming" sounds get priority.
  • No study measures risers and YouTube retention directly. Treat them as a tension tool and test against your own key moments report.
  • Many feed viewers watch muted, so a riser can't carry a sound-off ad on its own.

What a riser is

A riser is a sound effect that builds: a rising synth, a swelling noise sweep, a drum roll that speeds up, or a reversed cymbal. It usually runs for one to four seconds and ends on a hit, a drop, a cut or sudden silence. Editors use risers before reveals, transitions, price announcements, before-and-after shots and the main point of a video.

The structure is a small open loop in sound. The riser asks "what's coming?" and the hit answers it.

Why tension holds attention, and why it can backfire

Farbood's model shows that listeners feel more tension as loudness and pitch rise. Turrell's study shows that tension builds toward an expected release and falls once that release comes. In video terms, a riser makes viewers expect something. That expectation is a reason to keep watching for the next few seconds, which is exactly the window where many viewers decide whether to stay.

The catch is in the same research: the release has to come. If the riser ends and nothing changes, you've spent the viewer's patience. Do that a few times and the riser stops working, because viewers learn it predicts nothing.

There is also a mix problem. Google's ABCDs guidance for video ads recommends supporting the message with audio and on-screen text and avoiding competing elements. A loud riser under a voiceover is a competing element. Duck the riser under speech, or place it in a gap.

How to use a riser well

  1. Pick the payoff first: the moment that deserves a build.
  2. Start the riser one to three seconds before it. Shorter in Shorts and ads, longer in long-form.
  3. End it exactly on the payoff, with a hit, a cut, a drop or a beat of silence.
  4. Keep it under the voice. If someone is speaking, lower the riser or wait for a pause.
  5. Pair it with a visual change (a zoom, a cut, text) so muted viewers still get a cue.
  6. Use risers sparingly: a handful in a ten-minute video, not dozens.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on sound design and open loops for video retention?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops, pacing and pattern interrupts with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. Sound-library guides cover risers themselves for free.

How long should a riser be?

Often one to three seconds in short-form and ads, and up to several seconds in long-form or trailers.

Where can I get riser sounds?

Royalty-free sound libraries and many editing apps include risers. Check the licence covers your use, especially for paid ads.

Do risers work with sound off?

No. Pair the riser with a visual cue such as a zoom, cut or text so muted viewers still feel the build.

Can too many risers hurt retention?

Yes. If risers keep leading to nothing, viewers stop reacting to them.
