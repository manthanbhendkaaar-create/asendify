How do I set retention goals for a video team?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Set retention goals against your own baseline, not a number from the internet. Take your last 10 videos of similar length, record the share still watching after 30 seconds and the average percentage viewed, then set modest targets above that median. Add one process goal the team controls, such as "every video gets a dip review", because editors can't control topic or traffic.

Key takeaways

  • There is no universal "good" retention figure; it depends on length, format and audience. YouTube itself compares a video with your 10 latest videos of similar length.
  • Use three numbers: the 30-second intro figure, average percentage viewed and the number of sharp dips.
  • Length changes what is realistic. Wistia reports videos under a minute average a 52% engagement rate, and an 11% drop in engagement once videos pass 30 minutes.
  • Pair outcome goals with process goals (hook reviewed, dip review done) so the team is judged on what it controls.
  • Review monthly, by format, and reset baselines each quarter.

Why a universal target doesn't work

Teams often ask for "the" retention benchmark, such as 50%. But retention falls naturally as videos get longer. A large study of edX lecture videos by Guo, Kim and Rubin found "the median engagement time is at most 6 minutes, regardless of total video length". A 50% target is easy for a 90-second Short and very hard for a 40-minute podcast. A shared, fixed number either demotivates the long-form editors or flatters the short-form ones.

YouTube's own report avoids this by offering "typical retention" against your recent videos of similar length, and a comparison with all YouTube videos of similar length. Build your goals the same way.

YouTube describes the intro figure as "what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds", and dips as places where "viewers are abandoning or skipping". Those two definitions map neatly to the two jobs most editors own: the opening and the middle.

How to set the goals: five steps

  1. Group videos by format and length. Shorts, 8 to 15-minute videos, long podcasts and paid ads each get their own baseline.
  2. Take a baseline. For each group, write down the intro figure and average percentage viewed for the last 10 videos. Use the median, not the best video.
  3. Set a modest outcome goal. A few percentage points above the median for the next quarter is realistic; doubling it is not.
  4. Add process goals. For example: "every script has the payoff of the title inside the first 30 seconds", "every video gets a 15-minute dip review 48 hours after publishing".
  5. Review monthly, reset quarterly. Look at the trend across several videos, not single uploads.

Outcome goals vs process goals

TypeExampleProsCons
OutcomeRaise median intro figure from 62% to 66%Directly tied to resultsAffected by topic and traffic source
Process100% of videos get a written dip reviewFully in the team's controlCan become box-ticking
LearningTest two hook styles per month and record the resultBuilds team skillSlow to show in numbers

A healthy team plan has one of each. If the only goal is an outcome number, editors will be blamed for a weak topic. If the only goal is a process, nobody checks whether the process works.

Worked example

Example: a four-person team makes weekly 10-minute explainers and three Shorts a week.

After eight weeks, the explainers' intro median is 65% but average percentage viewed hasn't moved. The dip reviews show a recurring drop at the halfway point where each video recaps. The team cuts the recap, and the next month the median rises to 40%. The figures are illustrative; your baseline will differ.

Common mistakes

  • Chasing one viral outlier. One video with unusual traffic distorts any average. Use medians.
  • Comparing across formats. A Short and a 30-minute interview should never share a target.
  • Judging after one day. The first viewers may not be typical of later traffic; wait a few days before drawing conclusions.
  • Ignoring packaging. A poor intro figure can mean the title promised something the video didn't deliver, not that the edit was slow. See why a video can have good retention but few views.

What is a good audience retention percentage?

It depends on length and format. Compare with your own recent videos of similar length, as YouTube's typical retention view does.

Should editors be paid on retention results?

Be careful. Topic and traffic affect retention as much as editing, so mix outcome bonuses with process goals.

How often should we review retention goals?

Look monthly at trends across several videos and reset baselines each quarter.

Which retention number matters most?

The 30-second intro figure is the clearest signal for the opening; average percentage viewed shows how the whole video holds.
