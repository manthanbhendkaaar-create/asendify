How do you cut an event video to the beat of the music without it feeling repetitive?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Choose a licensed track first, mark its beats and sections, then cut on the beats that matter: the downbeats, the drop and the changes between sections. Cutting on every single beat quickly feels mechanical. Vary shot length with the song, slower in the build and faster at the peak, and drop the music low whenever someone speaks so the words carry.

Key takeaways

  • Pick and license the track before you edit; changing music late means re-cutting everything.
  • Beat markers make the job faster: Adobe's Premiere mobile app, for example, "identifies rhythms in your audio and displays them as markers on the timeline."
  • Cut on structurally important beats, not all of them; match pace to the song's sections.
  • Put the strongest shot on the first big beat; the opening seconds decide whether people stay.
  • Duck the music under speech; clear words matter more than a perfect cut.

Pacing by song section

Song sectionTypical shot lengthWhat to show
IntroLonger shots, cut every 2-4 barsEstablishing shots, arrivals, venue
BuildShorter, cut every barPeople moving, preparation, anticipation
Drop or chorusFastest, cut on downbeatsPeak moments: crowd, stage, reactions
BreakdownSlow, one or two long shotsA speech line, a quiet emotional moment
Final chorusFast againBest moments not yet used
OutroLong final shotWide shot, logo, call to action

Step 1: choose and license the music

Choose the track to suit the event's tone and the video length. Check rights before you start: copyright in music is separate from copyright in your footage, and GOV.UK's guidance on ownership of copyright explains that the creator is usually the first owner unless rights are assigned. Use a track you have licensed for this use (including social platforms and any paid ads), or a library track whose licence covers it.

Step 2: mark the beats

Most editors use beat markers. Adobe's help page on beat detection in the Premiere mobile app explains that it detects rhythm and places markers on the timeline, with a choice of "Fewer", "More" or "All" beats, and clips snap to the nearest marker when you drag them. Choosing fewer markers is a useful habit: it nudges you to cut on the strong beats rather than every one. Desktop editors can tap markers in by hand while the track plays.

Also mark the sections: where the build starts, where the drop or chorus hits, where it breaks down.

Step 3: put the best shot on the first big beat

Open with a strong moment, then hit the first big beat with your best shot of the day. YouTube's key moments for audience retention report shows the share of viewers still watching after 30 seconds; a slow musical intro over logo shots often shows up as an early dip. If the track's intro is long, start the music partway in or trim the intro.

Step 4: avoid the repetitive trap

  • Don't cut on every beat for the whole video. It becomes a metronome.
  • Change rhythm with the song. Long shots in the intro and breakdown, fast cuts at the peak.
  • Cut on action, too. A hand raising, a glass clinking or a jump landing on the beat feels better than a random cut on the beat.
  • Let some shots run across a beat. A great moment can hold for two bars.

Step 5: let speech breathe

If the video uses speech (a speaker's line, a testimonial), drop the music low under it, and time the line to start just after a beat. Canon's recap video guide recommends a lavalier for interviews and a shotgun mic for overall sound; clean recordings make it much easier to mix speech over music.

Example: a 75-second festival recap

Example: a food festival cuts a 75-second recap to a 120 bpm track it has licensed for social and web use.

  • 0-8 s: cold open on a chef flipping a pan as the crowd cheers, before the music starts.
  • 8-20 s (intro): three long shots of gates opening and stalls setting up, cut every 4 bars.
  • 20-35 s (build): cut every bar: queues, sizzling grills, people laughing.
  • 35-50 s (chorus): fast cuts on downbeats: the busiest moments of the day.
  • 50-58 s (breakdown): music drops; one visitor says, "I've come back three years running."
  • 58-75 s (final chorus and outro): best remaining shots, then a wide aerial and next year's dates.

The pace changes four times, so it never feels like a slideshow on a click track.

A quick test before you publish

Watch the finished cut once with the sound off. If the pictures alone feel rhythmic and the story still makes sense, the edit is working. Then listen once with your eyes closed to check the music and speech levels. Both checks take two minutes and catch most problems.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing music after the edit.
  • Cutting on every beat from start to finish.
  • A long musical intro over logos.
  • Music too loud under speech.
  • Using a track you haven't licensed for the platforms you post on.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches pacing so videos don't drag or feel repetitive?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches cold opens and pacing for YouTube, which applies to music-led event edits. Your editor's help pages cover beat markers for free.

Should I cut on every beat?

No. Cut on the strong beats and section changes, and let some shots run longer, or the edit feels mechanical.

How do I find the beats?

Use your editor's beat detection if it has one, such as Premiere's mobile beat detection, or tap markers by hand while the track plays.

What tempo works for an event recap?

Match the event's mood. Faster tracks suit festivals and launches; slower ones suit galas and conferences.

Can I use a popular song?

Only with a licence that covers your use and platforms. Otherwise use licensed library music.

Should speech go over music?

Yes, with the music lowered under the words. Clear speech matters more than the beat.
