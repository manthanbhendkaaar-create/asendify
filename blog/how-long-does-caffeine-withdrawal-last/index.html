How long does caffeine withdrawal last?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 7 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Caffeine withdrawal usually lasts 2 to 9 days. Symptoms such as headache and tiredness tend to start 12 to 24 hours after your last caffeine and are worst at around 20 to 51 hours, according to a major review of the research. Cutting down gradually makes it shorter and milder for most people.

Key takeaways

  • Withdrawal typically starts within a day of stopping, peaks on day one to two and fades within about a week.
  • Headache is the best-known symptom, but tiredness, low mood, poor concentration and irritability are common too.
  • Even people who drink around 100 mg a day, about one cup of instant coffee, can get symptoms.
  • The more caffeine you usually have, the more likely and stronger the symptoms.
  • A slow taper over one to three weeks avoids most of the discomfort.

This page is general information, not medical advice. If you have severe or unusual symptoms, a headache that will not go away, or a health condition, see a doctor or GP.

The caffeine withdrawal timeline

The clearest evidence comes from Juliano and Griffiths, who reviewed decades of controlled studies for the journal Psychopharmacology. They found that symptoms generally begin 12 to 24 hours after stopping, reach their peak between 20 and 51 hours, and can last anywhere from 2 to 9 days (Juliano and Griffiths, 2004).

Time since last caffeineWhat many people notice
0 to 12 hoursUsually little or nothing. Caffeine is still clearing from your body.
12 to 24 hoursHeadache starts for many people, along with tiredness and lower alertness.
About day 1 to 2 (20 to 51 hours)Peak symptoms: headache, fatigue, low mood, fogginess, irritability.
Days 3 to 5Symptoms ease for most people. Energy starts to come back.
Days 6 to 9The last symptoms fade for those who had a longer withdrawal.

These are averages from studies. Your own timeline depends on how much you usually have, how suddenly you stop and how quickly your body clears caffeine.

What the symptoms are

The same review looked at 49 possible symptoms and found 10 that met its standards for being real withdrawal effects:

  • headache
  • fatigue or tiredness
  • reduced energy
  • reduced alertness
  • drowsiness
  • feeling less content
  • low or depressed mood
  • trouble concentrating
  • irritability
  • feeling foggy or not clear-headed

Flu-like symptoms, nausea and muscle pain or stiffness were also judged likely to be real. In the experimental studies, about half of participants got a headache, and 13% had symptoms bad enough to cause clear distress or to get in the way of normal life (Juliano and Griffiths, 2004).

The US Food and Drug Administration puts it plainly: caffeine withdrawal is not considered dangerous, but it can be unpleasant, and if you want to cut back it is best to do it gradually.

Why stopping causes symptoms

Caffeine works mainly by blocking adenosine, a chemical that builds up while you are awake and makes you feel sleepy. If you take caffeine every day, your body adapts to having it around. When you suddenly stop, that adaptation is still in place but the caffeine is not, so for a few days you feel more tired than usual and many people get a headache.

That adaptation also explains why your first coffee can feel so good. Part of the lift may simply be the end of overnight withdrawal, a point covered in our guide to whether your morning coffee is just reversing caffeine withdrawal.

How much caffeine does it take to get withdrawal?

Less than most people think. The review found that stopping intakes as low as 100 mg a day could produce symptoms, and that symptoms became more likely and more severe at higher daily doses (Juliano and Griffiths, 2004).

For a sense of scale, the NHS gives these typical amounts:

DrinkTypical caffeine
Cup of instant coffeeabout 100 mg
Cup of teaabout 75 mg
250 ml energy drinkabout 80 mg
Can of colaabout 40 mg
50 g bar of milk chocolateless than 10 mg

So someone who has one instant coffee and one tea a day is already at about 175 mg, enough for many people to notice a headache if they stop suddenly. For context, both the FDA and the European Food Safety Authority say up to 400 mg a day does not raise safety concerns for healthy adults, though pregnancy limits are lower.

How to make withdrawal shorter and easier

You cannot skip the adjustment entirely, but you can make it much gentler.

  1. Taper rather than stop. Cut your daily caffeine by roughly a quarter every few days. Many people get through with mild symptoms or none.
  2. Swap, do not just remove. Replace one regular cup with half-caf, then decaf, so the habit stays while the dose falls.
  3. Keep your timing steady. Have your remaining caffeine at the same time each morning so your body is not caught out.
  4. Sleep and drink water. Tiredness is worse when you are short of sleep, and dehydration can add to headaches.
  5. Plan around busy days. If you do stop suddenly, avoid doing it right before an exam, a long drive or a big deadline, because days one and two are usually the hardest.
  6. Check hidden caffeine. Cola, iced tea, energy drinks, pre-workout and some painkillers all add caffeine. If you forget them, your taper is not really a taper.

Example: quitting cold turkey versus tapering

Example: Tom drinks two mugs of filter coffee and an energy drink most days. Using typical figures, that is roughly 2 × 100 mg plus 80 mg, so about 280 mg a day.

Cold turkey. Tom stops on a Sunday night. By Monday afternoon, about 16 to 20 hours later, he has a dull headache. Tuesday and Wednesday are the worst: he feels foggy at work and irritable. By Friday he feels mostly normal again. That fits the 2 to 9 day window.

Taper. Instead, Tom drops the energy drink in week one (down to about 200 mg), swaps his second mug for decaf in week two (about 100 mg plus a few milligrams), and swaps the first mug for half-caf in week three (about 50 mg). Each step is a cut of 25 to 50%. He notices mild tiredness on a couple of afternoons but no real headache. It takes longer, but he keeps working normally throughout.

If he wants to keep some caffeine, he could stop at one half-caf in the morning. At that level, the daily total guidance is no longer a concern and his sleep is less likely to be affected.

When to see a doctor

Normal caffeine withdrawal is temporary. See a doctor or GP if:

  • your headache is very severe, sudden, or different from your usual headaches
  • symptoms last much longer than a couple of weeks
  • you have low mood that does not lift
  • you are cutting caffeine because of a heart condition, pregnancy or a medicine, and want advice on how quickly to do it

FAQ

How long does a caffeine withdrawal headache last?

For most people the headache starts within 12 to 24 hours of the last dose, is worst on day one or two, and eases within a few days. Some people have milder symptoms for up to about nine days.

Is it better to quit caffeine cold turkey or slowly?

Slowly is easier for most people. A gradual cut over one to three weeks usually avoids the headache and fogginess that come with stopping suddenly, and the FDA also suggests cutting back gradually.

Can decaf help with caffeine withdrawal?

Yes. Decaf still has a small amount of caffeine and keeps the habit in place, so swapping regular cups for half-caf and then decaf is a common way to taper.

Does caffeine withdrawal cause anxiety?

The best-supported symptoms are headache, tiredness, low mood, poor concentration and irritability. Some people describe feeling on edge, but if you have significant anxiety or low mood, speak to a GP.

Will a cup of coffee stop withdrawal symptoms?

Usually, yes. Taking caffeine again generally relieves the symptoms. That is why many people give up on quitting by day two, and why tapering works better.
