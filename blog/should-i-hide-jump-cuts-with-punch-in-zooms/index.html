Should I hide jump cuts in interview footage with punch-in zooms?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Yes, in moderation. A punch-in changes the framing across a cut, so a trimmed pause looks like a deliberate edit instead of a glitch. But a zoom on every cut becomes a pattern the viewer notices, and a slow zoom on every shot can feel mechanical. Cut at a change of idea, keep the zoom modest, vary the size and use B-roll or a second camera when you can. This is editing practice, not a research finding, so test it on your own retention graph.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's podcast guide says "dynamic editing keeps the viewer's eye engaged" (YouTube Blog). Punch-ins are one simple way to add movement to a single-camera interview.
  • Descript's guidance says editing out every filler can make a speaker sound "robotic and inauthentic" (Descript). The same logic applies to visuals: too many cuts and zooms can make footage feel over-processed.
  • YouTube Help says dips mean "viewers are abandoning or skipping" and a flat line means they watch "from start to finish" (YouTube Help). Use the graph to see whether your cutting style is helping.
  • Nothing cited here says that punch-ins improve retention. Treat the advice below as a technique to test.

Why jump cuts feel jarring

A jump cut happens when two takes of the same shot are joined and the subject appears to jump slightly. Our eyes notice the mismatch. A punch-in solves it by changing the shot size on the second clip, so the cut reads as a switch between two framings rather than a glitch. A second camera or a cutaway solves it more completely.

Step by step

  1. Edit for content first. Cut the sentences, repeats and pauses you do not need.
  2. Mark each cut. Note where the subject's position changes noticeably.
  3. Choose the cut point well. Cut between sentences or ideas, not mid-word. A cut at a natural pause hides better.
  4. Alternate two framings. For example, a wide shot and a punch-in to about 110 to 125 percent. Alternate them at the cuts. Do not go too tight, because quality drops and faces look soft.
  5. Vary the move. Use a static punch-in for some cuts and a slow push for emphasis points.
  6. Match the energy. Punch in on an important line, stay wide on a relaxed one.
  7. Use B-roll where you can. A cutaway covers the cut entirely and adds information.
  8. Keep a few straight cuts. Not every cut needs disguising, especially across a clear change of topic.
  9. Watch it back at full speed. If you notice the pattern, there are too many.

Technical points

  • Resolution. Shooting in 4K and delivering in 1080p gives room to punch in without losing sharpness.
  • Framing. Leave headroom so the crop does not cut off the top of the head. Check that the eyes stay about a third from the top.
  • Keyframes. For a slow push, keep the speed gentle and consistent.
  • Consistency. Use the same two or three zoom levels across the video.
  • Audio. Cuts need room tone so the audio does not stutter.

Check the result

  1. Look at your retention graph around heavily cut sections. Dips can mean the edit is distracting or the content is thin.
  2. Compare a punch-in-heavy video with a calmer one of similar length.
  3. Ask a colleague whether they noticed the cuts. If they did, reduce them.
  4. Keep what your audience tolerates and adjust the rest.

Common mistakes

  • Zooming at a fixed rhythm.
  • Zooming so far that footage looks soft.
  • Cutting mid-word.
  • Using a punch-in where a cutaway would add information.
  • Never watching the video at full speed.

Zoom settings that usually work

Editors differ, but a simple starting point is to pick two levels, a base framing and a punch-in at roughly 110 to 125 percent, and keep them consistent across the video. Set the zoom as a scale change on the clip rather than an animated move, so the change happens at the cut. For emphasis, add a slow push of a few percent over the length of a key sentence. Avoid fast zooms in and out, which draw attention to themselves.

If your editor supports adjustment layers or presets, save your two framings as presets. That makes the look consistent and speeds up long videos. When working with several editors, document the numbers in your style guide.

When not to punch in

  • On emotional or serious moments where a stable shot feels more respectful.
  • When the subject is gesturing with their hands and a zoom would crop them.
  • When the footage is already soft or compressed.
  • When the next shot is a cutaway anyway.
  • When the cut falls across a topic change, where a straight cut reads clearly.

A good test is to watch the video once without sound. If you can see a pattern of zooms, reduce them. If the cuts are invisible and the picture still has life, you are in the right range.

How much should I zoom?

Modestly, with a few set levels. Shooting at higher resolution helps.

Should I zoom on every cut?

No. Use it where it helps and vary your methods.

Is B-roll better?

Often, because it adds information as well as hiding the cut.

How do I know it works?

Compare retention for similar-length videos and ask viewers.
