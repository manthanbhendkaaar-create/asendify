How do I use caffeine to handle jet lag when travelling?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 29 October 2026 · Last updated 29 October 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Use caffeine to help you switch to the new time zone, not to fight it: take it only in the local morning and early afternoon at your destination, keep each serving at or below 200 mg, and stop early enough that it does not wreck your first nights of sleep there. Pair it with daylight, a set local bedtime and a short nap if you need one. Caffeine can make the first few days easier, but it does not reset your body clock on its own.

Before you fly, work out your caffeine cut-off for your destination bedtime with the free Caffeine Cutoff Calculator (enter the local time you plan to sleep), so you are not guessing while tired.

This page is education for healthy adults aged 18 and over. It is general information, not medical advice.

Why caffeine helps with jet lag, and where it stops

Jet lag happens because your internal body clock is still running on home time while the clock on the wall has moved. Caffeine blocks some of the signals that make you feel sleepy, so it can keep you alert through a local afternoon when your body thinks it is the middle of the night.

What it does not do is move your body clock in any reliable way you can plan around. Light, sleep timing and meal timing do most of that work. The NHS page on jet lag explains the condition and the general steps that help, such as adjusting your sleep and getting outside in daylight.

The big trap is timing. Caffeine stays in your body for hours (see how long caffeine stays in your system), so a "rescue" coffee at 6pm local time can keep you awake at midnight, which keeps you jet-lagged for longer.

Step 1: Decide your destination bedtime before you travel

Pick the local time you want to go to bed on the first night and stick to it. Everything else, including your caffeine, is planned backwards from that time. Treat that bedtime as fixed even if you feel wide awake or completely shattered.

Step 2: On the flight, keep caffeine light

  • If it is night-time at your destination: skip caffeine and try to rest. Use an eye mask and earplugs.
  • If it is daytime at your destination: a single coffee or tea is fine, but do not stack cups because the drinks trolley keeps coming round.
  • Drink water regularly. Never use alcohol to sleep on a flight, and never mix caffeine with alcohol.

Step 3: Day one at your destination

  1. Morning: get outside in daylight as soon as you can and have your first caffeine with breakfast. A regular mug of coffee or a couple of cups of tea is enough for most people.
  2. Early afternoon: if you hit a wall, a second small serving is reasonable, ideally well before mid-afternoon. A short nap of around 20 minutes is also fine, but set an alarm.
  3. Late afternoon and evening: no caffeine. Use daylight, a walk, a shower and food to stay up until your planned bedtime.

Keep each serving at or below 200 mg and your daily total at or below 400 mg from all sources. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) says these amounts do not raise safety concerns for healthy adults. Tired travellers often overshoot because airport and hotel coffees can be large, so count servings, not cups.

Step 4: Days two to four

Repeat the same pattern: caffeine in the local morning, maybe one small top-up early in the afternoon, nothing later. Resist the urge to "catch up" with bigger doses after a bad night. Higher doses make the next night worse, and the cycle drags on.

If you are only away for one or two days, it can make more sense to keep caffeine and sleep roughly on home time rather than switching fully. Decide which plan you are following before you go.

Flying east vs flying west

  • Flying east (for example, London to Dubai or Asia): you need to fall asleep earlier than your body wants. Late caffeine is the most common mistake here, so be strict with your cut-off.
  • Flying west (for example, London to New York): you need to stay up later. Morning and early-afternoon caffeine plus afternoon daylight usually helps you hold on until the local bedtime.

Example plan: London to New York, morning arrival

You leave London in the morning and land in New York around midday local time. On the flight, have one coffee with the first meal and then switch to water. After landing, get outside, eat a light meal, and have one more small coffee or tea in the early afternoon if you need it. Stop caffeine after that, stay up until around 10pm local time, and repeat the morning-only pattern the next day.

Who should avoid caffeine or ask first

Some people should avoid or limit caffeine and check with their doctor or pharmacist before using it to manage travel: anyone pregnant or breastfeeding (EFSA advises no more than 200 mg a day), anyone under 18, people with heart conditions or high blood pressure, people with anxiety or panic disorders, and anyone taking medicines that may interact with caffeine. If you take timed medication, ask your doctor how to adjust it across time zones. Never use caffeine powders or highly concentrated liquid caffeine.

Key takeaways

  • Fix your destination bedtime first and plan caffeine backwards from it.
  • Use caffeine only in the local morning and early afternoon.
  • Keep each serving at or below 200 mg and the daily total at or below 400 mg.
  • Daylight, sleep timing and short naps do most of the work; caffeine supports them.
  • Never mix caffeine with alcohol, including on the plane.

FAQ

Should I drink coffee on a long-haul flight?

Only if it is daytime at your destination. If it is night there, skip it and try to sleep, so you land closer to the local rhythm.

What time should I stop caffeine on the first day?

Base it on your local bedtime, not on the time back home. For a typical cup, many people do best stopping by early afternoon; the calculator above gives you a cut-off for your own bedtime.

Can I use energy drinks to get through jet lag?

They work in the same way as coffee but large cans can contain a lot of caffeine and sugar. If you use one, read the label and count it in your daily total.

How long does jet lag last?

It varies with how many time zones you cross and the direction of travel. The NHS jet lag page covers what to expect and when to get help.
