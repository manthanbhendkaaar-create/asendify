What should a trading journal template include?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A trading journal template should include trade details (date, market, direction, entry, stop, target, exit, size and fees), calculated results (profit or loss, money at risk and R-multiple), the reason for the trade (setup and screenshot) and your behaviour (emotion, mistakes, rules followed). It also needs a weekly summary, because the review is where a journal pays off.

This page is for education only, not financial advice. Trading is risky and you can lose money.

Key takeaways

  • Group the fields into four blocks: trade details, calculated results, the reason for the trade, and your behaviour.
  • Record the stop and target before you enter, or you cannot measure risk or planned reward honestly.
  • R-multiple (result divided by the risk you took) lets you compare trades of different sizes.
  • Tag mistakes with a short fixed list, so you can add up what each one costs.
  • A summary view by week and by setup matters as much as the trade log itself.

Trade details: record the plan, not just the result

The most common gap in home-made journals is the stop and target. Without them you can see that a trade made £120, but not whether that was a good result for the risk taken. Write the stop before you enter. If you use stop orders, remember that Investor.gov notes a stop order becomes a market order once triggered, and your fill may differ from the stop price in a fast market. Recording both the planned stop and the actual exit shows you how much slippage costs you.

Fees deserve their own column too. They look tiny on one trade, but they add up over hundreds of trades, and a journal without them overstates your results.

Calculated results: let the template do the maths

If you use a spreadsheet or Notion, the results block should calculate itself, so you never fudge a number after a bad day. Notion's help pages explain how formulas can calculate values from other properties in the same row, and how relations and rollups can pull those results into a summary database, for example total R per setup. A spreadsheet can do the same with simple cell formulas.

The key formulas:

ResultFormula (long trade)
Profit or loss(Exit − entry) × size − fees
Money at risk(Entry − stop) × size
R-multipleProfit or loss ÷ money at risk
Planned reward-to-risk(Target − entry) ÷ (entry − stop)
Win rateWinning trades ÷ all closed trades

For short trades, reverse the subtraction. Our guide to trading expectancy with R-multiples shows how these numbers combine to tell you whether a setup is worth keeping.

The reason for the trade

Name the setup from a short list you define in advance, such as "breakout", "pullback" or "range fade". Free-typed setup names drift over time ("pullback", "PB", "dip buy") and break your summaries. Attach a screenshot of the chart at entry. Weeks later, it shows whether the trade really matched your rules or whether you saw what you wanted to see.

Behaviour: the fields most templates leave out

FINRA's guide to frequent intraday trading warns that it comes with risks, "including losing some or all of your investment." Much of that loss, for many traders, comes from behaviour rather than strategy: moving a stop, doubling up after a loss, or chasing a move. The behaviour block makes those habits visible.

Keep two short, fixed lists:

  • Emotion: calm, confident, fearful, FOMO, revenge, bored, tired.
  • Mistakes: moved stop, no stop, oversized, early exit, late entry, chased, broke a rule, revenge trade.

Add a yes/no "followed my rules" box. Then compare your total profit or loss on rule-following trades with rule-breaking ones. That single comparison often tells you more than any indicator.

The weekly summary

A trade log on its own is a diary. A template also needs a summary that groups trades by week and by setup:

Summary itemWhat it shows
Net profit or lossWhether the week made or lost money
Number of tradesWhether you overtraded
Win rate and average RWhether results came from accuracy or size of wins
Money lost to mistakesThe total of trades tagged with a mistake
P&L, rules kept vs brokenWhat discipline is worth in money
One rule for next weekThe single change you will make

If you tend to take too many trades, read our page on how to stop overtrading, which uses the same weekly numbers.

Example: one trade logged in full

Example: Sam buys 100 shares at £25.00 with a stop at £24.00 and a target at £27.00. He exits at £26.50 and pays £6 in fees in total. He felt calm, followed his rules and tagged the setup "pullback".

FieldValue
Money at risk(£25.00 − £24.00) × 100 = £100
Planned reward-to-risk(£27.00 − £25.00) ÷ (£25.00 − £24.00) = 2.0
Profit or loss(£26.50 − £25.00) × 100 − £6 = £144
R-multiple£144 ÷ £100 = 1.44R
ResultWin
Followed rulesYes

Now imagine Sam's next trade: same setup, but he moves his stop lower when price falls, and loses £230, or −2.3R, against a planned £100 risk. Tagged "moved stop", that trade shows that the mistake cost him £130 more than his plan allowed. After a month, adding up the "moved stop" tags tells him exactly what that habit costs.

FAQ

What is the most important field in a trading journal?

The stop-loss price, because without it you cannot work out risk or R-multiple. The "followed my rules" box is a close second.

Should a trading journal include screenshots?

Yes, if you can. A chart screenshot at entry shows what you actually saw, which makes your weekly review far more honest.

How many fields is too many?

If logging a trade takes more than a couple of minutes, you will stop doing it. Keep the fields in the table above and add more only when you have a question they would answer.

Can I use a spreadsheet as a trading journal template?

Yes. A spreadsheet with the same fields and formulas works well. The main extra work is building summary tables by week and by setup.

What should I review each week?

Net result, number of trades, win rate, average R, money lost to mistakes, and your results on rule-following versus rule-breaking trades. End with one rule to change for next week.
