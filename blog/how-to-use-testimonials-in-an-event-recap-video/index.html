How do you use attendee testimonials in an event recap video?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Use testimonials as proof, not padding. Ask real attendees open questions that invite a specific story, film them with clean audio, and keep only the five-to-ten-second lines that say something concrete ("I rewrote our onboarding on the train home"). Place them right after a strong moment in the recap so they confirm what the viewer just saw, caption them, and never script, invent or misattribute what someone said.

Key takeaways

  • Vimeo's recap guide lists social proof as a best practice: ask attendees and speakers to share their experiences.
  • Cvent recommends lavalier mics for testimonials and warns "Bad audio will lose your audience."
  • Wistia's 2025 data puts testimonial videos under a minute at an average 46% engagement, a little below product videos (50%), so testimonials work best as short moments inside a recap.
  • Specific beats general: one concrete change someone made is worth more than five "it was amazing" clips.
  • Get consent for every testimonial, and never edit a clip to change its meaning.

Why testimonials help, and when they hurt

A recap shows what happened. A testimonial tells the viewer what it meant to someone like them. That is powerful for a viewer deciding whether to buy a ticket next year.

But a run of people saying "great event, really enjoyed it" is where viewers leave. Generic praise sounds like an advert and adds nothing new. Wistia's video length guide (2025) reports that testimonial videos under a minute average 46% engagement, compared with 50% for product videos. Testimonials hold attention when they are short, specific and surrounded by action.

Asking the right questions

Weak questionBetter questionWhy it works
Did you enjoy the event?What surprised you today?Invites a story, not a yes
Would you recommend it?Who should come next year, and why?Specific and future-facing
What was your favourite session?What will you do differently on Monday?Concrete change is strong proof
How was the networking?Tell me about someone you met today.Gets a story with a person in it

Ask people to repeat your question in their answer ("What surprised me was..."), so the clip works without your voice.

Filming them well

  • Audio first. Cvent's event video guide recommends lavalier mics for testimonials and recording room tone. A noisy clip is unusable however good the words.
  • Quiet spot, busy background. A soft-focus crowd behind them shows the event without drowning the voice.
  • Eye line just off camera. Ask them to talk to the interviewer, not the lens.
  • Film plenty. Cvent's own case study describes recording more than 60 testimonial clips in one day at a large event. You'll use a fraction.
  • Consent. Explain how the clip will be used, including ads if relevant. The Irish Data Protection Commission's guidance on event photos treats images of people as personal data.

Choosing and cutting

  1. Transcribe every clip. Highlight lines with a specific detail: a number, a name, an action.
  2. Cut to the line. Start on the strongest word, not on "So, um, yeah." Most usable testimonial moments are five to ten seconds.
  3. Mix voices. Different roles, ages and backgrounds make the proof feel broader.
  4. Don't change meaning. You can trim, but never splice words together to say something the person didn't.
  5. Caption them. Many people watch with the sound off.

Where to place them in a recap

The Retention Editing Playbook's cold open rule says the video should start on the most interesting moment. A testimonial is rarely that moment; a reaction or reveal usually is. Testimonials work best as the "proof" beat after something visual:

  • Show the keynote's big moment, then one attendee saying what they took from it.
  • Show a packed workshop, then one line about what someone built there.
  • Near the end, two or three quick lines that lead into the call to action.

Vimeo's recap guide suggests including social proof by asking attendees and speakers to share their experiences.

Example: from 40 clips to four

Example: a one-day product management conference films 40 short attendee interviews.

  • The editor transcribes them and marks 11 lines with a specific detail.
  • Four make the final 2-minute recap: a product lead who "cut our roadmap meeting from two hours to 30 minutes" after one session; a founder who met their first designer hire; a student who "finally understood pricing"; and a returning attendee saying why they came back.
  • Each is 6 to 9 seconds, captioned, placed after the related visual moment.
  • The other 36 clips are kept, with consent, for social posts and sales emails.

Honesty rules

Testimonials are claims about your event. Use real people, real words and real context. Don't present staff or paid speakers as ordinary attendees without saying so, and don't imply results the person didn't describe.

Quick checklist for testimonial interviews

  • Prepare four open questions that invite a specific story.
  • Bring a lavalier mic and test audio before every interview.
  • Explain how the clip will be used and record consent.
  • Ask people to repeat the question in their answer.
  • Note the timecode of standout lines while filming to speed up the edit.
  • Transcribe and pick lines with a concrete detail.
  • Caption every clip and keep the speaker's meaning intact.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches cold opens and where to place proof in a video?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches cold opens, Promise-Proof-Path and pacing. Vimeo's and Cvent's free guides are fair alternatives.

How long should a testimonial clip be?

Usually five to ten seconds in a recap. Longer clips suit a dedicated testimonial video.

How many testimonials should a recap have?

Often three or four strong, varied ones. More tends to slow the video down.

Should I open the recap with a testimonial?

Usually not. Open on your most striking visual moment, then use testimonials as proof.

Can I edit what someone said?

You can trim, but never combine or cut words in a way that changes the meaning.

Do I need written consent?

Clear, recorded consent is best, especially if the clip will appear in paid ads.
