How do I write a creative brief for a video editor that focuses on retention?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 25 November 2026 · Last updated 25 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A retention-focused brief names the viewer, the one action you want, the promise made by the title and thumbnail, and how you will judge the result on the retention graph. Add references, constraints and assets so the editor does not have to ask. Keep it to one page.

Key takeaways

  • Shootsta says a video needs one clear action: "if you have two actions, you need two videos", and that the key message should fit in one sentence.
  • Storyblocks lists goals, audience, key message, call to action, tone and references, technical specs and deadlines as the parts of a video brief.
  • YouTube Help describes typical retention as the engagement your last 10 videos of a similar length maintained, which gives a baseline to put in the brief.
  • Say how success will be read before the edit starts, so feedback later is about the graph, not taste.

Why a brief matters more for retention

A rough cut is only as good as the question the editor was given. If the brief says "make it engaging", the editor guesses. If it says who is watching, what they were promised and which moment you worry about, the editor can make choices. Shootsta puts it plainly: a vague audience makes the whole video vague, and generic direction such as "professional and engaging" gives the team nothing to aim at. It adds that leaving out the audience, action, message or tone is the most common cause of revisions.

For retention work, add three items beyond a standard brief: the promise made before the click, the target moments on the graph and the baseline you will compare with.

What to put in the brief

Storyblocks sets out the usual parts: goals, audience, key message, call to action, tone and style, references, technical specs and a clear timeline. Add the retention items below.

Steps to write it

  1. Write the title and thumbnail first. The promise is the first thing the editor must honour.
  2. Name the viewer. Use a specific description: "marketing managers at small software firms", not "business people".
  3. Choose one action. If you have two, make two videos.
  4. Write the key message in one sentence. If you cannot, you are not ready to brief.
  5. Describe the first 15 seconds. For example: "Show the finished result, then state the three steps."
  6. Give references with reasons. Per Shootsta's example, "like our customer case study, but for a tech audience" works better than "professional but approachable".
  7. State the baseline. For YouTube, give the typical retention or the Intro figure from recent videos; both are in Studio, per YouTube Help.
  8. Add constraints and assets. Length, legal wording, brand rules, deadlines, raw files and anything that must or must not appear.
  9. Say how feedback will work. For example, one consolidated set of timestamped notes per round.
  10. Send it before the footage. The editor should read the brief before they watch the rushes.

Example: a brief for a 7-minute product tutorial

Example: a software company sends this one-page brief. Viewer: operations managers at small logistics firms. Promise: title "How to cut manual data entry in your weekly report" with a thumbnail showing a finished report. Action: start a free trial. Key message: the weekly report can be built in three steps. First 15 seconds: show the finished report, say what it replaces, state the three steps. References: two earlier tutorials, with notes ("fast cuts, screen zoom, no intro animation"). Baseline: last five tutorials held a given share at the 30-second mark; the editor will compare. Constraints: 6 to 8 minutes, no pricing on screen, brand colours for captions. Assets: screen recordings, voiceover, product logo. Review: one set of timestamped notes after the first cut. This scenario is invented for illustration.

Common mistakes

Do not give a brief that lists adjectives only. Do not leave out the thumbnail and title, since the editor cannot match a promise they have not seen. Do not ask for several actions. Do not promise a retention number to the editor or the client; no brief can guarantee one. And do not keep the brief secret from the person who reviews the work: everyone should read the same page, then use the graph to judge, not taste.

How long should a video brief be?

One page is usually enough. Include the viewer, promise, action, message, references, constraints and review plan.

Should the brief include the title and thumbnail?

Yes. The opening needs to deliver what they promise.

Can I put a retention target in the brief?

Put a baseline and the moments you will check. Avoid promising a number, since no edit can guarantee one.

How many actions should a video have?

One. Shootsta says if you have two actions, you need two videos.
