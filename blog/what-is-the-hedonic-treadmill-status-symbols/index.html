What is the hedonic treadmill, and why do status symbols stop feeling special?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 3 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The hedonic treadmill is the tendency to get used to changes in our lives and drift back towards our usual level of happiness. A new car, watch or membership feels exciting at first, then becomes normal, and the next upgrade starts to look necessary. The term was coined in 1971; later research shows adaptation is real but uneven, and people differ a lot in how fully they adapt.

Key takeaways

  • Wikipedia's summary credits Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell with the term in 1971, describing a return to a fairly stable baseline after major events.
  • Diener, Lucas and Scollon (2006) revised the idea: baselines aren't neutral, they differ between people, and they can change.
  • Brickman, Coates and Janoff-Bulman's 1978 paper, "Lottery Winners and Accident Victims: Is Happiness Relative?", appeared in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (World Database of Happiness).
  • Psychologist Robert Biswas-Diener warns in Psychology Today that the study is often misread as proving full adaptation; later work found full adaptation did not always occur.
  • Status purchases are especially prone to the treadmill because their value depends on comparison.

Why status symbols adapt faster

Things whose value comes from comparison lose their shine when your comparison group catches up or moves on. That's why economists talk about positional goods: their worth depends on what others have. One purchase can also trigger others to match it, known as the Diderot effect.

Spreading spending across separate experiences gives novelty more than once, which is one common way people try to slow adaptation. Neither choice is wrong; the point is knowing which one is likely to fade. This is an illustrative example.

Ways people slow the treadmill

  1. Notice the anticipation. Planning can be part of the pleasure.
  2. Space out pleasures rather than buying everything at once.
  3. Change your comparison group away from people who always have more.
  4. Choose things that grow with use, such as skills and relationships.
  5. Be honest about status. If what you want is the signal, know that it will fade.

Who came up with the hedonic treadmill?

Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell coined the term in 1971.

Do lottery winners end up no happier?

The 1978 study is often quoted that way, but it is commonly misread; later research shows adaptation varies.

Can you get off the hedonic treadmill?

Not entirely, but spacing out pleasures and changing comparisons can slow it.

Is the hedonic treadmill the same for bad events?

People adapt to many negative events too, but research suggests not always fully, especially for serious ones like disability or unemployment.
