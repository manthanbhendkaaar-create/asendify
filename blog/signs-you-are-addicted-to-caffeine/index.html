What are the signs you are addicted to caffeine?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The main signs you are dependent on caffeine are withdrawal symptoms such as headache or fogginess when you skip it, wanting to cut down but not managing to, and carrying on even though caffeine is clearly hurting your sleep, anxiety or health. Researchers treat these three as the core signs, and most regular users show one or two rather than all three.

Key takeaways

  • Caffeine dependence is real, but "caffeine use disorder" is not yet an official diagnosis. The DSM-5 lists it as a condition for further study.
  • The three core signs researchers use: withdrawal, failed attempts to cut down, and continued use despite harm.
  • Withdrawal can appear after stopping as little as 100 mg a day, about one cup of instant coffee.
  • In one US survey, 56% of caffeine users wanted to cut down, but no more than 9% were likely to meet all three core signs.
  • Needing caffeine every morning is common. It becomes a problem when it controls your sleep, mood or health.

This page is general information, not medical advice. If you have palpitations, chest pain, severe anxiety or ongoing sleep problems, see a doctor or GP.

Is caffeine addiction real?

Yes, in the sense that people can become physically dependent on caffeine and find it hard to stop. But the language matters. A comprehensive review of caffeine use disorder by Meredith and colleagues explains that the DSM-5, the main US psychiatric manual, does not recognise caffeine use disorder as an official diagnosis. It lists it as a condition for further study. The ICD-10, used internationally, does include a caffeine dependence syndrome.

For most people, "addicted" really means "dependent": your body has adapted to daily caffeine and complains when it does not get it.

The signs researchers look for

The proposed criteria in the Meredith review require a problem pattern that causes real distress or impairment, with three core criteria that must all be present:

Core signWhat it looks like day to day
Wanting to cut down but failingYou have tried to drink less several times and slipped back within days
Using despite harmYou keep having late coffees even though you know they wreck your sleep, or caffeine makes your anxiety or reflux worse
WithdrawalHeadache, tiredness or fog when you skip it, or you drink caffeine to stop those symptoms

The review lists six further signs that can count towards the pattern: taking more than you meant to, spending a lot of time on caffeine, failing to meet obligations, continuing despite social problems, tolerance and craving.

Withdrawal: the clearest physical sign

Withdrawal is the best-studied sign. A review of the experimental research found symptoms typically start 12 to 24 hours after the last dose, peak at around 20 to 51 hours and last 2 to 9 days (Juliano and Griffiths, 2004). The common symptoms are:

  • headache
  • tiredness and low energy
  • drowsiness and low alertness
  • low mood and irritability
  • trouble concentrating
  • feeling foggy

The same review found withdrawal could appear after stopping intakes as low as 100 mg a day. So you do not need to be a heavy user to be dependent.

Tolerance: needing more for the same effect

Tolerance is listed among the additional signs. If your two coffees no longer wake you up, and you are now on four, that is tolerance. It is common and not harmful in itself, but it often comes with a creeping daily total and later caffeine, which can hurt sleep. Our page on resetting caffeine tolerance explains how to bring it down.

How common is caffeine dependence?

The figures depend a lot on who is studied. The Meredith review summarises several studies:

Group studiedFinding
US caffeine users (Vermont, 162 people)56% wanted to cut down; 18% reported withdrawal; no more than 9% likely met all three core signs
Italian general population (104 people)6% met the caffeine dependence criteria
People seeking treatment for caffeine problems (94 people)79% met the DSM-5 criteria, but this group was selected for problems

So wanting to cut down is very common, but meeting all the core signs is much less so.

How much is too much?

Dependence and safety are different questions. The US Food and Drug Administration says 400 mg a day is an amount not generally linked to negative effects in healthy adults. The European Food Safety Authority gives the same daily figure, plus up to 200 mg in a single dose, and up to 200 mg a day in pregnancy. You can be dependent on 150 mg a day and still be well within safe limits. You can also be over the limit without feeling dependent.

Example: a quick self-check

Example: Jess, 29, works in marketing. She has a large coffee on waking, another at 11 am, an energy drink at 3 pm and sometimes a cola at dinner. Rough total: 2 × 100 mg + 80 mg + 40 mg = 320 mg.

Running through the core signs:

  • Withdrawal? Yes. On holiday she had a pounding headache on day two when she could not find coffee.
  • Failed to cut down? Yes. She has tried to drop the energy drink three times this year.
  • Using despite harm? Partly. She knows the 3 pm energy drink makes it harder to sleep but keeps having it.

She is under 400 mg a day, so not over the safety guidance, but she shows signs of dependence. A sensible plan would be to move the energy drink to before 1 pm for two weeks, then swap it for tea, and cut the second coffee to half-caf after that. Tapering by about a quarter every few days is usually easier than stopping outright.

What to do if you think you are dependent

  1. Measure it. Write down every caffeine drink for three days, including cola, tea and pre-workout.
  2. Set a cut-off. Pick a last-caffeine time at least eight hours before bed and keep it for a week.
  3. Taper, do not stop dead. Cut by roughly a quarter every few days to avoid withdrawal.
  4. Swap, do not just remove. Half-caf, decaf and herbal tea keep the habit while the dose falls.
  5. Get help if needed. If caffeine is linked to heart symptoms, panic, or you cannot cut down despite real harm, talk to a GP.

FAQ

Can you be addicted to caffeine?

You can become dependent, with withdrawal and difficulty cutting down. Caffeine use disorder is listed in the DSM-5 as a condition for further study, not yet a formal diagnosis.

How quickly do you become dependent on caffeine?

Researchers have not set a fixed timeline, but withdrawal has been seen after stopping regular intakes as low as 100 mg a day. Daily use is the main driver.

Is needing coffee every morning a sign of addiction?

It can be a sign of mild dependence, especially if you get a headache without it. On its own, it is very common and not necessarily a problem.

Is the caffeine quiz a test for addiction?

No. The free caffeine quiz is a 1-minute self-check that sorts your habits into one of four types and suggests first changes. It does not diagnose dependence.

How do I stop being dependent on caffeine?

Taper gradually over one to three weeks, swap some drinks for decaf or herbal tea, keep a firm cut-off time and protect your sleep.
