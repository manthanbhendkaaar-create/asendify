What are the most common mistakes with agency case studies?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 26 November 2026 · Last updated 26 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The most common agency case study mistakes are publishing without the client's permission, giving results with no numbers, using jargon, making the agency the hero instead of the client and leaving out a call to action. The UK advertising rules also require evidence that any testimonial is genuine and permission to use it.

Key takeaways

  • Mailchimp describes a case study as the challenge, the solution and the results, backed by specific metrics, percentages, timeframes and before-and-after comparisons, with client quotes for third-party validation.
  • AgencyAnalytics recommends contacting clients before writing about them, sharing the draft before publishing, and treating the client as the hero. It suggests roughly 700 to 1,200 words.
  • The ASA's testimonial guidance says marketers must hold documentary evidence that a testimonial is genuine and the giver's contact details, must not use a testimonial without permission and must not mislead through editing or context.
  • The ASA also says marketers must not pose as consumers; never write a review of your own work as a client.
  • A case study with no numbers and no client approval is a liability, not an asset.

A simple structure

  1. Background: who the client is and what they wanted.
  2. Challenge: the specific problem, with a number if you have one.
  3. Solution: what you did, in plain steps, and why you chose it over alternatives.
  4. Results: before-and-after numbers over a stated period.
  5. Client quote: with written permission.
  6. Next step: how a reader can talk to you.

Permission and evidence

AgencyAnalytics advises contacting current or former clients before writing about them and showing them the draft before publishing. If a client wants privacy, you can anonymise the case study by describing the company by industry. The ASA rules go further for testimonials: keep documentary evidence that a quote is genuine and the giver's contact details, do not edit it so it misleads, and never present yourself as a customer. A signed, dated email is typically enough, though check the ASA page for what it accepts.

Example: a channel retention case study

Example: an editing agency wants to write up a project for a cooking channel. It asks the owner first and gets an email saying yes. It writes: the goal (keep viewers past the 30-second mark), the challenge (average view duration of 3:10 on 9-minute videos), what changed (a re-cut hook and chapters), and the result over eight weeks (average view duration of 4:05 on comparable videos). It adds one sentence on an alternative it rejected, a longer intro, and a quote from the owner. It shares the draft before publishing and saves the approval email. The figures are invented for illustration.

Honest limits

A case study shows one client's result, not a promise. Say what else changed during the period, such as seasonality or a viral video, and do not suggest the same result for every client. Check the ASA's advertising rules if you use quotes or claims.
