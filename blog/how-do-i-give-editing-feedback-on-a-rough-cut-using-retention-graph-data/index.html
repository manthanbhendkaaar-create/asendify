How do I give editing feedback on a rough cut using retention graph data?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Tie every note to a moment in the rough cut and to what the graph of your previous videos showed: name the timestamp, say what a viewer probably does there, suggest one change and say how you will check it after publishing. A rough cut has no graph of its own, so the data comes from earlier uploads and from a test viewer. Feedback that says "I felt bored here" is opinion; feedback that says "this is the same setup as the 0:40 dip in the last video, so try cutting the second example" gives the editor something to act on.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's retention report says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" and "gradual declines mean that viewers are losing interest over time" (YouTube Help). Those two shapes need different notes: a dip points to one moment, a slope points to pace.
  • The same page says "spikes appear when more viewers are watching, rewatching, or sharing those parts of your video". Tell the editor what to keep as well as what to cut.
  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded, rather than included" (Dartmouth). It is a useful test for any shot or line a note wants removed.
  • Wistia says "engagement naturally declines after the five-minute mark" (Wistia), so a note on a long cut should ask whether a section earns its place, not whether the video is "too long".

Why graph-based notes work better than taste

A rough cut is the first full assembly: the structure is there, the pacing is loose, and the editor is close to the material. Both people involved have a bias. The editor knows what every shot cost to make. The reviewer reacts to what they like. Neither is the viewer.

Retention data gives you a third voice. It cannot tell you what to do, but it shows where earlier viewers left or stayed. Used with care, it turns "I do not like the middle" into a checkable claim. It also keeps the conversation friendly, because the note is about the audience, not the editor's taste.

The limits matter. Last month's graph describes a different video. A small channel has few views, so a single dip may be noise. Treat the graph as a pattern across several uploads, and treat a note as a hypothesis, not an order.

Match the shape on the graph to the type of note

What the old graph showedLikely meaningType of note on the rough cut
Steep drop in the first 30 secondsThe opening did not match the promiseHook and first-minute notes
One sharp dip mid-videoA specific moment lost peopleCut, shorten or re-order that moment
Steady slope all the wayPace or interest wears downTighten, add re-hooks, vary visuals
Flat stretchPeople watched that part throughProtect it; do not trim for the sake of it
SpikeViewers rewatched or shared itKeep it; consider giving it more room
Dip at a section boundaryThe bridge was weakRewrite the transition line

YouTube describes a flat line as viewers "watching that part of your video from start to finish" and an intro measure as the share still watching after the first 30 seconds (YouTube Help). Use both when you review the first minute.

Step by step: write the feedback

  1. Open the last two or three graphs next to the rough cut. Note the repeating shapes, not one-off ones.
  2. Watch the rough cut once without stopping. Mark only the places you reached for your phone or lost the thread. Time them.
  3. Watch again with notes open. For each mark, write the timestamp, what is on screen and what is being said.
  4. Name the likely cause. Pick one: repeated information, slow setup, unclear point, weak transition, long shot, flat audio, unrelated detour.
  5. Link it to the graph if it fits. "Same position as the dip in the last upload, where we also did a long recap" is useful. If there is no link, say so.
  6. Suggest one change, not three. "Cut the second example" or "start on the result" is easier to act on than a list of options.
  7. Say what to keep. Mark the moment you expect people to rewatch or enjoy so the editor does not trim it.
  8. Rank the notes. Put the first minute and the biggest dip first; label small taste notes as optional.
  9. Agree how to check. After publishing, compare the same moment on the new graph and write down what moved.
  10. Keep a log. A short table of note, change and result helps the next rough cut.

Example: a creator sends a 9-minute rough cut of a software tutorial to an editor. The last three uploads each dipped at around the 40% mark, where there was a long explanation before the demonstration. The reviewer writes six notes: start on the finished result; cut a 50-second recap at 2:15; move the demo up to 3:30; add a clear label at each step; keep a short joke at 6:00 because the earlier video's joke had a spike; and check the 40% position on the new graph. After upload they compare the same position and note what moved. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

What to avoid

  • Treating the graph as a verdict. One video is a sample; a pattern across uploads is stronger.
  • Notes that are about taste. Say what a viewer does, not what you like.
  • Too many notes. Ten notes of equal weight hide the three that matter.
  • Hiding the data. Share the graph with the editor so they can see what you see.
  • Blaming people. The note concerns the cut, not the editor.
  • Chasing an average. A 40% retention at the end can be good for a long video and poor for a short one; compare with your own earlier uploads.

After the next upload

Return to your log. For each note, check the moment on the new graph and write one line: improved, unchanged or worse, with what you think caused it. Keep changes that helped and drop changes that did not. If you changed several things at once, do not claim which one worked.

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, open loops and pacing with templates, a tracker and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. It does not promise results.

Can I use the graph if the channel is new?

Use it lightly. With few views the graph is noisy, so lean on a test viewer and look for patterns across uploads.

How many notes should I give?

Aim for the three to six that matter most and mark the rest as optional.

Should the editor see the graph?

Yes. Sharing it keeps the note about viewers rather than taste.

How do I know a change worked?

Compare the same moment on the next graph and record the result; do not credit one change when you made several.
