How does the YouTube algorithm decide what to recommend?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 18 November 2026 · Last updated 18 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

YouTube recommends videos based on each viewer, not on a fixed formula. It uses the viewer's watch and search history and subscriptions, then signals about each video: whether people click (click-through rate), how long they watch (average view duration) and whether they are satisfied (likes, dislikes and surveys). Clicks alone count for little; videos people watch and rate well get shown more.

Key takeaways

  • Watch history is the main input for homepage recommendations; Up Next is based mainly on the video being watched.
  • YouTube's VP of Engineering wrote in 2021 that clicks proved unreliable, so watch time was added in 2012 and survey-based "valued watchtime" later.
  • Video-level signals include appeal (click-through rate), engagement (average view duration) and satisfaction (likes, dislikes and surveys).
  • There is no single formula: the weight of each signal varies by viewer.
  • For creators, the practical lever is simple to say and hard to do: make videos the right people click and are glad they watched.

What YouTube itself says

YouTube has published several explanations. The most detailed is the 2021 post On YouTube's recommendation system by Cristos Goodrow, YouTube's VP of Engineering. It traces the system's history:

  • 2008: recommendations started, largely ranking popular videos.
  • 2011: YouTube found clicks were an unreliable signal, because people often clicked videos that did not deliver.
  • 2012: watch time was added. Views initially dropped by about 20%.
  • Later: surveys asking viewers to rate videos from 1 to 5 stars were used to measure "valued watchtime".

The post says recommendations drive more viewing than subscriptions or search, that the system draws on over 80 billion signals, and that it does not follow a fixed formula: the weight of each signal varies by viewer.

The signals, grouped

YouTube's help page How YouTube recommendations work lists the viewer-side signals: watch history (the main input for the homepage), search history, subscriptions, likes and dislikes, "not interested" and "don't recommend channel" feedback, and satisfaction surveys. YouTube's How YouTube recommends videos overview adds context signals and video performance signals.

Where recommendations appear

The help page explains that the homepage is driven mainly by watch history, while Up Next is based mainly on the video someone is currently watching. So a video can be found in two ways: on the homepage of people whose history suggests they will enjoy it, and next to related videos they are already watching.

Content YouTube recommends less

Recommendations are not neutral about every kind of video. Goodrow's post says that from 2019 YouTube began demoting borderline content, meaning material that comes close to breaking the Community Guidelines, and that such content is also not monetised under the advertiser-friendly guidelines. For news and information, authoritative sources are promoted more heavily.

YouTube's recommendations overview gives numbers for that change: a 2019 update led to a 70% drop in watch time on non-subscribed recommended borderline content in the US that year, the change reached every market by March 2021, and YouTube's stated goal is to keep views of non-subscribed recommended borderline content below 0.5%. If a video sits close to the line, expect it to be shown far less, however well it performs on clicks.

What this means for creators

  1. Packaging earns the test. Without clicks, YouTube has nothing else to measure. A clear thumbnail and title matter.
  2. Retention earns the reach. Watch time was added because clicks alone misled the system. A video that loses most viewers in the first 30 seconds sends a weak signal.
  3. Satisfaction keeps it going. Surveys, likes and dislikes reward videos that deliver what the title promised. Clickbait tends to backfire.
  4. Know your audience. Because recommendations are personal, a video made for everyone is often recommended to no one in particular.
  5. Ignore "hacks". YouTube's own explanations describe viewer-level signals, not upload-time tricks or tag stuffing.

Common myths

MythWhat YouTube's explanations suggest
"The algorithm hates small channels"Signals are about each video and viewer; small channels can be recommended when viewers respond
"More clicks = more reach"YouTube moved away from clicks because they were unreliable
"Length is what matters"Watch time and satisfaction matter, not length on its own
"One formula ranks everything"The 2021 post says there is no fixed formula; weights vary by viewer

Worked example

Example: a fitness creator uploads two videos in the same week.

  • Video A, "I Tried the Hardest Workout on YouTube": CTR 9%, but most viewers leave within 40 seconds because the workout only starts at 3:30. Comments say "clickbait".
  • Video B, "20-Minute Dumbbell Workout for Beginners (No Jumping)": CTR 4%, but viewers watch most of it and many like it.

Given YouTube's description of its signals, Video B is the stronger candidate for continued recommendations: lower appeal, but far better engagement and satisfaction. These figures are an illustration, not measured data.

FAQ

Is there a self-paced course on hooks, pacing and structure for YouTube?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Accelerator is self-paced with instant access and six modules. YouTube's free help pages and your own analytics are alternatives.

Does YouTube use watch time to recommend videos?

Yes. Watch time was added as a signal in 2012, and average view duration is listed as an engagement signal.

Do likes and comments help?

Likes, dislikes and surveys are listed as satisfaction signals. YouTube's overview does not list comments separately.

Why does Up Next show different videos from my homepage?

Up Next is based mainly on the video you are watching; the homepage is based mainly on your watch history.

Can I reset my recommendations?

Yes. YouTube says turning off and deleting watch history stops history-based homepage recommendations.
