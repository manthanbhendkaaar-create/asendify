What does the "Most replayed" graph on YouTube mean, and how can creators use it?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The "Most replayed" graph is the faint grey wave that appears above a YouTube video's progress bar when you scrub through it. The higher the wave, the more that part of the video has been replayed; the highest peak is labelled "Most replayed". YouTube rolled it out to everyone in May 2022. For creators it is a free, public view of what viewers rewatch, on your own videos and on other channels' videos, which makes it a useful companion to the retention graph in YouTube Studio.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube describes it simply: "If the graph is high, then that part of the video has been replayed often" (TechCrunch, May 2022).
  • It started as a Premium experiment and became available to all users on desktop, iOS and Android.
  • MacRumors noted the thumbnail preview also marks the "Most replayed" segment, and the feature targets longer videos or those without chapters.
  • In YouTube Studio, spikes are "moments in your video that were rewatched or shared", which can mean the part was great, or confusing.
  • Use it to find what viewers value, then make more of that and move it earlier.

Most replayed vs the Studio retention graph

The two graphs answer different questions.

What it showsWhich parts get replayed mostWhat share of viewers are still watching at each moment
Shows drop-off?NoYes, dips and slopes
Shows rewatching?Yes, peaksYes, as spikes

The retention graph tells you where people leave. Most replayed tells you where people come back. Together they show what to cut and what to keep. Our explainer on the YouTube retention graph covers the Studio side in detail.

What a peak usually means

A peak means many viewers dragged back or seeked to that moment. Common reasons:

  • A payoff. The reveal, the result, the punchline, the moment the title promised.
  • Something hard to follow. A fast explanation, a number on screen for one second, a step people missed. YouTube's own help page warns that spikes can mean viewers had to rewatch because the content was unclear.
  • Something people want to copy. A recipe step, a settings screen, a chord shape, a workout move.
  • Something people want to share or quote. A clip-worthy line or moment.
  • A skip target. If viewers jump straight to one section, that section will show a peak too.

To tell these apart, watch the moment yourself and read the comments with timestamps. "Wait, what did he type at 4:12?" means it was unclear. "4:12 got me" means it was a highlight.

How to use it on your own videos

  1. List your peaks. For your last ten videos, write down where the Most replayed peak sits and what happens there.
  2. Sort them. Mark each as payoff, unclear, reference or skip target.
  3. Fix the unclear ones. Slow down, add an on-screen label, or hold the shot longer.
  4. Move payoffs earlier. If the best moment is at 9:00 in a 10-minute video, tease it in the opening seconds.
  5. Turn reference peaks into assets. If everyone rewinds to your settings screen, put the settings in the description or pin them in a comment.
  6. Cut Shorts from payoff peaks. A moment people replay is often a strong standalone Short.

How to use it on other channels' videos

Because the graph is public, it is a fair research tool. Open the most viewed videos in your niche and note where peaks sit. You will often see patterns: viewers replaying the results of a test, a before-and-after, or a single dramatic moment. That tells you what your shared audience values. Do not copy the content; learn the structure.

Example: a cooking channel

A 14-minute curry recipe video shows:

TimeWhat happensMost replayedStudio retention
0:00-1:10Story about a holidayFlatSteep drop
3:40Spice quantities shown on screen for 2 secondsHighest peakSmall spike
8:20Tasting reactionSecond peakLine flattens
12:00Washing up, outroFlatGradual decline

The quantities peak at 3:40 is a reference peak: people are rewinding to read them. The fix is to hold the quantities on screen for five or six seconds and put the full recipe in the description. The holiday story should move to the end or go. The tasting reaction is a payoff and belongs in the first 20 seconds as a tease. None of these changes need extra filming.

Limits of the graph

  • Not every video shows it. It depends on having enough viewing data.
  • It has no numbers. You see relative height, not counts.
  • It mixes reasons. Rewatches for confusion and rewatches for joy look the same.
  • It reflects viewers who stayed. Moments after a big drop-off are judged by a smaller group.

Why don't I see the Most replayed graph on my video?

It needs enough viewing data, so new or low-view videos often do not show it.

Can I turn Most replayed off for my videos?

YouTube has not offered creators a setting to hide it.

Is Most replayed the same as audience retention?

No. Retention shows how many viewers are still watching; Most replayed shows which parts get rewatched.

Does a big peak mean a part was good?

Not always. It can be a highlight, or a moment people did not understand. Check comments and watch it yourself.

Should I make Shorts from the Most replayed moment?

It is often a good candidate, as long as the moment makes sense without the rest of the video.
