What pricing mistakes do YouTube and video agencies make, and how do you fix them?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The most common agency pricing mistakes are setting prices from cost alone, ignoring non-billable time, not tracking profit per client, letting scope creep run, and never revisiting retainers. Fix them by pricing from real time data, capping revisions in writing, and reviewing every client's margin at least once a year.

Key takeaways

  • Cost-plus pricing covers your bills but ignores what the work is worth to the client, and copying competitors' prices leads to "a race to the bottom", according to Toggl's agency pricing guide.
  • Non-billable time (admin, meetings, sales calls) is a hidden cost, and the same guide says that without time tracking, pricing is "just theory".
  • In a survey quoted by Productive, project fees were the most used model in digital agencies at just under 50% of revenue, retainers 44%, hourly 30% and value-based 10%. Totals pass 100% because agencies mix models.
  • Teamwork lists the usual trap in each model: no buffer in fixed fees, open-ended work on retainers, and unpredictable revenue on hourly billing.
  • The model matters less than knowing your real margin per client.

Why video work is easy to misprice

Video is hard to scope. A "10-minute YouTube edit" can take two hours or twelve, depending on footage quality, how many revisions the client wants and how much sound work is needed. Hourly billing hides the problem but gives little incentive to finish quickly, and a flat fee per video punishes you when the raw footage is messy. A good middle route is a fixed fee per video with a stated number of revision rounds and a stated limit on raw footage length.

Example: two clients on the same fee

Example: an agency charges two channels £2,000 a month each for a set of monthly videos. Channel A sends clean footage and approves in one round, taking 20 hours. Channel B sends hours of unstructured footage and asks for three rounds on every video, taking 50 hours. The effective rates are £100 an hour and £40 an hour. If both look identical on the invoice, only per-client time tracking shows that Channel B is unprofitable. The numbers are illustrative, but the pattern is the one Toggl describes with its two-client comparison: the same fee can hide very different margins.

Possible responses with Channel B: cap raw footage per month, add a paid extra-revision rate, or move them to a higher tier. If none works, ending the contract may be the right call, after checking its notice terms.

How to review your own pricing in one afternoon

  1. List your last 10 projects with fee and total hours, including calls and revisions.
  2. Work out the effective hourly rate for each (fee divided by hours).
  3. Mark any project below your target as a pricing or scoping problem, not a client problem.
  4. Check which clients drain time through repeated revisions.
  5. Rewrite the scope and revision limits on your next proposal, and add a buffer to estimates.
  6. Put a yearly review in your calendar, and retainers on a quarterly one.

FAQ

Which pricing model is best for a video agency?

None is best for everyone. Fixed fees per video with stated revisions suit clear scopes; retainers suit ongoing work with defined deliverables. Value-based pricing is harder to start with because you need data on the results you deliver.

How often should an agency raise prices?

Review at least once a year, and retainers ideally every quarter, because wages and other costs rise. Even a small rise is better than letting inflation cut your margin.

Should I discount the first project to win a client?

Usually not. Toggl's guide says underpricing the first job does not build trust. A small paid pilot at your normal rate is a safer way to start.

How do I stop scope creep?

Write the included revisions, deliverables and footage limits into the contract, and give extras a price. Track revisions per client so you can see the drift early.

What if a client is unprofitable?

Compare fee against total hours. Try changing scope, adding limits or raising the fee. If that fails, end the relationship properly, following the contract's notice terms.
