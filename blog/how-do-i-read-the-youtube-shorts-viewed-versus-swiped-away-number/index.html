How do I read the YouTube Shorts viewed versus swiped away number?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 7 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The viewed versus swiped away number tells you how many viewers stayed past the first seconds of a Short and how many moved on. It measures your opening, not whether people finished. Read it next to how much of the Short people watched, compare it with your own recent Shorts rather than a universal target, and use it to decide whether to fix the first frame or the delivery.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube Help says views are counted "the moment a video starts to play across all formats, including Shorts", and defines "Stayed to watch" as "the percentage of times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short" (YouTube Help). A view is not the same as someone who stayed.
  • A guide to Shorts analytics notes that neither label means the viewer completed the Short, and that "there is no universal percentage that guarantees wider distribution" (ContentStudio). Your own Shorts are the benchmark.
  • In long videos, YouTube says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). The same idea applies to Shorts: the swipe is a dip at the very start.
  • Wistia reports that "videos under a minute average a 52% engagement rate" (Wistia). Short videos can hold about half of a viewer's time, so the opening has little room for slow starts.

What the number actually measures

When a Short appears in the feed, the view counts as soon as it starts to play. Many of those viewers leave almost at once, because swiping is effortless. The viewed versus swiped away split separates the people who stayed beyond the opening seconds from those who moved on. It is therefore a measure of the first moment: does the first frame, the first line and the first movement give a viewer a reason to stay?

It does not tell you whether the Short was good overall. A Short can keep a high share of viewers past the opening and still lose them at second 12. It can also lose many at the start and still hold the ones who stay all the way to the end. That is why the number is only half of the picture.

How to read it with the rest of the report

What you seeWhat it suggestsWhat to test first
Low stayed-to-watch, strong viewing depthThe opening loses people, but those who stay like itA clearer first frame; a more specific promise in the first line
High stayed-to-watch, weak viewing depthThe opening works; the middle or payoff loses peopleAn earlier payoff; a shorter explanation; a cut in the middle

High on bothThe Short is doing its jobMake a close variant and compare
Stayed-to-watch high, but few viewsNot many were shown the Short, or the topic is narrowTest the topic and title, not the edit

The ContentStudio guide adds a caution worth keeping: a low rate "identifies a problem area, not its exact cause". Do not rewrite the whole Short from one number. Look at the footage first.

Step-by-step: use the number to improve the next Short

  1. Open the Short's analytics in YouTube Studio and find the viewed versus swiped away figure for the Short you are checking.
  2. Collect your last 10 to 15 Shorts in a simple sheet with the figure, the average view duration and the opening you used (face, text, motion, question).
  3. Compare like with like. Compare Shorts of similar length and topic. A 15-second tip and a 55-second story will not behave the same.
  4. Find your own range. Note your lowest, typical and highest figures. Your typical figure is the baseline.
  5. Sort by opening type. Which openings sit at the top of your range: a face and a claim, a text hook, a visual surprise, a question?
  6. Watch the first three seconds of your weakest Short with the sound off. Is it clear what the Short is about from the picture alone?
  7. Change one thing. Rewrite the first line, move the strongest frame to the start, or cut a slow intro. One change, so you can tell what worked.
  8. Check viewing depth. If the opening figure rises but the viewing depth falls, the promise is now bigger than the delivery. Pay it off sooner.
  9. Do not chase a single Short. Wait for a handful of Shorts with the new opening before drawing a conclusion.
  10. Write down the result and keep the opening that wins as your default.

What a good opening usually contains

  • The subject in the first frame. Show what the Short is about, not a logo or a blank background.
  • A first line that promises something specific. "Three cuts that fixed my intro" says more than "Here is something useful".
  • Movement or change in the first second. A cut, a hand entering the frame or text appearing gives the eye something to follow.
  • Captions from the start. The W3C notes captions can convey dialogue and "meaningful sound effects" (W3C); many people watch Shorts without sound, and captions let them follow from the first word.
  • One idea. A Short that tries to cover three points gives viewers three chances to leave.

What not to do with this number

  • Do not treat it as a score to maximise at any cost. A misleading first frame can raise the figure and then lose viewers a second later, which damages trust and the rest of the report.
  • Do not copy a rival's figure. You cannot see theirs, and any number quoted online is not a rule.
  • Do not judge it alone. A figure without viewing depth can mislead in both directions.
  • Do not change several things at once. You will not know which change worked.

Example: ten Shorts from a cooking channel

Example: a creator posts ten 30-second Shorts in two weeks. Five open with a wide shot of the kitchen and "Today I am making pasta". Five open with the finished dish close to the camera and the line "This takes eight minutes". She notes the stayed-to-watch figure for each, and the average view duration. The five with the finished dish in the first frame sit higher on stayed-to-watch, but two of them fall away at second 20 because the method is rushed. She keeps the opening, slows the method slightly and cuts one step. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

After you change the opening

Give the new opening a handful of Shorts and compare the range, not one result. If the figure rises and viewing depth holds, keep it. If the figure rises but depth falls, the opening is promising more than the Short delivers. If nothing moves, the topic or audience may be the issue rather than the edit.

What is a good viewed versus swiped away percentage?

There is no universal figure. Compare each Short with your own recent Shorts of similar length and topic.

Does swiped away mean the viewer disliked the Short?

Not necessarily. It means they left during the opening seconds, which can be a weak first frame, a mismatch with the audience or simply browsing behaviour.

Does a high stayed-to-watch figure mean people finished the Short?

No. Neither label means the viewer completed it, so check viewing depth as well.

How many Shorts do I need before I trust a change?

A handful with the same new opening, compared with your earlier range.
