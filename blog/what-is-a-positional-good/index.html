What is a positional good, and is there one that is pure status?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A positional good is something whose value to you depends heavily on how it compares with what other people have, rather than on what it does by itself. The economist Fred Hirsch coined the term in 1976 for goods that are scarce and fixed in supply, such as elite school places or a house with an unspoilt view. Most status purchases mix positional value with real use; a few are almost pure position.

This page is general education, not financial advice. Only spend money you can genuinely spare on anything bought mainly for status.

Key takeaways

  • Hirsch defined positional goods as "scarce goods that are fixed or near-fixed in supply", according to a 2025 review in Economics & Philosophy.
  • The economist Robert Frank uses the term for goods where the link between context and how we value them is strongest, with housing as his main example.
  • When everyone competes for position, spending can rise without anyone ending up better placed, which Frank compares to an arms race.
  • Not every good is positional: most people would rather have more holiday in absolute terms than more holiday than their neighbours.
  • A pure positional good strips out the practical use and keeps only the ranking or signal.

Where the idea comes from

Fred Hirsch introduced positional goods in Social Limits to Growth (1976). In a 2025 paper in Economics & Philosophy, the philosopher Jens Jørund Tyssedal sets out three ways the term is used: Hirsch's own version (scarce goods "fixed or near-fixed in supply"), Robert Frank's version (goods whose value depends "relatively heavily on how it compares with other goods in the same category") and a version used by philosophers Brighouse and Swift (goods whose value depends on your place in the distribution).

Hirsch's examples, as listed in that paper, include Old Masters, natural landscapes, suburban land, authentic tourist spots, leadership jobs and elite education. He separated two kinds of scarcity:

Type of scarcityWhat it meansExample
PhysicalThere simply isn't any more of itAn Old Master painting, a famous view
Direct socialMore supply would spoil it, because its point is being rareStatus symbols
Incidental socialOther people using it makes it worse for youCrowded roads, a "secret" beach that becomes popular

Positional versus non-positional goods

Frank tested the idea with simple thought experiments in a 2005 paper in the American Economic Review. Would you rather live in a 4,000-square-foot house while others have 6,000, or in a smaller house while others have smaller still? He reports that most people choose the second world, where their house is smaller in absolute terms but bigger relative to others. Asked the same question about holiday time, most people choose more weeks off in absolute terms, even if others get more.

Positional goodNon-positional good
What matters mostYour rankThe absolute amount
Frank's exampleHouse sizeHoliday time
If everyone gets moreLittle gain for anyoneEveryone gains
Typical resultSpending racesSteady improvement

Why positional spending can be wasteful

Frank argues that the gap between what makes sense for one person and what makes sense for everyone is "structurally identical" to a military arms race. If every family stretches to buy a bigger house to stay level with the neighbours, the ranking stays the same and everyone has spent more. The same logic applies to some job interview suits, wedding budgets and school catchment house prices.

That doesn't make positional goods bad. People enjoy rank, rarity and recognition, and spending your own spare money on them is a choice. It does explain why such spending often brings less lasting satisfaction than expected.

How it relates to Veblen goods and conspicuous consumption

These terms overlap but are not the same:

TermCoined byCore idea
Conspicuous consumptionThorstein Veblen, 1899Spending to display wealth and status
Veblen goodEconomists after VeblenDemand rises as the price rises, because the price is the point
Positional goodFred Hirsch, 1976Value depends on scarcity or on your rank
Costly signalSignalling theoryA display is believable because it is expensive to fake

Britannica traces conspicuous consumption to Veblen's The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899) and describes it as using goods of higher quality, or in greater quantity, than practical needs justify. A positional good is the wider category: it can be status-driven, like a designer handbag, or simply scarce, like a seat in a famous school.

FAQ

Is there a product that is a pure positional good?

Yes. Asendify's The £6,000 Club is a numbered certificate stating only that you paid £6,000, with no perks or resale value. A numbered limited-edition print is a cheaper alternative that keeps some value.

Who coined the term positional good?

Fred Hirsch, in Social Limits to Growth (1976).

What are examples of positional goods?

Elite school places, houses in sought-after areas, leadership jobs, rare art and many luxury status symbols.

Is a positional good the same as a Veblen good?

No. A Veblen good is one where higher prices raise demand. A positional good is valued for its scarcity or your rank. Many luxury items are both.

Are positional goods a waste of money?

Not necessarily for the individual, but when everyone competes for rank, spending can rise without anyone moving up, which Robert Frank compares to an arms race.
