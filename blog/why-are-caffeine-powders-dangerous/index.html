Why are caffeine powders dangerous?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 29 October 2026 · Last updated 4 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Caffeine powders are dangerous because a tiny amount holds a huge dose. The US Food and Drug Administration says one teaspoon of pure powdered caffeine can contain as much caffeine as 28 cups of coffee, and a safe serving cannot be measured with kitchen spoons. These products have contributed to deaths, so do not buy or use them.

Key takeaways

  • One teaspoon of pure caffeine powder can hold about as much caffeine as 28 cups of coffee, according to the FDA.
  • The FDA says toxic effects such as seizures can occur with about 1,200 mg taken quickly, less than half a teaspoon of powder.
  • You cannot measure a safe dose accurately at home, so avoid powders and highly concentrated liquid caffeine.
  • Stick to drinks and products with a labelled amount: up to 200 mg per dose and 400 mg a day for healthy adults.
  • Get emergency help for any suspected caffeine overdose.

This page is education for healthy adults aged 18 and over. It is general information, not medical advice. In an emergency, call 999 (UK), 911 (US) or 112 (India and the EU).

The maths that makes powder so risky

The FDA's page on pure and highly concentrated caffeine sets out the problem:

  • About 28 cups of coffee in one teaspoon of pure powdered caffeine.
  • More than 20 cups of coffee in half a cup of a highly concentrated liquid caffeine product.
  • At least two deaths in the United States linked to these products.

The FDA's consumer guide, Spilling the Beans: How Much Caffeine Is Too Much?, adds that toxic effects, such as seizures, can be seen with rapid consumption of about 1,200 mg of caffeine, which is less than half a teaspoon of pure caffeine.

Compare that with the limits from the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA): for healthy adults, up to 200 mg in a single dose and up to 400 mg a day from all sources do not raise safety concerns.

With powder, the gap between a normal dose and a dangerous one is a pinch, not a few cups.

Why you cannot measure it safely at home

A sensible dose of pure caffeine is a fraction of a gram. Kitchen spoons, scoops and cheap scales are not accurate enough for that. Powder also clumps, settles and gets spilled, so two "level" spoons can contain very different amounts. Mistakes that would be harmless with coffee become serious with powder.

Highly concentrated liquid caffeine is just as risky

Some products sell caffeine as a concentrated liquid, to be measured out by drops or caps. The risk is the same: a small measuring error means a large dose. In April 2018 the FDA issued guidance for industry on highly concentrated caffeine in dietary supplements, to help firms judge when such products are considered adulterated and illegal. Avoid both forms.

Warning signs of too much caffeine

The FDA lists signs of too much caffeine, including a fast heart rate, palpitations, high blood pressure, insomnia, anxiety, jitters, an upset stomach, nausea and headache. Severe overdose can cause seizures. If you or someone else has taken a large amount of caffeine, especially from powder or concentrate, or has worrying symptoms after caffeine, get medical help straight away. In the UK, call 999 or go to A&E in an emergency, or call 111 for urgent advice.

Safer ways to get the effect you want

People usually turn to powders for gym sessions, study or long workdays. You can get a useful dose from products you can actually measure:

  1. Coffee or tea of a known size. A cup of instant coffee or a cup of tea gives a moderate dose that is easy to repeat.
  2. Read labels. Energy drinks and pre-workouts list caffeine per serving. Check it, count it in your daily total, and do not take more than one serving at once.
  3. Use fixed-dose tablets only with care. Tablets remove the measuring problem, but they still count towards your total. Ask a pharmacist first, especially if you take other medicines.
  4. Time it. Plan caffeine around the task and stop early enough to protect your sleep.
  5. Do not mix caffeine with alcohol. The CDC explains that caffeine does not reduce alcohol's effects but can make you feel less affected, which can lead to drinking more.

Worked example: a gym-goer's choice

Example: Chris, 26, wants more energy for 6pm training and sees a cheap bag of pure caffeine powder online. The figures are rough estimates for illustration.

  • The powder route: the bag suggests a dose of 200 mg. That is a tiny fraction of a gram. His kitchen scale reads to the nearest gram, so it cannot measure that. A level teaspoon, by the FDA's comparison, holds as much as 28 cups of coffee. One mis-scooped serving could put him well above 1,200 mg.
  • The measured route: he uses a pre-workout with 150 mg per labelled scoop, one scoop only, at 5:30pm on training days. That is inside the 200 mg single-dose level, and with one morning coffee (estimated 100 mg) his day stays at about 250 mg.
  • The sleep check: a 2023 review estimated a typical pre-workout serve should be taken about 13 hours before bed to avoid losing sleep, so he moves training to lunchtime on work days when he can.

He skips the powder entirely. This is an illustration of the reasoning, not personal advice.

Who should avoid caffeine or ask first

Some people should avoid or limit caffeine and check with a doctor or pharmacist first: anyone pregnant or breastfeeding (the NHS advises no more than 200 mg a day in pregnancy), anyone under 18, people with heart conditions or high blood pressure, people with anxiety or panic disorders, and anyone on medicines that may interact with caffeine. For these groups, concentrated caffeine products are even more risky.

FAQ

Is caffeine powder legal in the UK?

Rules differ between countries and can change, so check current local guidance. Whatever the law, the safety advice is the same: do not use pure or highly concentrated caffeine.

Are caffeine tablets the same as powder?

No. Tablets come in fixed, labelled doses, which removes the measuring problem. They still count towards your daily total, so read the label and do not exceed the stated dose.

How much caffeine is in a cup of coffee?

It varies. The NHS puts a cup of instant at about 100 mg, and the FDA puts a 12-ounce cup of brewed coffee at 113 to 247 mg. Coffee-shop drinks can contain more.

What should I do if I've taken too much?

Stop taking any more caffeine and get medical help, especially if you have chest pain, a racing or irregular heartbeat, confusion or vomiting. Call 999 in a UK emergency.

Why do some products still sell caffeine powder?

Regulation varies by country and enforcement takes time. The FDA has warned consumers about pure and highly concentrated caffeine, and the safest choice is to avoid it.
