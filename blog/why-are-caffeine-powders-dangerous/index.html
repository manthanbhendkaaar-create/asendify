Why are caffeine powders dangerous?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 29 October 2026 · Last updated 29 October 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Caffeine powders are dangerous because a tiny amount contains a huge dose. The US Food and Drug Administration says a single teaspoon of pure powdered caffeine can contain about as much caffeine as 28 cups of coffee, and it is almost impossible to measure a safe serving with kitchen spoons. People have died from accidental overdoses, which is why you should not buy or use them at all.

If you want to plan ordinary drinks instead, the free Caffeine Cutoff Calculator estimates how much caffeine is still in you at bedtime. Coffee, tea and the occasional energy drink are far easier to keep within safe limits.

This page is education for healthy adults aged 18 and over. It is general information, not medical advice.

The maths that makes powder so risky

The FDA's consumer page Spilling the Beans: How Much Caffeine Is Too Much? explains the problem clearly:

  • About 28 cups of coffee in one teaspoon. Pure powdered caffeine is so concentrated that a normal household spoon holds a dose far beyond what anyone should take.
  • Toxic effects at around 1,200 mg. The FDA says toxic effects, such as seizures, can be seen with rapid consumption of around 1,200 mg of caffeine, which is only a small fraction of a tablespoon of pure powder.

Compare that with the safe limits from the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA): for healthy adults, up to 200 mg in a single dose and up to 400 mg a day from all sources do not raise safety concerns. With powder, the gap between "a normal dose" and "a dangerous dose" is a few grains, not a few cups.

Why you can't measure it safely at home

A safe dose of pure caffeine is a fraction of a gram. Kitchen spoons, scoops and cheap scales are not accurate enough for that. Powder also clumps, settles and gets spilled, so two "level" spoons can contain very different amounts. Mistakes that would be harmless with coffee become serious with powder.

Highly concentrated liquid caffeine is just as risky

Some products sell caffeine as a concentrated liquid in a bottle, to be measured out by drops or caps. The risk is the same: a small measuring error means a large dose. In 2018 the FDA announced action against pure and highly concentrated caffeine sold in bulk to consumers, saying these products present a significant public health risk. Avoid both forms.

Warning signs of too much caffeine

Too much caffeine can cause a fast or irregular heartbeat, shaking, feeling sick or vomiting, restlessness, anxiety, confusion and, in severe cases, seizures. If you or someone else has taken a large amount of caffeine, especially from powder or concentrate, or has these symptoms after caffeine, get medical help straight away. In the UK, call 999 or go to A&E in an emergency, or call 111 for urgent advice.

Safer ways to get the effect you want

People usually turn to powders for gym sessions, study or long workdays. You can get a useful dose from normal drinks you can actually measure:

  1. Coffee or tea with a known size. A standard mug of instant coffee or a cup of tea gives a moderate dose that is easy to repeat.
  2. Read labels. Energy drinks and pre-workouts list caffeine per serving. Check it, count it in your daily total, and do not take more than one serving at once.
  3. Time it. Caffeine works best when you plan it around the task and stop early enough to protect your sleep. See how much caffeine before a workout and how much caffeine is too much in a day.
  4. Never mix caffeine with alcohol. Caffeine can mask how drunk you feel without reducing how drunk you are. The CDC explains the risks.

Who should avoid caffeine or ask first

Some people should avoid or limit caffeine and check with a doctor or pharmacist first: anyone pregnant or breastfeeding (EFSA advises no more than 200 mg a day), anyone under 18, people with heart conditions or high blood pressure, people with anxiety or panic disorders, and anyone on medicines that may interact with caffeine. For these groups, concentrated caffeine products are even more risky.

Key takeaways

  • One teaspoon of pure caffeine powder can hold about as much caffeine as 28 cups of coffee, according to the FDA.
  • Toxic effects can appear around 1,200 mg taken quickly, a tiny amount of powder.
  • You cannot measure a safe dose accurately at home, so do not use powders or concentrated liquid caffeine.
  • Stick to drinks with a known caffeine content: up to 200 mg per dose and 400 mg a day for healthy adults.
  • Get emergency help for any suspected caffeine overdose.

FAQ

Is caffeine powder legal in the UK?

Rules differ between countries and can change, so check current local guidance. Whatever the law, the safety advice is the same: do not use pure or highly concentrated caffeine.

Are caffeine tablets the same as powder?

No. Tablets come in fixed, labelled doses, which removes the measuring problem. They still count towards your daily total, so read the label and do not take more than the stated dose.

How much caffeine is in a cup of coffee?

It varies by type and size. The FDA says a typical 8-ounce cup of coffee has around 80 to 100 mg, while a mug from a coffee shop can contain more. Check the label or the shop's published figures for your usual drink.

What should I do if I've taken too much?

Stop taking any more caffeine and get medical help, particularly if you have chest pain, a racing heartbeat, confusion or vomiting. Call 999 in an emergency.
