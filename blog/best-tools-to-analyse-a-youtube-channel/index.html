What are the best tools to analyse a YouTube channel?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 11 November 2026 · Last updated 11 November 2026

The best tools to analyse a YouTube channel are YouTube Studio for your own private data, Social Blade for tracking any channel's public numbers over time, and vidIQ or TubeBuddy for keyword and competitor research. For a fast, blunt check of any channel's titles, thumbnails and upload habits, the free YouTube Channel Roast by Asendify suits creators who want three fixes in a minute with no sign-up.

Disclosure: Asendify makes the YouTube Channel Roast and Asendify AI. They are ours.

Key takeaways

  • First decide whose channel you are analysing. Your own channel has private data (retention, click-through rate, traffic sources) that only YouTube Studio and tools you connect can see.
  • For other people's channels, every tool works from public data: subscribers, views, upload dates, titles and thumbnails.
  • YouTube Studio is free and the most accurate source for your own channel. Most other tools add interpretation, history or research on top.
  • Free tiers cover most needs for small channels. Pay only when a tool answers a question Studio cannot.
  • No tool can tell you why viewers leave without you rewatching the video at that moment.

How we chose

A good channel analysis tool should:

  1. Use accurate data, either directly from YouTube or clearly labelled as public estimates.
  2. Answer a specific question (where viewers leave, what topics work, how a channel is trending) rather than show numbers for their own sake.
  3. Offer a free way to start.
  4. Be honest about its limits, such as not seeing private analytics.

We order the list by "best for", not by score. We have not run lab tests on these tools; descriptions come from their official pages and YouTube's own help centre.

Best for your own channel: YouTube Studio

Nothing beats the source. YouTube Studio's analytics show impressions, click-through rate, average view duration, traffic sources and the full retention graph for every video. YouTube's guide to understanding your content performance explains the Content tab, and its guide to key moments for audience retention explains the intro figure, spikes and dips.

Where it is available, Ask Studio adds a built-in AI assistant that can review channel trends and answer questions.

Choose it if: you want accurate data about your own videos, free.

Best for tracking any channel over time: Social Blade

Social Blade tracks public statistics for YouTube channels and other platforms, such as subscriber and view counts over time. It is widely used to see whether a channel is growing, flat or declining, including channels you do not own. Wikipedia's entry on Social Blade gives background on the service.

Choose it if: you want a quick history of a competitor's or collaborator's public numbers. Remember it cannot see why the numbers moved.

Best for keyword and competitor research: vidIQ and TubeBuddy

vidIQ and TubeBuddy are browser extensions and apps focused on research and optimisation: keyword ideas, competitor tracking and SEO checks. Both offer a free tier and paid plans; check each site for current pricing.

Choose them if: your question is "what should I make next and how should I title it?" rather than "why did this one video drop?".

Best quick outside check: YouTube Channel Roast

The free YouTube Channel Roast works on any public channel. Paste a link and it scores the channel out of 100 on titles, thumbnails and upload consistency, then gives blunt feedback and three specific fixes. No sign-up. Because it uses public data, it cannot judge retention or hooks; use it as a packaging check alongside Studio.

Best for per-video answers from your own data: Asendify AI

If you want answers drawn from your private analytics, Asendify AI connects with read-only Google access and covers retention diagnosis, hook reviews, title work and best-versus-worst comparisons. It cannot post or change anything and does not analyse thumbnail images. It is paid with no free trial, so start with the free tools above.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Treating public estimates as exact. Third-party tools see what YouTube makes public. Your own Studio data is the true figure for your channel.
  • Paying before you know your question. Most small channels get everything they need from Studio and free tiers.
  • Copying a competitor's topics without checking your own data. What works for their audience may not work for yours.
  • Looking at numbers without rewatching. A dip in retention means nothing until you watch what was on screen at that moment.

FAQ

What is the best free tool to analyse my YouTube channel?

YouTube Studio is the best free tool for your own channel because it uses your private data directly. For a quick outside view of your packaging, the free YouTube Channel Roast gives a score and three fixes without sign-up.

Can I see another channel's analytics?

Only its public numbers, such as subscribers, views and upload history. Retention, click-through rate and traffic sources are private to the channel owner.

Is Social Blade accurate?

It shows public statistics and their history. It cannot see private analytics, so use it for trends, not for exact performance of individual videos.

Do I need vidIQ or TubeBuddy?

Not to start. They are most useful for keyword research and optimisation once you know what kind of videos you want to make. Both have free tiers.

Which tool tells me why a video underperformed?

YouTube Studio shows where viewers left; you then rewatch to find out why. Tools connected to your analytics, such as Asendify AI, can compare a video with your usual results and point to specific moments.
