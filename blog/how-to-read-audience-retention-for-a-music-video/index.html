How do I read audience retention for a music video on YouTube?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 3 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Read it against your own other music uploads, not against talking videos. Music viewers often listen in the background, replay favourite sections and skip intros, so the graph tells you different things: early dips usually point to a slow intro, spikes show the parts people rewatch or share (often the hook or a striking visual), and the end of the curve shows whether your outro holds or loses people. Compare videos of similar length and look for patterns across releases.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's key moments report includes the Intro, the share of viewers still watching after the first 30 seconds.
  • Spikes are "moments in your video that were rewatched or shared"; in music, these are often the hook or a memorable shot.
  • Dips mean "viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part".
  • Typical retention lets you compare with your 10 latest videos of similar length, the fairest benchmark for a music channel.
  • Retention is one signal; for music, also look at returning viewers, traffic sources and playlist additions.

What each part of the graph usually means for music

PatternLikely causeWhat to try
Steep drop in the first 10 to 30 secondsLong intro, skit, slow fade, mismatch with thumbnailStart the song sooner; match the thumbnail to the video
Dip then a jump forwardViewers skipping a non-musical sectionShorten or move that section
Spike at the first chorusFans replaying the hookUse that moment in Shorts and the thumbnail
Gradual, steady declineNormal for most musicCompare with your own similar uploads
Sharp drop after the last chorusLong outro or creditsEnd sooner; use the end screen
Flat line, very highBackground listening or playlist playCheck traffic sources and playback locations

Steps to analyse a release

  1. Open the video's analytics and go to the audience retention section.
  2. Note the Intro figure and where the first big drop happens.
  3. Mark spikes and dips against the song's timeline (verse, chorus, bridge).
  4. Compare with typical retention for your recent uploads of similar length.
  5. Check traffic sources: search, suggested, playlists, external. Viewers from a playlist behave differently from people who clicked your thumbnail.
  6. Write one change to test in the next release (shorter intro, different first frame, shorter outro).

Using spikes

Spikes show what people want more of. Use those moments as:

  • The opening of your Shorts or Reels teasers.
  • The thumbnail frame.
  • The idea for the next video's visual concept.

Example: an indie band's three singles

Example: a band compares three videos of similar length. Two with 20-second atmospheric intros have a steep early drop; the one that starts on the first chord holds more viewers past 30 seconds. All three spike at the first chorus. For the next video, the band starts on the riff, uses the chorus moment in its Shorts, and cuts the outro to end on the last note. Details are illustrative.

FAQ

Is there a book on improving YouTube retention?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, pacing and structure for £1,720 one-time; it isn't music-specific.

What's good audience retention for a music video?

There's no universal number. Compare with your own recent videos of similar length using typical retention.

Why does my music video spike in the middle?

Spikes are moments people rewatch or share, often the chorus or a standout shot.

Does background listening count?

Watch time from people listening still counts as viewing; that's one reason music curves differ from talking videos.

Should I change a song to improve retention?

No. Change the video: intro, first frame, outro and visuals.
