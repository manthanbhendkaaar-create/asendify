How do I brief a video editor for a client ad?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Brief a video editor for a client ad with one page that answers eight things: who the viewer is, the single promise, what the first 3 seconds must show, the proof, the call to action, the versions and specs needed, the client's must-haves and no-gos, and the deadline with review rounds. Add one or two reference ads and the raw assets, labelled. A clear brief is the cheapest way to get a first cut that holds attention.

Key takeaways

  • One page is enough. If it doesn't fit on one page, the ad is trying to say too much.
  • Name one viewer and one promise. Editors judge every shot against them.
  • Write the first 3 seconds into the brief; don't leave the hook to chance.
  • List every version and aspect ratio up front so the editor frames for all of them.
  • Agree how feedback will be given, ideally timecoded comments on the cut.

Why editor briefs go wrong

Agencies often pass the client's brief straight to the editor. That brief was written for strategy, not for cutting footage. The UK research behind the IPA-backed guide The best way for a client to brief an agency found that only 38% of creative agencies were clear on the target group in the briefs they received, and only 30% of marketers had clear criteria for judging work against the brief. If the account team is unclear, the editor is guessing.

An editor brief translates strategy into decisions on a timeline: what goes first, what proves the claim, what the viewer should do at the end.

Viewer and promise

Write the viewer the way they would describe themselves, not as a demographic. Then write the promise as an outcome. "A modern banking app" is not a promise; "split bills with friends in one tap" is.

The first 3 seconds

Spell out the opening. TikTok's creative best practices recommend introducing the proposition in the first 3 seconds and putting the hook in the first 6. Google's ABCDs of effective video ads also advise bringing in the brand "from the start" and keeping it present, so tell the editor how the brand should appear inside the hook rather than as a separate logo intro.

Proof and call to action

List the proof shots in priority order and say which are essential. For the close, give the exact call-to-action wording. Google recommends reinforcing an on-screen call to action with voice-over, so ask for both.

Versions and specs

Framing for vertical and horizontal at the same time changes how an editor builds the cut. TikTok's in-feed ad specifications list vertical 9:16 as recommended, with 16:9 and 1:1 also accepted, and remind you to keep content inside the safe zone. Put every placement in the brief so nothing has to be re-cut later.

Must-haves and no-gos

Include brand rules, legal lines and claims the client cannot make. If the ad uses customer testimonials, note that UK advertising rules require the advertiser to hold evidence that a testimonial is genuine and to have permission to use it, as the ASA explains in its guidance on testimonials and endorsements.

Assets and references

  • Label every file. "Interview_customer_Sarah_approved.mp4" saves an hour of searching.
  • Mark the best takes. If you were on the shoot, note timecodes of the strongest lines.
  • Give one or two reference ads. Say what to borrow from each: the pace, the text style, the opening.
  • Supply brand assets. Fonts, colours, logo files and any end card template.

How to give feedback on the first cut

Agree in the brief how feedback will arrive. Timecoded comments are easier to act on than an email list. In review tools such as Frame.io, comments are tied to the timecode, can cover a range of frames, and can include drawn annotations. Ask reviewers to refer to the brief: "this misses the single promise" is more useful than "it doesn't feel right".

Worked example

Example: a client wants a 30-second ad for a new meal-kit offer. The account manager forwards a three-page strategy deck. The editor's first cut opens with the brand animation and gets to the offer at 0:12.

The agency rewrites it as a one-page editor brief. Viewer: people who cook after work and are bored of the same meals. Promise: "Five new dinners a week, ready in 20 minutes." First 3 seconds: a pan sizzling, text "Dinner in 20 minutes?" Proof: an unboxing shot and a timer on screen. Call to action: "Get your first box" said and shown. Versions: 15 and 30 seconds, 9:16 and 4:5. The second cut reaches the offer by second 2, and the review needs one round instead of three. The brief added nothing new to film; it removed guessing.

FAQ

Is there a template for briefing a video editor on an ad?

Yes: use the eight-section table above. A system such as Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook adds shared hook and pacing rules your editors can apply to every brief.

How long should an editor brief be?

One page. Link the strategy deck for background, but keep the decisions on a single sheet.

Should I write the script into the brief?

If there is a voice-over or a set line order, yes. If the ad is cut from interviews, list the key lines with timecodes instead.

How many review rounds should a brief allow?

Agree a number before work starts, often two. A clear brief usually means fewer rounds are needed.

What if the client keeps changing the brief?

Treat a new promise or new viewer as a new brief, with a new deadline, rather than a revision.
