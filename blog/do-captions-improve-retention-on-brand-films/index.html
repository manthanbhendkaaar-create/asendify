Do captions improve retention on brand films, and what style works best?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes, captions help brand films hold more viewers, mainly because many people watch muted on social feeds and others simply prefer reading along. The style that works best for a brand film is usually quiet and precise: accurate words timed to speech, two short lines at most, a clean font in the brand's typeface or a neutral sans-serif, and good contrast. Burn captions into social cut-downs; upload a caption file for YouTube and your website so viewers can switch them on or off.

Key takeaways

  • Many viewers rely on text. Nielsen Norman Group reports that 26% of views on its own video channel are with subtitles.
  • Captions are an accessibility basic: the W3C says they serve people who are Deaf or hard of hearing and "people who process written information better than audio".
  • Social cuts must work muted. Facebook's guidance, as reported by Search Engine Journal, says videos "should be able to be viewed, understood, and enjoyed without sound".
  • Don't trust auto-captions blindly. YouTube warns automatic captions "might misrepresent the spoken content" and says to "always review" them.
  • Brand films need a calmer caption style than Shorts: precision and readability over bouncing, word-by-word effects.

Why captions matter more for brand films than you'd think

Brand films often run on muted autoplay: website hero sections, LinkedIn, Instagram, event screens. Without captions, a muted viewer sees beautiful pictures with no story, and the emotional line that the whole film builds towards is lost. Captions keep those viewers following the narrative. They also help non-native speakers, viewers in noisy places and anyone who wants to check a name or a figure.

Style guide for brand film captions

  1. Accuracy first. Every word right, names spelt correctly, no auto-caption errors.
  2. Two lines maximum, broken at natural phrase points.
  3. Timing. Captions appear with the speech and stay long enough to read. TikTok's guidance of "5-10 words per second" for on-screen text is an upper limit for short, punchy cuts; slower films can use a calmer pace.
  4. Font. The brand's typeface if it reads well small; otherwise a clean sans-serif.
  5. Contrast. White or off-white with a subtle shadow or semi-transparent box; check over bright skies and white walls.
  6. Position. Lower third, but move up for platform interface areas in vertical cuts and avoid covering faces or key product shots.
  7. Sound descriptions where they matter: [music], [laughter], [machine hums], for viewers who can't hear.
  8. Consistency across all versions of the film.

Common mistakes

  • Uncorrected auto-captions with misspelt names.
  • Bouncing, emoji-filled captions on a cinematic film.
  • Captions covering the product or a face at the key moment.
  • Different styles across cut-downs of the same film.
  • Forgetting sound descriptions where sound tells the story.

A caption quality check before release

Before release, watch every version once with the sound off, reading only the captions. Check that names, places and product terms are spelt correctly, that no caption appears before the words are spoken, that each one stays on screen long enough to read, and that nothing important is hidden behind platform buttons on vertical cuts. Then watch once with the sound on to confirm the captions match the speech exactly. Ask someone outside the project to do the muted watch too; fresh eyes catch errors the team has stopped seeing.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches retention editing for brand films?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook from Asendify covers hooks, pacing and structure for £1,720 one-time. The W3C's and YouTube's caption guidance is free.

Should brand film captions be burned in?

For social cut-downs and muted autoplay, yes. For YouTube and your own player, upload a caption file so viewers can choose.

Are YouTube auto-captions good enough?

They're a starting point. YouTube itself says to review and edit them.

What font should captions use?

The brand's typeface if it's readable at small sizes; otherwise a clean sans-serif with strong contrast.

Do captions spoil a cinematic look?

Not if they're clean, well placed and consistent. Busy animated styles are the bigger risk.
