Should I edit out ums and filler words in course and lecture videos?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Cut the fillers that distract and keep the ones that give the learner thinking time. In course and lecture videos, removing every um makes the instructor sound clipped and robotic, while leaving every stumble makes lessons longer and harder to follow. A good rule is to remove repeated starts, long stumbles and fillers piled on top of each other, keep natural pauses at the end of a clause, and listen to every cut. This is editing judgement supported by tool vendor guidance, not a measured result, so check it against your own completion and retention data.

Key takeaways

  • Descript's guidance says filler "marks thinking time, so removing every instance makes speech sound clipped and strange," and that the ones worth keeping "sit at natural clause breaks" (Descript). It also says that for formal presentations "you probably don't want filler words."
  • Descript's help describes four handling options for each detected filler: delete, delete and replace with a gap "to maintain timing," ignore, or remove from the transcript only (Descript Help). It also offers "Avoid harsh cuts," which skips words that "can't be removed cleanly."
  • Descript says its detection currently works only on English transcripts, so check whether your tool supports your language.
  • YouTube Help says dips in the retention graph mean "viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). If you publish lessons on YouTube, use the graph to see whether tighter cuts help.
  • Wistia says "viewers typically watch over 50% of an educational video and a tutorial video" in the 1 to 5 minute range and that engagement "naturally declines after the five-minute mark" (Wistia). Tight cuts matter more as lessons get longer.

Why the answer is not "all of them" or "none"

A lecture is a different job from a comedy podcast. The learner is trying to follow an idea, and the instructor needs to be clear. But a lesson delivered with no pauses can feel like a machine reading a script, and learners need time to process. Fillers do two things: they signal that the speaker is thinking, and they give the listener a beat to catch up. Removing them all can take away both.

On the other hand, a lesson with a lot of repeated "um, so, um, basically" slows the explanation and adds minutes that the learner has to sit through. The goal is balance, not purity.

A practical rule set

TypeExampleUsually
Stacked fillers"So, um, uh, basically, like..."Cut
Repeated false starts"The first thing is, the first thing you should..."Cut to one clean version
Long stumble with silenceTwo seconds of "uhhh"Cut
Short filler at a clause break"...and then, um, we add the next step"Often keep or shorten
Filler that covers a mouse click or screen change"Um, let me just..."Cut and let the screen change speak
Verbal tic repeated every sentence"Right?" or "you know"Thin out

Step by step

  1. Edit for content first. Cut tangents and repeated explanations before you touch the fillers. This usually removes many of them.
  2. Run the tool's filler detection as a first pass. Descript, for example, lists each detected word with timestamps and lets you preview audio before choosing a handling option.
  3. Choose gaps over deletions when timing matters. Delete-and-replace-with-gap keeps the rhythm, which helps when a filler sits between two ideas.
  4. Turn on harsh-cut protection if your tool has it. Descript's Avoid harsh cuts skips fillers that cannot be removed cleanly, which saves you fixing clipped words later.
  5. Listen to the whole lesson at normal speed. Any cut that sounds like a jump or a clipped word needs fixing by hand.
  6. Add room tone under the cuts. Silence with no background hiss often sounds more obvious than the filler did.
  7. Use visual cuts to hide edits. A screen zoom, slide change or B-roll can cover a trimmed section.
  8. Keep a few natural ones. If the instructor sounds human at the start, keep a handful of clause-break fillers so the rhythm stays alive.

Course-specific considerations

  • Slide and screen lessons. The learner is looking at the content, not the face, so audio edits are less visible. You can cut tighter, but keep enough pause for the learner to read.
  • Talking-head lessons. Jump cuts show. Use punch-ins or cutaways to mask them.
  • Live workshop recordings. The room has questions and reactions. Keep only what the later viewer needs.
  • Non-native speakers. Do not "fix" accents or natural pace. Cut stumbles, not character.
  • Accessibility. Captions should help learners follow, and W3C notes automatic captions need review to be accurate (W3C WAI). Transcripts and captions share the same text.

How to check your edit

  1. Compare the old and new lesson on a few learners if you can, and ask what felt too fast or too slow.
  2. If the lesson is on YouTube, check the retention graph for dips where you trimmed heavily.
  3. If it is on a course platform, check completion and replay data per lesson, if the platform shows it.
  4. Compare lessons of similar length. A change in one lesson is an anecdote; a pattern across several is more useful.
  5. Adjust your filler policy and write it into your style guide.

Example: a 12-minute accounting lesson

Example: an instructor records a 12-minute lesson on balance sheets with many stacked fillers and several restarts. In the first pass, the editor deletes every filler automatically; the lesson drops to 10 minutes but several cuts clip the next word and the instructor sounds rushed. In the second pass, the editor reverts to the original, cuts only stacked fillers and restarts, keeps short ones at clause breaks, uses gaps where timing matters and covers jump cuts with a slide zoom. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Common mistakes

  • Running one-click removal and publishing without listening.
  • Leaving long stumbles because cutting takes time.
  • Cutting thinking pauses that learners need.
  • Forgetting room tone.
  • Using an English filler detector on another language.
