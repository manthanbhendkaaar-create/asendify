Can I use popular music in my event recap video?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Not safely without the right licence. A chart song in your recap is usually protected by copyright in both the composition and the recording, so posting it online can need permission from the rights holders, and platforms can mute, block or claim the video. Safer options are royalty-free or subscription library music that licenses online and commercial use, music commissioned for your event, or tracks the artist has cleared for you in writing.

Key takeaways

  • The UK government's licence finder says you might need licences from PRS for Music and PPL or VPL to use copyright music online, and that you could be sued for damages without one.
  • A venue's licence to play music at your event does not usually cover putting that music in a video online. Check the scope.
  • YouTube blocks Shorts over one minute that have an active Content ID claim, worldwide.
  • Library music with a commercial online licence is the simplest route for most event teams.
  • Music choice is also an editing choice: cut on the beat, then break the pattern for your biggest moment.

Why "we played it at the event" is not enough

Music at an event is usually covered by the venue's or organiser's licences for playing music in public. That is a different use from copying the music into a video and publishing it online. The UK government's page on licences to use music online says you "might need a licence" from PRS for Music, and from PPL or VPL, to use copyright music online, and warns that you "could be sued for damages if you use copyright music or music videos online" without one. It adds that "The type of licence you need depends on your operation", and points businesses to PRS for Music's online licensing team.

In practice, for a one-off marketing video, the rights you would need to put a commercial chart track in it are often hard or expensive to obtain. That is why most event recaps use library music.

What can happen on platforms

Even before legal risk, platforms act on their own systems. YouTube's page on three-minute Shorts says: "Any Short that is over one minute in duration with an active Content ID claim of any type" is blocked worldwide. Blocked Shorts can't be played, recommended or monetised. Long-form videos with claimed music can be muted, demonetised or blocked in some countries, depending on the rights holder's choice.

A blocked recap is the worst kind of retention problem: nobody watches it at all.

Choosing music that helps retention

Music sets the pace of the edit. A few habits help hold attention:

  • Pick a track with a clear build. Your three or four story beats can rise with it.
  • Start on a strong moment, not a long intro. The Retention Editing Playbook's cold open rule applies to music too: begin where the energy is, and trim a slow musical intro.
  • Cut on the beat, then break the pattern. Hold one shot across a bar, or drop the music for a single line of real audio from the room. That change is a pattern interrupt, and it re-grabs attention.
  • Leave space for voices. Duck the music under interview lines so they stay clear. Cvent's event video guide warns: "Bad audio will lose your audience."

Example: choosing music for a recap

Example: a charity gala wants a 90-second highlight film for its website, YouTube and a paid Instagram campaign.

  • The band played a famous cover on the night. The team checks: the cover needs the song publisher's permission for online and ad use, which would take weeks.
  • Instead, they buy a track from a stock library on a licence tier that covers paid social and YouTube.
  • The edit opens on the auctioneer's gavel and the room cheering, then the track's first beat drops.
  • For 3 seconds in the middle, the music cuts out for the band's live audio of the crowd singing along, a short clip the band has agreed in writing can be used.
  • The film is uploaded with no claims.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches pacing and pattern interrupts you can use with music?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches pacing, pattern interrupts and cold opens. It doesn't cover licensing, so check official guidance for rights.

Is 15 seconds of a song fair use?

There is no safe "15-second rule" in UK law. Short clips can still infringe and can still be claimed by platforms.

Does crediting the artist make it legal?

No. Credit is polite, but it is not permission.

Can I use the music the DJ played?

Not just because it played at the event. The venue licence covers playing it there, not publishing it online.

What about Instagram's music library?

It can be fine for organic posts in the app, but check the terms, especially for business accounts and paid ads.

What if my video gets a claim?

Check whether the claim is valid. If it is, replace the track; if not, dispute it through the platform.
