How do I use open loops in online course lessons without annoying students?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Open one learning question in the first 15 seconds of each lesson ("Why does this formula break when you copy it down?"), answer it before the lesson ends, and finish with a one-line teaser for the next lesson. That is an open loop that serves learning. What annoys students is a loop that withholds what they need to do the task. Research on educational video supports this kind of structure: Brame's review recommends signalling, segmenting and questions embedded in the video.

Key takeaways

  • In teaching, an open loop is a question the lesson answers, not a cliffhanger.
  • One main loop per lesson; a short bridge loop into the next lesson at the end.
  • Brame's review notes segmenting can include "click forward" pauses with questions. A prediction question is a loop students actively engage with.
  • The same review reports median engagement close to 100% for edX videos under 6 minutes, falling to about 50% at 9 to 12 minutes. Short lessons need fewer loops.
  • Wistia says viewers usually watch over 50% of educational videos of 1 to 5 minutes, so don't hold the main answer until the last seconds.

Good loops and bad loops in lessons

LoopExampleEffect
Learning question"By the end, you'll know why this formula breaks, and the one character that fixes it."Focuses attention on the key idea
Prediction"Before I run it, pause and guess what the output will be."Students think, then check
Common mistake"There's a mistake in this code that most beginners make. See if you can spot it."Active looking
Next-lesson teaser"Next lesson, we'll use this to build a live dashboard."Motivates continuing
Withheld instruction (bad)"I'll tell you the setting later" when they need it nowFrustration, skipping
Endless tease (bad)"The secret is coming up" repeatedLost trust

Where loops go in a 6-minute lesson

TimeLoop activity
0:00 to 0:15Main learning question opened, with the finished result shown
0:15 to 1:30First step taught; prediction loop opened ("pause and guess")
1:30 to 2:00Prediction answered
2:00 to 4:00Core skill; common-mistake loop opened and closed
4:00 to 5:30Main learning question answered in full; practice task
5:30 to 6:00Next-lesson teaser (one sentence)

Loops across a module

A course is a series, so loops can work at module level too:

The module loop gives students a reason to keep going between lessons, which is where many online courses lose people.

If your lessons are on YouTube

Chapters help students jump to what they need, which can feel at odds with open loops. Use both: chapters for navigation, loops for motivation. YouTube needs a first timestamp at 00:00, at least three timestamps and chapters of 10 seconds or more. Title chapters as outcomes ("Fix the broken formula") rather than topics ("Absolute references").

Mistakes to avoid

  • Loops that block the task. If students need a setting to follow along, give it now.
  • Too many teasers. One next-lesson line is enough; a minute of "coming up" wastes time.
  • Clickbait for paying students. People who have already bought the course need clarity, not hype.
  • Answering a loop off-screen. Close every loop inside the lesson or point to exactly where it is answered.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches open loops for course videos?

The Retention Editing Playbook teaches open loops and pacing for £1,720 one-time; it is YouTube-first. Brame's CBE Life Sciences Education review is a free learning-focused alternative.

Are open loops manipulative in teaching?

Not when the loop is a real learning question answered in the lesson. Withholding what students need is the problem.

How many loops should a lesson have?

One main learning question, perhaps one prediction or mistake-spotting moment, and a one-line teaser at the end.

Should every lesson end with a teaser?

A short one helps students continue. Keep it to one sentence.

Do loops work with chapters?

Yes. Chapters help navigation; loops give students a reason to watch each chapter.
