When do I need a stock music licence for short-form video?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

You need a stock music licence whenever the in-app music library doesn't cover what you are doing: posting from a business account, running the video as an ad, making it for a client, or using the same edit on more than one platform or your website. In-app sounds are licensed for use inside that app only. A stock licence lets you pick a track once and use it everywhere the licence names.

Key takeaways

  • UK law: you cannot copy or use copyright material without permission, and a licence gives permission only for the purposes it names.
  • Free options exist: YouTube's Audio Library offers royalty-free music and sound effects that Partner Programme creators can monetise, and TikTok's Commercial Music Library is pre-cleared for businesses on TikTok.
  • A paid licence still may not stop a claim: YouTube notes that Content ID "doesn't know if you bought the rights to the music on other websites or offline". Keep your licence and know how to dispute.
  • When music is claimed, the rights holder can monetise, mute or block your video in some or all countries.
  • For short-form, choose tracks that start strong. You don't have 20 seconds of build-up.

What to check in a stock music licence

  1. Channels covered. Social, YouTube, website, paid ads, broadcast, podcasts. Short-form editors usually need social plus ads.
  2. Who is licensed. You, your company, or your client. Freelancers editing for clients often need a licence in the client's name or a plan that covers client work.
  3. Term. Some subscriptions cover videos published while you subscribe, forever; others need you to keep paying. Read the cancellation clause.
  4. Content ID handling. Good libraries let you whitelist your channel or clear claims quickly.
  5. Attribution. Most paid licences don't require credit; Creative Commons tracks often do. YouTube says Creative Commons tracks in its library need the artist credited in your description.
  6. Edits allowed. Can you cut, loop and change the track's length? For 15 to 30 second edits you almost always need to.

Picking a track that holds attention

A licence solves the legal problem, not the retention one. For a short video:

  • Start on energy. Skip intros that build for 10 seconds; cut into the track at its strongest point.
  • Match the beat to your cuts. Visual changes that land on beats feel deliberate.
  • Leave room for voice. If someone speaks, choose a track without vocals in that range, and keep music well under the voice.
  • Avoid the most-used stock tracks for your niche. Viewers recognise them.

Example: a freelance editor with a gym client

Example: a freelance editor makes eight 20-second Reels a month for a gym. The gym posts them to Instagram, TikTok and YouTube Shorts and boosts two as ads. The general in-app library won't cover the boosted posts or the cross-posting, so the editor uses a paid stock subscription whose plan includes client work and paid social, and logs each track's licence ID in a spreadsheet next to the video name. When one Short gets a Content ID claim, she submits the licence certificate through the library's claim tool, and the claim is released. FAQ

Is there a book that teaches editors how to pace short videos?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook covers pacing and pattern interrupts with templates. Studying your own retention graphs is the free alternative.

Do I need a licence for music in my TikTok?

Not for personal posts using the in-app library. Business accounts should use the Commercial Music Library or licensed music.

Is YouTube's Audio Library free for monetised videos?

Yes. YouTube says Partner Programme creators can monetise videos that use Audio Library music and sound effects.

Why did I get a claim on music I paid for?

Content ID can't see off-platform licences. Dispute the claim with your licence or use the library's claim-clearing tool.

Can I use one stock track across all platforms?

If the licence lists those channels. Check it covers social, YouTube, your website and paid ads as needed.
