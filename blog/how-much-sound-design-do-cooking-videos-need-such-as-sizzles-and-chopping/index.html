How much sound design do cooking videos need, such as sizzles and chopping?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Less than most editors add, and mostly real. A cooking video needs the genuine sound of the kitchen (the sizzle when food hits the pan, the knife on the board, the lid lifting) recorded close and mixed clearly, with the voice on top and music underneath. Add spot effects only to restore a sound the microphone missed, never to invent one. The goal is that the viewer can hear whether the pan is hot enough, which is real information, not decoration.

Key takeaways

  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded, rather than included", and signaling says cues "emphasize information that is more important" (Dartmouth). A sizzle that tells you the pan is ready is a cue; a random whoosh is clutter.
  • The W3C says captions should convey "meaningful sound effects" (W3C). If the sizzle matters to the instruction, it is worth captioning for viewers who cannot hear it.
  • A LinkedIn marketer writes in IAB UK that "79% of videos are watched on mute" on LinkedIn, using LinkedIn's data (IAB UK). Food content on social is often seen silently, so sound should add to, not carry, the instruction.
  • YouTube's report says "spikes appear when more viewers are watching, rewatching, or sharing those parts" (YouTube Help); sizzle and finish moments often attract rewatching.

Why sound matters in cooking

Cooking is sensory and sound is part of the information. A sizzle tells you the pan is hot, a crackle tells you the crust is forming, a dull thud tells you the dough is proofed or not. Viewers who cook along use those cues. Viewers who watch for pleasure get a sense of presence from the sound. In both cases the sound that matters is the real one.

Cooking videos also pose recording problems. Kitchens are noisy (extractor fans, fridges), the best sounds are quiet (chopping herbs), and the camera is often far from the pan. That is where editing comes in: capture what you can and restore the rest carefully.

What to capture and how

SoundWhere to recordTips
SizzleClose to the pan, off to the sideKeep the mic out of steam and splatter
ChoppingClose to the boardUse a mic on a boom or small stand just out of frame
Pouring and stirringClose to the pot or bowlRecord slightly longer than needed for clean edits

Room toneKitchen with the extractor offUse for filling gaps
VoiceLavalier or close micRecord separately from cooking sounds when possible

If you cannot record clean sound at the stove, record it afterwards as a short sound-only pass: chop another onion, drop food into a hot pan, and use those clips to sync. This is common practice, and it is honest as long as it matches what is on screen.

Step-by-step: mix a cooking video

  1. Clean the voice first. Remove noise from the extractor fan, level between takes, and keep it natural.
  2. Lay room tone. A quiet kitchen bed under the whole video so cuts do not jump.
  3. Place the real cooking sounds. Align sizzle, chopping and pouring to the picture. If a clip lacks sound, use your close-mic recordings.
  4. Mix the sounds below the voice. Real kitchen sound sits underneath narration and rises in natural pauses.
  5. Keep the sizzle audible at the key moment. When the food hits the pan, let the sound come forward for a second or two, because that is the cue.
  6. Add music last, and low. A gentle bed that ducks under speech; no music over the moments where the sound is the point.
  7. Use silence. A moment with only the real sound (steam, bubbling) is often more appetising than music.
  8. Add only restorative spot effects. If a sound was missed, add a matching real recording; avoid cartoon effects on cooking actions.
  9. Check on a phone speaker and headphones. Sizzle and chopping are mid-to-high frequency; check they are present and not harsh.
  10. Caption meaningful sounds only when they teach. "[sizzling]" matters when it is the doneness cue; do not caption every clink.

How much is too much

A useful rule of thumb from editing practice, not a research finding: if a viewer would notice the sound before the food, it is too much. Signs of overdoing it:

  • A whoosh or pop on every cut.
  • Exaggerated crunch added over a quiet bite.
  • Music that is louder than the chopping sound.
  • Effects that do not match the on-screen action.
  • A constant sound bed with no breaks.

Signs of too little:

  • Dead silence in the picture of a sizzling pan.
  • The viewer cannot hear whether the oil is hot.
  • Cuts that jump between loud and silent clips.

Example: a 90-second pasta recipe

Example: a food creator makes a quick pasta video. First version: constant upbeat music, a whoosh on every cut, and the voice at the same level as the music. Second version: the voice recorded close and cleaned, real sounds for garlic in oil (the sizzle is let up for two seconds), the board chopping, and the water pour, light music low under speech, and one beat of silence as the pasta is tossed. Accessibility

Captions and a short description of key sounds help viewers who cannot hear the video. The W3C says captions should convey "meaningful sound effects", and for a cooking video the meaningful sound is usually the cue ("sizzling means ready for the next step"). Say the cue in the voice too, so sound-off viewers who read captions and sound-on viewers both get it.

Should I add fake sizzle sounds?

Only to restore a missed real sound that matches the picture; avoid exaggerated effects.

Should I use music?

Light and low, ducked under speech, and absent at moments where the real sound is the point.

What is the most important sound?

The cue that tells the viewer what to do: the sizzle, the bubble, the chop rhythm.

Do I caption the sounds?

Only meaningful ones, such as a doneness cue.
