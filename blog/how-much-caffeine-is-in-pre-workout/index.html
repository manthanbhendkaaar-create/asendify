How much caffeine is in pre-workout, and is it too much?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 30 October 2026 · Last updated 4 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The caffeine in pre-workout varies widely by brand. A typical serve used in sleep research contained about 217 mg, and some scoops carry more. That is already above the 200 mg single dose that the European Food Safety Authority says raises no safety concerns for healthy adults, so many people should use less, choose a lower-caffeine product, or skip it.

Key takeaways

  • Pre-workout caffeine varies widely; a typical serve in research was about 217 mg, and some contain more.
  • Keep any single dose at or under 200 mg, and consider less if you weigh under 70 kg.
  • Sports research suggests caffeine helps at 3 to 6 mg per kg, and possibly from about 2 mg per kg.
  • Count the scoop with your coffee and tea against 400 mg a day.
  • A typical pre-workout needs about 13 hours before bed, so evening use usually costs you sleep.

This page is general education for healthy adults aged 18 and over. It is not medical advice. If you have a health condition or take regular medicine, ask your doctor or pharmacist before using pre-workout supplements.

How much is "too much" for one scoop?

Use the official numbers as your ceiling:

  • Single dose: the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) says up to 200 mg in one go, about 3 mg per kg of body weight, raises no safety concerns for healthy adults. It says the same amount is also safe when taken less than two hours before intense exercise under normal conditions.
  • Daily total: EFSA and the US FDA both use 400 mg a day for healthy adults.

Because EFSA expresses the single dose as about 3 mg per kg, a cautious approach for lighter people is to scale down:

Body weightCautious single-dose ceiling (about 3 mg per kg)
50 kgAbout 150 mg
60 kgAbout 180 mg
70 kg and over200 mg, never higher

Scaling down is a cautious reading of the EFSA figures, not an official rule. If your scoop says 250 mg or 300 mg, that is more than the ceiling in one go.

How much caffeine you actually need to train well

The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand on caffeine (2021) found caffeine consistently improves exercise performance at 3 to 6 mg per kg of body weight. The minimum effective dose is unclear but may be as low as 2 mg per kg. Very high doses, such as 9 mg per kg, are linked to a high rate of side effects and do not appear necessary. The most common timing is about 60 minutes before exercise.

Note the overlap problem: 3 to 6 mg per kg for a 70 kg person is 210 to 420 mg, which goes past the EFSA single-dose level. Staying near the lower end, around 2 to 3 mg per kg and never above 200 mg, is the cautious middle ground. That is often half a scoop of a strong pre-workout, or simply a coffee about 60 minutes before you start.

How to read a pre-workout label

  1. Find the caffeine per scoop in milligrams. If it is not listed clearly, do not use it.
  2. Check for "proprietary blends" that hide how much of each stimulant is inside.
  3. Look for other stimulants. If you do not recognise an ingredient, check with a pharmacist before taking it.
  4. Do not trust "stim-free" blindly. Read the full ingredient list.
  5. Never double-scoop, and never add energy drinks or caffeine tablets on top.
  6. If you compete in tested sport, choose third-party tested products, as supplements can be contaminated with banned substances.

Count the whole day, not just the scoop

Here is how quickly an ordinary training day adds up, using typical figures:

Coffee and tea figures are averages from the Mayo Clinic's caffeine guide; coffee-shop drinks can be much stronger. One more large coffee would take this day past 400 mg.

Timing: why evening pre-workout costs you sleep

A 2023 meta-analysis of 24 studies by Gardiner and colleagues in Sleep Medicine Reviews (open-access copy, Leeds Beckett University) found that a typical pre-workout serve of about 217 mg should be taken at least 13.2 hours before bedtime to avoid reducing total sleep. For a 10:30pm bedtime, that means before about 9:15am.

So if you train after work:

  • Skip the caffeine and use a proper meal 1 to 3 hours before, music and a full warm-up.
  • Use a much smaller dose, such as a regular coffee, only if it is at least 9 hours before bed.
  • Save the harder sessions for days you can train earlier.

Worked example: Mia's 6pm sessions

Example: Mia, 28, weighs 60 kg, trains at 6pm and goes to bed at 10:30pm. She uses a pre-workout with 275 mg per scoop and has two coffees earlier in the day. The figures are rough estimates for illustration.

  • Single dose: 275 mg is above both the 200 mg EFSA level and her cautious ceiling of about 180 mg.
  • Daily total: two coffees (estimated 190 mg) plus the scoop is about 465 mg, above 400 mg.
  • Timing: the scoop at 5:30pm is only 5 hours before bed, against the 13.2 hours the meta-analysis suggests.

Her new plan: on weekday evenings she skips caffeine before training and eats a light meal at 4pm. On Saturdays she trains at 10am with half a scoop (about 138 mg, roughly 2.3 mg per kg) and one morning coffee, keeping her day near 230 mg. This is an illustration, not personal advice.

Who should avoid pre-workout or ask a doctor first

  • Under 18s: this guidance is for adults only. The FDA notes that the American Academy of Pediatrics advises against energy drinks for children and teens.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding: the NHS advises no more than 200 mg of caffeine a day in pregnancy.
  • Heart conditions, high blood pressure or an irregular heartbeat.
  • Anxiety or panic disorder.
  • Anyone on regular medicines, including ADHD stimulants, some antidepressants and decongestants. Ask your pharmacist.

Stop training and get medical help if you have chest pain, a racing or irregular heartbeat, dizziness or fainting. Never use caffeine powders, which the FDA warns can be dangerous in tiny amounts, and never mix pre-workout or caffeine with alcohol.

FAQ

Is 300 mg of caffeine in pre-workout too much?

It is above the 200 mg single dose that EFSA says raises no safety concerns for healthy adults. Use a partial scoop or a lower-caffeine product instead.

Can I take pre-workout on an empty stomach?

Some people feel shaky or sick doing this. A small meal or snack beforehand often helps, and it also fuels the session.

Do I build a tolerance to pre-workout?

Habitual caffeine intake may affect how strongly you respond, according to the sports nutrition position stand. Using caffeine only for key sessions is a common way to keep it noticeable.

Is a coffee as good as pre-workout?

For the caffeine part, often yes. A brewed coffee about 60 minutes before training gives a modest dose, about 96 mg per 8 oz on Mayo Clinic averages, without extra stimulants.

When should I take pre-workout?

Research most often uses about 60 minutes before exercise. Also check it against your bedtime: a typical serve needs about 13 hours before sleep.
