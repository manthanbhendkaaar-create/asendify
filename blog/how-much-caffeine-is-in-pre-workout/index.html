How much caffeine is in pre-workout, and is it too much?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 30 October 2026 · Last updated 30 October 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The caffeine in pre-workout varies widely from brand to brand. A typical dose used in sleep research contained about 217 mg, and some products carry more than that in a single scoop. That's already above the 200 mg single-dose level that the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) says raises no safety concerns for healthy adults, so many people should use a smaller scoop, choose a lower-caffeine product, or skip pre-workout altogether.

Before you train, add your scoop to everything else you've had today with our free caffeine calculator. Pre-workout plus two coffees is an easy way to overshoot without noticing.

This page is general education for healthy adults aged 18 and over. It is not medical advice. If you have a health condition or take regular medicine, ask your doctor or pharmacist before using pre-workout supplements.

How much is "too much" for one scoop?

Use the official numbers as your ceiling:

  • Single dose: EFSA found that up to 200 mg in one go raises no safety concerns for healthy adults, including when taken less than two hours before intense exercise.
  • Daily total: EFSA and the US FDA both use 400 mg a day as the amount not generally linked with harmful effects in healthy adults.

Secrets to Using Caffeine to Win adds a cautious house rule: never more than 200 mg in one dose, and if you weigh less than 70 kg, scale down to about 3 mg per kg of body weight.

Body weightSuggested single-dose ceiling
50 kg150 mg
60 kg180 mg
70 kg and over200 mg (never higher)

Scaling down for lighter bodies is the book's cautious reading of the EFSA figures, not an official rule. If your scoop says 250 mg or 300 mg, that's more than this ceiling in one go.

How much caffeine you actually need to train well

The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand (2021) found that caffeine improves many types of exercise performance. Studies most often use 3 to 6 mg per kg, but lower doses of around 1 to 3 mg per kg also show benefits with fewer side effects, and side effects such as a fast heart rate, anxiety, headaches and poor sleep rise with the dose.

The book stays at the lower end: start with about 1 to 2 mg per kg (70 to 140 mg for a 70 kg person) and never go above 200 mg. That's often half a scoop of a strong pre-workout, or simply a coffee about 60 minutes before you start.

How to read a pre-workout label

  1. Find the caffeine per scoop in milligrams. If it isn't listed clearly, don't use it.
  2. Check for "proprietary blends" that hide how much of each stimulant is inside.
  3. Look for other stimulants. Some products add stimulants beyond caffeine. If you don't recognise an ingredient, check with a pharmacist before taking it.
  4. Don't trust "stim-free" blindly. Some products marketed that way still contain stimulants, and some contain poorly tested ingredients.
  5. Never double-scoop, and never add energy drinks or caffeine tablets on top.
  6. If you compete in tested sport, choose third-party tested products, as supplements can be contaminated with banned substances.

Count the whole day, not just the scoop

Here's how quickly an ordinary training day adds up, using typical figures:

Coffee and tea figures are Mayo Clinic averages; coffee-shop drinks can be far stronger. One more large coffee would take this day past 400 mg.

Timing: why evening pre-workout wrecks sleep

A 2023 review of sleep studies by Gardiner and colleagues (Sleep Medicine Reviews) found that a typical pre-workout dose of about 217 mg should be taken at least 13.2 hours before bedtime to avoid cutting into sleep. For a 10:30pm bedtime, that means before about 9:15am.

So if you train after work:

  • Skip the caffeine and use a proper meal 1 to 3 hours before, music and a full warm-up.
  • Use a much smaller dose (around 50 to 100 mg) only if it's at least 9 hours before bed.
  • Save the bigger sessions for days you can train earlier.

Who should avoid pre-workout or ask a doctor first

  • Under 18s: this guidance is for adults only, and paediatricians advise against energy drinks for young people.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding: the NHS advises no more than 200 mg of caffeine a day in pregnancy.
  • Heart conditions, high blood pressure or an irregular heartbeat.
  • Anxiety or panic disorder.
  • Anyone on regular medicines, including ADHD stimulants, some antidepressants and decongestants. Ask your pharmacist.

Stop training and get medical help if you have chest pain, a racing or irregular heartbeat, dizziness or fainting. Never use caffeine powders, which the FDA warns can be dangerous in tiny amounts, and never mix pre-workout or caffeine with alcohol.

Key takeaways

  • Pre-workout caffeine varies widely; a typical dose in research was about 217 mg, and some scoops contain more.
  • Keep any single dose at or under 200 mg (EFSA), scaled down if you weigh under 70 kg.
  • Lower doses of 1 to 3 mg per kg still help performance with fewer side effects.
  • Count the scoop with your coffee and tea against 400 mg a day.
  • A typical pre-workout needs about 13 hours before bed, so evening use usually costs you sleep.

FAQ

Is 300 mg of caffeine in pre-workout too much?

It's above the 200 mg single dose that EFSA says raises no safety concerns for healthy adults. Use a partial scoop or a lower-caffeine product instead.

Can I take pre-workout on an empty stomach?

Some people feel shaky or nauseous doing this. Having a small meal or snack beforehand often helps, and it also fuels the session.

Do I build a tolerance to pre-workout?

Yes, partly. Research found the performance boost from daily caffeine shrank over about 20 days, though it didn't disappear. Using it only for key sessions keeps it more effective.

Is a coffee as good as pre-workout?

For the caffeine part, often yes. A filter coffee about 60 minutes before training gives a modest dose (about 96 mg for a 237 ml cup, on average) without extra stimulants.
