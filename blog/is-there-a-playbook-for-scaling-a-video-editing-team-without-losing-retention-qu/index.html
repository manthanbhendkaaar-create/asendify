Key takeaways

  • Quality drops when the rules live in one person's head. Write them down before hiring editor number two.
  • Use public standards as a base. Google's ABCDs ("Hook and sustain attention", "add a CTA") and TikTok's creative best practices (proposition "in the first 3 seconds", "5-10 words per second" of text) are checkable rules.
  • Judge edits on data, not taste. YouTube's key moments help page lets you "use typical retention to compare your 10 latest videos of similar length."
  • Review dips together. YouTube says "Dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video", which turns feedback into specific timestamps.
  • A shared method plus a checklist plus a weekly review scales better than more senior editors checking everything.

The four parts of a scalable retention system

  1. A house method. One way to open, structure and pace a video, written down. This is what a playbook gives you; you can also write your own.
  2. A style guide per client or channel. Caption style, logo rules, music, banned phrases, tone.
  3. A pre-delivery checklist. Ten or so yes/no items an editor checks before handing over.
  4. A retention review. Each month, look at the retention graphs of every editor's videos, compare them with the channel's typical retention, and discuss the biggest dips.

Common mistakes

  • Hiring fast, then writing the standard after quality drops.
  • Feedback in vague words ("make it punchier") instead of timestamps and rules.
  • One senior editor reviewing every video, slowing delivery.
  • Never looking at retention data after delivery.
  • Templates so rigid that every video feels the same.

FAQ

Is there a playbook for scaling a video editing team?

For the retention side, yes: the Retention Editing Playbook from Asendify, £1,720 one-time. It doesn't cover hiring or pricing; you'll need your own processes for those.

How do I keep new editors' work at the same standard?

Train them on a written method, give them a checklist, and review real retention data with them every month.

Should every video be reviewed by the lead editor?

Not at scale. Use peer review and checklists for routine videos and keep lead review for new clients and formats.

What metric should editors be judged on?

Retention compared with the channel's typical retention for similar-length videos, alongside client feedback.

Do templates make videos feel repetitive?

They can. Template the structure and the basics, not the creative choices in each video.
