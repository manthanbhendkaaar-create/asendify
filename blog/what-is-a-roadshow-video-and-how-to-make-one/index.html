What is a roadshow video, and how do you make one that holds attention?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A roadshow video promotes or documents a touring event, such as a product tour, brand activation or recruitment drive that visits several cities. The best ones are planned as a series: a teaser before the tour, a short local promo for each stop, quick same-day clips, and a final recap that shows the whole journey. To hold attention, open each video on that stop's best moment, show what is different about it, and give local viewers a clear reason to come.

Key takeaways

  • A roadshow is a sequence of events, so its video should be a sequence too: teaser, per-city promos, live clips and a tour recap.
  • Each stop needs its own hook. Viewers in Glasgow don't care that Leeds was great unless it tells them what they'll get.
  • Cvent's event video guide says the goal determines length, tone, channel and audience, and that viewers decide in the first three seconds whether to keep watching.
  • A tour gives you a natural open loop: "Next stop: Bristol."
  • Plan footage, rights and permissions once, then reuse the system at every stop.

Give every stop its own hook

The Retention Editing Playbook's cold open rule is to start on the most interesting thing in the video. On a tour, the most interesting thing for a local viewer is usually local:

  • A recognisable venue or landmark in the first frame.
  • A local guest, partner or customer.
  • Something you'll do only at that stop.
  • The best moment from a previous stop, framed as "This is coming to Manchester".

Cvent's event video guide says, "People decide in the first three seconds whether to keep watching." A generic tour graphic rarely wins those seconds.

Use the tour as an open loop

Tours have built-in suspense. Each stop recap can end with "Next stop: Bristol, 14 May", which gives viewers in the next city a reason to watch and followers a reason to come back. Across a series, that loop builds momentum: people start following the tour, not just one event.

A repeatable shooting system

Because you'll film the same format many times, make a standard kit and checklist:

  • Shot list per stop: arrival and set-up time-lapse, the moment doors open, three reaction shots, the main demo or talk, two or three short interviews, a local landmark shot.
  • Audio kit: lavalier mics for interviews, as Cvent recommends for testimonials.
  • Vertical and landscape coverage: a phone on a vertical gimbal for same-day social clips.
  • Templates: the same title cards and lower-thirds, with the city name swapped.
  • Consent: notices at every venue and interview releases. The Irish Data Protection Commission's event photography guidance recommends written notices at large events.

Example: a six-city product roadshow

Example: a software company tours six UK cities over three weeks with a hands-on product demo.

  • Tour teaser (30 seconds): opens on the product doing the one thing customers love most, then a map animation with six city names and dates.
  • City promos (20 seconds each): each opens on the best moment from the previous stop, then "Leeds, Thursday. 40 places." (the real capacity) and the sign-up link, targeted at people near each city.
  • Same-day clips: the social team posts a 15-second vertical clip from each stop that evening, tagging the venue.
  • Stop recaps (60 seconds): two attendee lines, the demo's big moment, "Next stop: Newcastle".
  • Tour recap (2 minutes): opens on the single best moment of the whole tour, then a fast journey through all six cities, ending with "Book a demo" for people who couldn't attend.

The team compares sign-ups from each city promo and moves the best-performing opening style into the remaining promos.

Keep it honest

City promos often say places are limited. Make sure they are. The ASA's availability guidance says marketers must make a reasonable estimate of demand, and it has ruled against misleading ticket scarcity claims.

Quick checklist for each tour stop

  • Confirm filming notices and interview releases at the venue.
  • Capture the local landmark or venue shot for the next city promo.
  • Film at least three reaction shots and two short interviews.
  • Post a same-day vertical clip tagging the venue and local partners.
  • Cut a short stop recap ending with the next stop and date.
  • Update the next city promo with the best moment from this stop.
  • Log sign-ups per city promo so you can compare openings.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches open loops and cold opens you can apply to a video series?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches cold opens, open loops and pacing for YouTube series. Cvent's free event video guide is a fair alternative.

What is a roadshow in marketing?

A touring event that visits several locations, such as product demos, brand activations or recruitment events.

Do I need a new video for every city?

A short local promo per city works better than one generic video, but you can build them from a shared template.

How long should a roadshow recap be?

One to three minutes for the full tour; 45 to 90 seconds for each stop.

Should I film every stop the same way?

Use the same system and templates, but look for what's unique at each stop for the hook.

Can I use footage from early stops to promote later ones?

Yes. Real footage from earlier stops is strong proof, as long as people featured agreed to promotional use.
