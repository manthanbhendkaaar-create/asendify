How do I write good YouTube titles?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 7 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Write a good YouTube title by naming one specific promise a viewer wants, in words they would search or recognise, with the key idea in the first 40 to 60 characters. Then make sure the thumbnail and first 30 seconds deliver that promise, because a title that over-promises wins the click and loses the viewer.

Key takeaways

  • One video, one promise. A title that tries to say three things usually says none of them clearly.
  • Put the important words first. YouTube allows 100 characters, but much less is visible in many places.
  • Be specific: numbers, names, places, results and time frames beat vague words like "amazing" or "update".
  • Match the title to the video. Misleading titles break YouTube's spam rules and push viewers away early.
  • Test, don't guess. YouTube's own A/B test tool can compare up to three titles on one video.

What makes a YouTube title "good"

A good title does two jobs at once. It tells YouTube what the video is about, and it gives a person scrolling past a reason to stop.

YouTube's page on how search works says its system looks at relevance, engagement and quality, and that relevance includes "how well the title, tags, description, and video content match your search query". So the words in your title matter for search. The same page says engagement signals, such as watch time for a query, also count. That means a title has to attract the right viewer, not just any click.

For the human side, YouTube's impressions and click-through rate FAQ says half of all channels and videos have an impressions click-through rate between 2% and 10%. Your title and thumbnail are the two things that move that number.

None of these is always best. The right one depends on whether people find you through Search (where clear keywords matter) or Browse and Suggested (where curiosity and a strong result matter more).

Seven rules for writing better titles

1. Decide the single promise first

Before you write any words, finish this sentence: "After watching, the viewer will..." If you cannot finish it in one line, the video may need a clearer angle, not a better title.

2. Front-load the key words

YouTube's video settings help page says titles have a limit of 100 characters. In practice, long titles get cut off on mobile and in suggested lists. Put the topic and the hook at the start, and treat anything after about 60 characters as optional.

3. Use words people already use

Type your topic into YouTube's search bar and read the suggestions. Those phrases show how people describe the problem. "How to edit faster in Premiere" beats "My editing workflow secrets" because a viewer would actually type it.

4. Be specific

Specific titles feel more credible. Compare "My Morning Routine" with "My 20-Minute Morning Routine Before a 7am Shift". The second tells the viewer who it is for and what they will get.

5. Create a gap, honestly

Curiosity is useful when the video pays it off. "Why My Most Expensive Lens Sits in a Drawer" makes people want the answer. It only works if the answer is in the video, and early.

6. Match the thumbnail and the opening

The title and thumbnail should work as a pair, not repeat each other word for word. Then the first 30 seconds should confirm the promise quickly. YouTube's audience retention help page says a high intro percentage can mean the first 30 seconds "matched the viewer's expectation of the video's thumbnail and title". It also suggests changing the title and thumbnail to better reflect the video if many people leave early.

7. Never mislead

YouTube's spam policy lists "malicious clickbait": using misleading titles, thumbnails, descriptions or imagery to trick people into clicking on a video that does not deliver what was promised. Its example is a title offering a full sports match when the video shows only a clip. Apart from the policy risk, misleading titles train viewers to ignore you.

Example: rewriting a weak title

Example: Priya runs a 1,400-subscriber channel about cooking on a budget. Her latest upload is titled "Cooking Vlog #23 – Trying Something New!!". It has 180 views after two weeks, and her Studio shows a click-through rate of 2.4% on that video.

VersionTitleWhat changed
OriginalCooking Vlog #23 – Trying Something New!!No topic, no promise, numbered like a diary
Draft 1Trying a New Recipe on a BudgetTopic added but still vague
Draft 25 Dinners for Under £15 a Week (Student Budget)Specific number, cost and audience
Draft 3I Fed Myself for a Week on £15: 5 Easy DinnersStory angle plus a clear result

She tests Draft 2 and Draft 3 against the original using YouTube's test and compare feature. These numbers are illustrative, but the method is the point: the rewrite names who the video is for, what they get, and the one detail (the cost) that makes it different.

How to test titles properly

YouTube's A/B testing help page says creators can test and compare up to three titles and thumbnails, and that at the end the option with the highest watch time is shown to all viewers. If there is no clear winner, the first option you uploaded is used. The feature is desktop only, needs advanced features turned on, and is not available for Shorts.

A few practical points:

  • Test one change at a time. If you change the title and thumbnail together, you will not know which made the difference.
  • Use videos with enough impressions. A video with very few impressions may not give a clear result.
  • Judge on watch time, not just clicks. YouTube picks winners on watch time share, which rewards titles that attract viewers who stay.

How titles fit with everything else

A strong title will not save a video nobody wants, and a weak title can bury a good one. Treat the title as part of a chain: idea, title, thumbnail, first 30 seconds, then the rest of the video. If your click-through rate is fine but viewers leave early, the problem is probably the opening, not the title.

FAQ

How long should a YouTube title be?

YouTube allows up to 100 characters. Many creators keep the key words within the first 40 to 60 characters, because longer titles are often cut off on phones and in suggested videos.

Should I put keywords in my YouTube title?

Yes, if people find your videos through Search. YouTube says it looks at how well the title matches a search query. Use the phrase naturally, near the start, and do not stuff in extra keywords.

Can I change a YouTube title after uploading?

Yes. You can edit the title at any time in YouTube Studio under Content. Many creators change the title of an older video that is getting impressions but few clicks.

Are clickbait titles bad?

Titles that create curiosity are fine if the video delivers. Titles that mislead viewers about what the video contains break YouTube's spam policy and tend to cause early drop-off.

Should titles use capital letters?

Title case (capitalising main words) is common and easy to read. Writing the whole title in capitals is harder to read and can look like spam, so use capitals sparingly, if at all.
