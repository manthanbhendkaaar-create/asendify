Key takeaways

  • A swipe-away on a Short happens before or right after the video starts, so it shows up first in YouTube's "Stayed to watch" percentage, not as a dip halfway through.
  • YouTube defines "Stayed to watch" as the percentage of times viewers viewed your Shorts versus swiped away, and "engaged views" as views that stayed past the initial seconds, not counting loops.
  • Later drop-offs inside the Short show on its retention data, just as on long videos.
  • On a Short, the first 2 to 3 seconds decide most of the outcome, so tools built for long videos that judge the first 30 seconds are measuring the wrong window.
  • Studio is free and has the core numbers; a tool is only worth paying for if it reads them for you and turns them into changes.

Where YouTube shows swipe-aways

YouTube's help page on understanding your content performance lists the Shorts metrics. Two matter here:

  • Stayed to watch: "The percentage of times that viewers viewed your Shorts versus swiped away."
  • Engaged views: "How many times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds, not including any loops."

The same page says average view duration and average percentage viewed are calculated from engaged views. In other words, people who swiped away straight away are counted in "Stayed to watch" but do not drag down your average view duration.

YouTube's Content tab analytics tips for Shorts shows this in a report called "How many chose to view", which "highlights the percentage of times that viewers viewed your Shorts versus swiped away". It also says watch time and audience retention are key metrics for understanding engagement with Shorts.

Worked example: two Shorts, same topic

Example: two 35-second Shorts about the same kitchen tip.

  • Short A opens on the presenter saying "So today I want to show you something". Stayed to watch: 48%. Of those who stayed, the retention data shows a steady fall to about 55% by the end.
  • Short B opens on the finished result with on-screen text "This saves 10 minutes every morning". Stayed to watch: 71%. Retention holds better and rises slightly at the end, where the last shot loops into the first.

The lesson is in the first two seconds, not the middle: Short A lost more than half its potential viewers before the tip started. A Shorts-aware report would flag the opening of Short A as the drop to fix, and suggest starting on the result. The figures are made up for illustration.

A five-minute routine in Studio

  1. Open Analytics > Content > Shorts and sort your recent Shorts by "Stayed to watch".
  2. Watch the first three seconds of your best two and worst two. Write down what is on screen in frame one.
  3. Open the retention data for the worst one and note any fall after the opening.
  4. Change one thing in the next Short: the first frame, the first line or the length of the set-up.
  5. Compare "Stayed to watch" across the next five Shorts, not just the next one; single Shorts vary a lot.

FAQ

Is there a tool that reads my Shorts' retention and judges the first 2 to 3 seconds?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Doctor checks the first 2 to 3 seconds of each Short and gives a fix for each drop. YouTube Studio's "Stayed to watch" metric is the free alternative.

What does "viewed vs swiped away" mean?

It is the share of times people chose to watch your Short rather than swipe past it. YouTube calls the metric "Stayed to watch".

Do swiped-away views count towards average view duration?

No. YouTube says average view duration is calculated from engaged views, which only count viewers who stayed past the initial seconds.

Why doesn't my Short show key moments?

YouTube only labels key moments on videos at least 60 seconds long with at least 100 views, so many Shorts never get them.

What is a good "Stayed to watch" percentage?

YouTube does not publish an official benchmark. Compare each Short with your own recent Shorts and aim to raise your typical figure over time.
