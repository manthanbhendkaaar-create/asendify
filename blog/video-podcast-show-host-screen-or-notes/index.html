Should a video podcast show the host's notes, screen or laptop on camera?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 7 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Show a screen only when it adds something the viewer cannot get from the audio, and keep notes and laptops out of the main shot. A host glancing down at a laptop gives viewers a reason to look away too, and a visible stack of notes tells them the conversation is scripted. If the screen matters, cut to it full-frame for a few seconds, then return to the faces. This is editing practice, not a proven rule, so check the result on your own retention graph.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's own podcast guidance says "a single webcam shot of the host or hosts talking is incredibly effective" (YouTube Blog). A clean face shot does not need a laptop in it.
  • The same guide says "dynamic editing keeps the viewer's eye engaged" and describes a more produced tier with multiple angles, B-roll and on-screen graphics. Graphics are the right place for anything you would otherwise show on a laptop.
  • YouTube Help says dips in the retention graph mean "viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). Your own graph will tell you whether a laptop moment costs viewers.
  • Nothing in YouTube's published guidance says a laptop or notes on camera lose viewers. The advice below is practical editing judgement. Treat it as a test to run, not a finding.

Why the question matters

On audio-only podcasts nobody sees your notes. On video, everything in the frame is part of the show. Viewers read the frame in the first seconds: who is talking, where they are, and whether this feels like a conversation or a read-out. Three things in the frame can pull attention away from the person speaking:

  1. The host's eyes moving to a screen. Every glance down breaks eye contact with the guest and with the camera.
  2. The screen itself. A bright laptop lid, a second monitor or a phone in shot is a moving target for the eye.
  3. Visible notes. Paper, tablets and printed question lists can signal that the host is following a script rather than listening.

None of these is fatal. Plenty of good shows have notes on the table. The goal is to make sure the viewer's eye goes where you want it to go.

How to handle notes without hurting the edit

  1. Reduce what you need on the table. Put three to five topics on one card instead of a page of questions. Fewer notes means fewer glances.
  2. Look up, then down, then up. If you must read a question, read it before you speak, make eye contact, then ask it. The edit can trim the pause.
  3. Cut the glances in post. On a single-camera show you can jump-cut over the downward look. On a multi-camera show, switch to the guest's camera while the host checks notes.
  4. Do not leave long silences while someone scrolls. If the host needs thirty seconds to find a question, cut it. The viewer never needed to see it.
  5. Keep the guest's name card, not your running order. A name lower third is useful. A running order is for you.

When a screen deserves to be on screen

Sometimes the screen is the content: a guest wants to show a chart, an app, a document or a clip. The viewer needs to see it, but they do not need to watch the host looking at it. A simple pattern works well:

  1. The guest says what they are about to show.
  2. The edit cuts to the screen content at full frame, large enough to read on a phone.
  3. The guest narrates what the viewer is seeing, for as long as the content is useful.
  4. The edit cuts back to the faces as soon as the point is made.

This is the same idea as a B-roll cutaway. The screen becomes an illustration, not a prop. If you need the guest's reaction, use a small picture-in-picture only if the text on screen is still readable. If it is not, go full-screen.

Remote shows

For remote video podcasts, the host's screen is often the guest's face. That is expected. The problem is the other tabs: chat windows, notes and notifications visible in a screen share. Share a single window, not the whole display, and turn off notifications before recording. If a private message pops up on a full-screen share, it is in the recording, and cutting it costs you the take.

How to check it on your own retention graph

You do not need to guess whether a screen moment helps or hurts. YouTube Studio shows where viewers leave.

  1. Publish an episode where you showed the screen for a stretch.
  2. Open the audience retention report. YouTube says "when the line on the chart is flat, it means viewers are watching that part of your video from start to finish," and "spikes appear when more viewers are watching, rewatching, or sharing those parts" (YouTube Help).
  3. Find the timestamp where the screen moment starts. Look at the 15 seconds after it.
  4. A dip right there is a signal to shorten the screen moment or add narration. A spike is a signal that viewers value it.
  5. Run the same check on the next two episodes before you change your style. One graph is an anecdote.

Example: a business podcast with a tool demo

Example: a host interviews a founder about a scheduling app. In the first cut, the host leaves a laptop open in the middle of the table and the founder turns it round to demo. The camera is behind the host, so viewers see the back of a lid and two people looking at something they cannot see. In the re-edit, the editor cuts to a screen recording the founder supplied, keeps it full-frame for 12 seconds with the founder's voice over it, and cuts back to their faces. The laptop lid is lowered in the next recording. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

A simple on-camera checklist

  • Before you record: dim or move laptops, silence phones, close tabs, and put notes on one card.
  • While recording: keep your eyes on the guest, and say out loud when you are about to look at a screen so the editor can cut around it.
  • While editing: cut glances, use full-frame cut-ins for anything the viewer needs to read, and keep graphics on screen long enough to read twice.
  • After publishing: check the retention graph around every screen moment.

Can the host read from notes on camera?

Briefly, yes. Keep notes low, minimise glances and cut long reading pauses in the edit.

Should I show the guest's screen full-frame?

If the viewer needs to read it, yes. Cut to it full-frame, narrate it, and return to the faces.

Does a laptop on the table lose viewers?

There is no published rule saying so. Test it on your retention graph.

What is the simplest fix?

Reduce your notes to one card, silence your phone, and cut the glances.
