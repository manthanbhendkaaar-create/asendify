How do I storyboard a client video ad so it holds attention?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Storyboard a client video ad by planning it in timed beats: the hook in the first seconds, the proposition, one or two proof shots and a clear action, with the picture, sound and on-screen text written for each frame. Storyboard two or three alternative hooks at the same time, so the shoot captures everything you'll need to test. Rough sketches or reference photos are enough.

Key takeaways

  • A storyboard is a timed plan, not artwork: each frame needs a time, a picture, sound and text.
  • Put the hook and the proposition in the first frames. TikTok advises the proposition in the first 3 seconds and the hook in the first 6.
  • Storyboard alternate openings so the shoot gets the footage for hook tests.
  • Plan for sound on and sound off: voice-over and captions should both carry the message.
  • Get client sign-off on the storyboard; it saves expensive changes after the shoot.

Why storyboard an ad at all

Most retention problems in ads are planned in, not edited in. If nobody filmed a strong opening, the editor can't invent one. A storyboard forces the agency and the client to agree what the viewer sees in the first seconds, before anyone books a crew.

Platform guidance tells you what those first seconds need. Google's ABCDs for YouTube ads say to "jump in": reach the core of the story sooner, with engaging pacing and tight framing. They also say to brand "early and throughout" and to include a call to action, reinforced with voice-over (Google Ads Help). TikTok recommends you "prioritize your hook in the first 6 seconds" and "introduce your content proposition in the first 3 seconds" (TikTok Ads Help). Google's production tips add that if you want viewers to take an action, you should "capture your talent demonstrating the action you want people to take" (Google Business).

Step by step

  1. Write the single message. One sentence the ad must land: "Book a boiler service in two minutes, with a fixed price."
  2. Draft the hook options first. Write three different openings: a problem, a result, and a question or bold statement. Each gets its own frame 1.
  3. Plan the middle around proof. What can the camera show that makes the promise believable? Real use beats claims.
  4. Plan the action shot. Show a person doing exactly what you want the viewer to do, as Google suggests.
  5. Write sound and text for every frame. Google notes "95% of YouTube videos have sound", so plan the audio, but captions should still carry the message for muted feeds.
  6. Time it. Read the voice-over aloud with a stopwatch. If the words don't fit, cut words, not frames.
  7. Plan formats. Mark which frames need wider framing so you can crop to 9:16, 1:1 and 16:9 without losing the subject.
  8. Get sign-off. Share the storyboard with the client and agree changes now.

Example: a storyboard for a heating engineer's local ad

Example (a hypothetical client, to show the method): a heating company wants a 20-second ad for boiler servicing.

  • Frame 1A (0 to 2 s): close-up of a cold radiator and a hand touching it, breath visible. Text: "Cold radiators again?"
  • Frame 1B (alternative): engineer finishing a service, the boiler display showing normal pressure. Text: "Serviced in 45 minutes."
  • Frame 1C (alternative): engineer to camera: "Most boiler breakdowns we see in winter start as small faults." (Only if the client's own records support it; otherwise use a different line.)
  • Frame 2 (2 to 5 s): van with logo pulls up, engineer at the door. VO: "Fixed-price boiler services from [company]."
  • Frame 3 (5 to 10 s): engineer checking flue and pressure. Text: "Gas Safe registered" (only if true and the number can be shown).
  • Frame 4 (10 to 15 s): customer handed a printed service record. Text: "Fixed price. No surprises."
  • Frame 5 (15 to 18 s): customer booking on a phone. VO: "Book online in two minutes."
  • Frame 6 (18 to 20 s): logo, phone number, website.

The shoot captures all three openings, so the agency can test 1A, 1B and 1C as separate ads with the same body.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Storyboarding a TV ad for a phone feed. Wide establishing shots and slow logo reveals lose people on mobile.
  • No alternative hooks. One opening means no test, and reshoots are expensive.
  • Over-detailed drawings. Stick figures or reference photos are fine. Time spent on art is time not spent on the hook.
  • Claims nobody checked. Every on-screen claim needs evidence from the client before it goes in the storyboard.
  • Ignoring the edit. Invite the editor to review the storyboard. They'll spot missing cutaways and transitions.

FAQ

Is there a playbook for planning video ad hooks?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, pacing and re-hooks for ads, with templates and a cheat sheet.

Do I need to draw a storyboard?

No. Sketches, reference photos or screenshots from earlier ads all work. What matters is timing, picture, sound and text for each frame.

How many frames should a 30-second ad storyboard have?

Usually six to ten. Each frame should be one beat of the story.

Should the client approve the storyboard?

Yes. Changes on paper are cheap; changes after a shoot are not.

How many hooks should I storyboard?

Two or three alternatives is a practical number for most shoots.

What is the difference between a storyboard and a shot list?

The storyboard shows what the viewer sees and hears over time. The shot list turns that into the camera set-ups the crew needs to film.
