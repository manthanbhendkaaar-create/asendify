How can I find the exact timestamps where viewers drop off in my YouTube videos?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 20 November 2026 · Last updated 20 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Open the video in YouTube Studio, go to its audience retention graph and hover along the line: the timestamp shows under your cursor, and the steepest downward slopes and the dips YouTube highlights are your drop-off points. Write down each timestamp with the percentage before and after it, then watch the five seconds before each one to see what caused it.

Key takeaways

  • The retention graph is per video: Studio > Content > the video > Analytics > Engagement.
  • YouTube marks four kinds of moment: flat lines, gradual declines, spikes and dips. Dips are where viewers skipped or stopped watching.
  • Key moments need at least 60 seconds of video and 100 views, and data takes one to two days to process.
  • A drop-off is a slope, not a point: log where the fall starts and how many percentage points it costs.
  • For many videos at once, the YouTube Analytics API gives the same retention data in small slices you can sort.

Where the timestamps live

YouTube's help page on key moments for audience retention gives the route: sign in to YouTube Studio, open Content, select the video, open Analytics, and find the audience retention report on the Overview or Engagement tab. See more compares the video with others of similar length.

The same page explains the four shapes you will see:

Shape on the graphWhat YouTube says it meansWhat to log
Flat lineViewers watched that part from start to finishNothing; this part works
Gradual declineViewers losing interest over time, normal on YouTubeOnly if steeper than your usual
SpikeMore viewers watching, rewatching or sharing that partTimestamp, to reuse or clarify
DipViewers abandoning or skipping that partTimestamp, % before, % after

YouTube adds a caution about spikes: a spike may mean strong interest, or that something was unclear and people had to rewatch it. So a spike is a timestamp worth watching too.

Step-by-step: build a drop-off log

  1. Wait two days. Retention data can take one to two days to process, so check after that.
  2. Read the intro. Note the share still watching at 0:30. YouTube's Content tab analytics tips say videos with at least 50% still watching at 30 seconds count as above typical intros.
  3. Hover slowly from left to right. Studio shows the timestamp and the percentage still watching under your cursor.
  4. Mark every slope steeper than the grey area. The grey "typical retention" band reflects your last 10 videos of similar length, so anything falling faster than it is a problem specific to this video.
  5. Record start and end of each fall. For example, "4:12 to 4:31, 61% to 49%". That 12-point loss matters more than a gentle 3-point slide over a minute.
  6. Watch five seconds before each fall. Note what happens: a topic change, a sponsor read, a long explanation, a quiet section, a cut.
  7. Rank by cost. Fix the drop that loses the most viewers first, especially early ones, because every later part of the video only reaches the people who stayed.

Doing it for many videos

Hovering works for one video. For a back catalogue, YouTube Studio's Advanced Mode lets you compare videos and groups and export the current view, capped at 500 rows per download. For second-by-second detail across many uploads, the YouTube Analytics API is the source most tools use. It reports audienceWatchRatio ("the absolute ratio of viewers watching the video at the given point"), stoppedWatching (how often a segment was the last one seen in a playback) and relativeRetentionPerformance (how the video compares with similar-length videos, from 0 to 1). The API splits the video into slices: its own example divides a five-minute video into 100 three-second segments.

With that data you can sort every slice by the biggest fall in audienceWatchRatio and get a ranked list of drop-off timestamps, which is what retention tools do behind the scenes.

Worked example

The 0:25 slope costs far more viewers than the other two put together, so the next video should state its question in the first sentence. The sponsor read is second: moving it after the first payoff is a fair test. These figures are illustrative.

Common mistakes

  • Reading the lowest point instead of the steepest slope. The end of every video is low; what matters is where the line falls fastest.
  • Ignoring segments. In Advanced Mode, compare new and returning viewers. A dip only among new viewers often means missing context.
  • Comparing across formats. YouTube's tips warn that audience behaviour differs between Shorts, videos and live, so compare like with like.
  • Logging and never acting. Pick one timestamp per video to fix next time, and check the next upload.

FAQ

Is there a tool that finds the drop-off timestamps for each new upload for me?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Doctor lists the seconds viewers left each new video, with a fix, using read-only access. YouTube Studio is the free, manual alternative.

Why does my retention graph not show key moments?

YouTube only highlights moments on videos at least 60 seconds long with at least 100 views, and only when it detects them.

Can I export retention data by timestamp?

Studio's Advanced Mode exports the current view, up to 500 rows. For second-level retention across many videos, use the YouTube Analytics API.

What counts as a big drop-off?

Any fall steeper than the grey typical retention band for your channel. Rank drops by percentage points lost, not by where the line ends.

Why does the graph go up in places?

That is a spike: viewers rewatched or shared that part. It can mean strong interest or that something was unclear.
