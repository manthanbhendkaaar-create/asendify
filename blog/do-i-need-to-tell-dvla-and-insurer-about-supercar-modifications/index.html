Do I need to tell DVLA and my insurer if I wrap or modify my supercar?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 21 November 2026 · Last updated 21 November 2026

Often yes, to one or both. DVLA requires you to update your V5C log book if you change certain details, including the colour, engine cylinder capacity or fuel type. Your insurer must get accurate answers to its questions: under UK law you must take reasonable care not to misrepresent your car, and if you do, a claim can be cut in proportion or refused entirely. Whether a wrap counts as a colour change for DVLA is a common question, so check with DVLA if the main colour looks different.

This page is general information, not legal or financial advice. Ask DVLA and your insurer about your specific changes.

Key takeaways

  • V5C must be updated for changes including colour, engine cylinder capacity, fuel type, seating capacity and chassis or body.
  • The Consumer Insurance (Disclosure and Representations) Act 2012 requires you to take reasonable care not to misrepresent.
  • Deliberate or reckless misrepresentation lets an insurer avoid the policy, refuse all claims and keep premiums.
  • Careless misrepresentation can lead to the insurer refusing a claim, adding terms, or paying only a proportion.
  • Performance, cosmetic and even some wheel changes are commonly asked about by insurers.

What DVLA needs to know

GOV.UK's guide to changing vehicle details on a V5C says you must update your V5C when you change any of these:

ChangeUpdate V5C?
ColourYes
Engine cylinder capacity (cc)Yes
Fuel type (for example, converting to electric)Yes
Seating capacityYes
Chassis, monocoque body or other listed structural changesYes
Wheels, exhaust, suspension, software remapNot on the list; check insurer instead

The GOV.UK list is longer than shown here; read the full page for your change.

What your insurer needs to know

The Consumer Insurance (Disclosure and Representations) Act 2012, section 2, says it is the consumer's duty to take reasonable care not to make a misrepresentation to the insurer, including when a policy is varied. Failing to confirm or amend details the insurer asks you to check can count as a misrepresentation.

Schedule 1 of the Act sets out the insurer's remedies:

Type of misrepresentationWhat the insurer can do
Deliberate or recklessAvoid the contract, refuse all claims, and usually keep the premiums
Careless, and the insurer would not have insured you at allAvoid the contract and refuse claims, but return premiums
Careless, and the insurer would have added different termsTreat the policy as if those terms applied
Careless, and the insurer would have charged morePay only a proportion of the claim

Worked example: a proportional payout

Example (illustrative): Alex fits a remap and a sports exhaust to his supercar but does not tell his insurer, although the renewal asks about modifications. He pays £2,000 a year. After a crash, the insurer says it would have charged £2,500 with the modifications.

ItemAmount
Premium paid£2,000
Premium the insurer says it would have charged£2,500
Proportion paid on the claim£2,000 ÷ £2,500 = 80%
Repair claim£20,000
Amount paid if the misrepresentation is treated as careless£16,000
Alex pays himself£4,000

This follows the proportional formula in Schedule 1. If the insurer showed the misrepresentation was deliberate or reckless, it could refuse the whole claim. Telling the insurer up front might have cost £500 more a year, far less than the shortfall.

Before you modify

  1. Ask your insurer how the change affects your premium and cover.
  2. Check whether a V5C update is needed on GOV.UK.
  3. Keep invoices and photos of the work.
  4. Check the manufacturer warranty, which some modifications affect.
  5. Re-run your yearly cost figures with the new insurance price.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming cosmetic changes do not count. Insurers often ask about them.
  • Not reading renewal documents. Confirming outdated details can be a misrepresentation.
  • Forgetting DVLA after a colour change.
  • Leaving the insurer to find out after a claim.

How to tell your insurer

  1. Call or use the online account before the work is done, or straight after.
  2. Describe each change precisely: part, brand, and what it changes (power, appearance, wheels).
  3. Ask for the new premium and any change in excess or cover in writing.
  4. Keep the confirmation with your policy documents.
  5. Re-check at every renewal that the listed modifications are still correct.

Wraps, plates and other cosmetic changes

Cosmetic changes are the ones owners most often forget to mention. A full colour-change wrap, new wheels or a body kit can all change how a car looks and how attractive it is to thieves, which is why insurers ask. Clear protective film is usually less of an issue, but it costs nothing to ask. Number plates must still meet DVLA display rules whatever else you change.

Is there a planner that lets you update a supercar's insurance cost and see its new yearly cost?

Yes. Asendify's Supercar Planner lets you edit insurance and other running costs and ranks cars by yearly cost. A spreadsheet is the free alternative.

Do I need to tell DVLA if I change my car's colour?

Yes. Colour is on GOV.UK's list of changes you must update on your V5C.

Do I have to tell my insurer about a remap?

You must answer the insurer's questions accurately, and most ask about performance changes. Tell them.

What happens if I do not declare modifications?

Depending on whether it was careless or deliberate, the insurer may cut or refuse a claim, or cancel the policy.

Does a wrap count as a colour change?

It can if it changes the main colour. Check with DVLA and tell your insurer.

Which law covers insurance disclosure?

The Consumer Insurance (Disclosure and Representations) Act 2012.
