Do multi-part video series keep viewers watching longer?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A multi-part series can keep viewers watching longer, but only when every part works on its own, ends with a genuine reason to continue and points clearly to the next episode. Splitting one video into parts does not create retention by itself: a weak Part 1 loses people before they ever reach Part 2. The gain comes from planning the series so each episode earns its own audience and then hands it on.

Key takeaways

  • There are two kinds of retention in a series: how much of each episode people watch, and how many go on to the next one. Plan for both.
  • A University at Buffalo study published in Media Psychology (UB news release, 2023) found cliffhanger endings were not significantly less enjoyable than positive endings, and the researchers described cliffhangers as a way to encourage people to continue. The study used written stories, not video.
  • YouTube end screens can only be added to the last 5-20 seconds of a video, and the video must be at least 25 seconds long, so the hand-off to the next part has to be planned into the edit.
  • Wistia's 2025 analysis of its customers' videos found "most viewers usually drop off around the halfway point". Splitting a long video does not fix that unless each part is tighter.
  • YouTube's typical retention comparison lets you compare each part with your other videos of similar length.

How to plan a series that holds people

  1. Give every part its own hook and payoff. Viewers arriving at Part 3 from search or recommendations need a reason to stay without having seen Part 1.
  2. Open one big question for the series, answer a smaller one each episode. The big question pulls people forward; the small payoff makes each part satisfying.
  3. Recap in seconds, not minutes. A 10-15 second recap at the start of later parts helps newcomers without boring returning viewers.
  4. End on the next question, not a summary. Put the unresolved point in the last 20 seconds, where the end screen can sit.
  5. Link every part. Use end screens, a pinned comment, the description and a playlist in order.
  6. Release on a predictable schedule. A cliffhanger loses force if Part 2 arrives months later.

Example: a woodworking channel turns a 38-minute table build into a four-part series.

If Part 2's audience is much smaller, the fix is usually a stronger reason to continue at the end of Part 1, or a clearer link to it. This is an illustrative scenario, not a promised result.

When a series hurts retention

  • Padding. Stretching 15 minutes of material into three parts makes each one slower.
  • Locking the payoff away. If Part 1 has no payoff at all, many viewers feel cheated and do not come back.
  • Inconsistent titles. "Part 2" with no topic in the title is hard to find and hard to click.
  • Long gaps. Momentum fades between uploads.

How to measure whether the series is working

Look at three numbers for each part, in this order:

MeasureWhere to find itWhat it tells you
Average percentage viewedYouTube Studio, each video's Engagement tabWhether the part holds attention on its own
Intro (still watching after 30 seconds)The video's key moments for audience retentionWhether the opening of a later part works for newcomers
End screen element clicksThe end screen reportHow many viewers you hand on to the next part

Compare each part with the channel's typical retention for videos of a similar length, not with Part 1. Later parts often have smaller audiences simply because fewer people reach them; what matters is whether the people who do arrive keep watching.

If one part's retention is clearly worse, rewatch it at normal speed on a phone and note where your own attention drifts. The usual causes are a slow recap, a section with no new information, or an ending that summarises instead of pointing forward. Fix that one part before planning the next series. Over two or three series you will learn which kinds of endings your audience follows and which they ignore, which is more useful than any general rule.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on open loops for video series?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops, payoffs and pacing with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. YouTube's analytics help is a free companion.

Should I split a long video into parts?

Only if each part can stand alone with its own hook and payoff. Otherwise, tighten the long video instead.

Do cliffhangers annoy viewers?

Research on written stories found cliffhangers were not significantly less enjoyable than happy endings, but each part still needs a real payoff so viewers do not feel cheated.

How do I get viewers from Part 1 to Part 2?

End on the next open question, add an end screen in the last 20 seconds, and link Part 2 in the description and a pinned comment.

Should series parts be the same length?

Not necessarily. Each part should be as long as its content needs; consistency in quality matters more than in runtime.
