How do I remove filler words from a voiceover quickly in Premiere Pro?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 25 November 2026 · Last updated 25 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Key takeaways

  • Red Shark News describes the workflow: transcribe, click the Filter icon in the Transcript panel, choose Filler, review the highlighted words, then delete them, choosing Extract to close the gap or Lift to leave it (Red Shark News).
  • The same article says the filler option was beta-only when written and "is not 100% effective in detecting filler words yet", and advises reviewing each hit rather than using Delete All.
  • Adobe's beta announcement says detection picks up "uh" and "umm", works in the 18 languages supported by Speech to Text, and lets you change the length threshold for pauses and fillers (Adobe Community).
  • Clips transcribed earlier must be re-transcribed to get filler detection, and one commenter reported the option was removed in a later update, so check your version.
  • Adobe lists pause detection and deletion as part of Text-Based Editing in its help section (Adobe Help).

How the quick method works

Text-Based Editing lets you edit audio and video by editing a transcript. For a voiceover, that is fast: you read the words, select the fillers and delete them, and the audio and video follow. Red Shark News says the approach works best with podcasts, interviews, monologues and any scene with heavy dialogue, and a voiceover is a clean version of that.

It is not a one-click fix. Red Shark News says the workflow still needs review, and mainly gives you a faster assembly cut. Fillers are detected by the software, and the software can miss some or mark words that are not fillers. Think of it as a first pass that saves time, followed by a listen.

Extract or Lift

The delete option decides what happens to the gap.

OptionWhat it doesBest for
ExtractRemoves the section and closes the gap, which ripples later clipsA voiceover on a single track where you want a tight read
LiftRemoves the section and leaves the gapWhen music, sync or other tracks must stay in place
Delete one by oneYou review each hit before removingVoiceovers with deliberate pauses
Delete AllRemoves every highlighted hit at onceOnly for a rough first pass, and then check

Step-by-step: remove fillers from a voiceover

  1. Open the project and select the voiceover clip. Use the original recording, not a bounced mix.
  2. Transcribe. In the Text panel, choose the Transcript tab and click Transcribe. If the clip was transcribed before filler detection existed, transcribe it again.
  3. Check the transcript. Fix obvious errors in names and terms, so you can read the text as you listen.
  4. Open the Filter menu. Click the filter icon beside the search bar and choose Filler. If you do not see it, check your Premiere Pro version, as the article and a commenter note it has changed.
  5. Set the threshold if needed. Adobe says you can change the length threshold for pauses and fillers in the transcript view options.
  6. Review each highlighted word. Play it. Some "ums" are fine as a human beat; some "actually" or "like" may not be flagged.
  7. Delete with Extract for a tight read. Use Lift if other tracks must stay in sync.
  8. Handle pauses separately. Use the Pause filter, and review before you delete, because a pause can be there for effect.
  9. Listen to every cut. Play a few seconds before and after each edit. Look for clipped breaths, odd rhythm or a missing consonant.
  10. Smooth visual jump cuts if you have a picture. Red Shark News suggests a Morph Cut on stable footage, though a voiceover over b-roll will not need one.
  11. Check the pace, not just the words. A voiceover with no breath can sound rushed. Leave a small gap between sentences.
  12. Play the whole thing once. Check the voiceover against the picture and the music.

Example: a 6 minute voiceover for a product explainer

Example: a creator records a 6 minute voiceover with about 40 "ums" and "uhs" and several long pauses. After transcription and the Filler filter, Premiere highlights 34 of them. She reviews each, deletes 31 with Extract and keeps 3 where the pause helps the sentence. She then uses the Pause filter and deletes about a dozen gaps of over a second, keeping two before key lines. On playback she finds two clipped breaths and adds a few frames back. The cleaned read is shorter and tighter, and she saves the time she would have spent scrubbing the waveform. What the software will not tell you

Software finds sounds, not meaning. It will not know that a pause before a key line is the point of the sentence, or that an "um" in a casual story keeps it human. A voiceover with every gap removed can sound like a machine. Leave a little air.

It also will not fix a weak script. If your voiceover needs forty fillers removed, the script may be too loose or the read too rushed. Trimming helps retention, but a clear script helps more.

Check the tool before you rely on it

The Adobe announcement and the Red Shark article both say filler detection began in beta, and a commenter in the Adobe thread reported it removed in a later update. Features change between versions. If you do not see the Filler option, you can still use the transcript to find and delete words by hand and use the pause filter, or use another editor's equivalent.

What is the difference between Extract and Lift?

Extract removes the section and closes the gap. Lift removes it and leaves the gap in place.

Is filler detection reliable?

Not fully. Red Shark News says it is "not 100% effective", so review each hit.

Do I need to re-transcribe old clips?

Yes. Adobe says clips transcribed earlier must be re-transcribed to get filler detection.

Can I delete every filler at once?

You can, but the article advises against it, as some pauses and fillers are deliberate. Review first.
