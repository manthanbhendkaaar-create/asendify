How often should you release podcast episodes to keep listeners?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Release podcast episodes as often as you can keep up without the quality dropping, and then stick to it. Weekly suits most interview and topic shows, fortnightly suits heavily edited ones, and seasons suit story-driven shows. Experts and platforms agree that consistency matters more than the exact number: listeners return to shows that turn up when they said they would.

Key takeaways

  • No platform sets a required release frequency.
  • Consistency is the common advice: missing releases is an early warning sign of a show fading out.
  • Pick the cadence you can sustain for a year, not the one you can manage for a month.
  • Seasons are a valid choice: be consistent within the season and tell listeners when you'll be back.
  • Keep at least one finished episode in reserve so a bad week doesn't break the schedule.

What the experts and platforms say

Spotify doesn't recommend a number. Its guide to podcast schedules says publishing can be "an episode every day or every week", but warns that when creators don't stick to a steady schedule, "it can be the first sign of" podfading. It also suggests that "ideally, you should have at least one episode scheduled for each one you release" (Spotify for Creators).

Pacific Content, a podcast production company, is equally clear: "There's no one-size-fits-all podcast release cadence." What it does recommend for nearly everyone is "consistency over time", which it describes as "showing up for your listeners, episode after episode, season after season, year after year" (Pacific Content). It also notes that limited-run and season-based shows can be consistent within each season, even with long breaks between seasons.

How your show type is set also changes what new listeners see. Apple shows episodic podcasts "in reverse chronological order with the newest episode at the top", while serial shows put the first episode at the top (Apple Podcasts for Creators). For an episodic show, a long gap means the newest episode at the top of your page gets old.

How to choose your cadence

  1. Time one episode end to end. Booking, research, recording, editing, show notes and clips. Be honest. If one episode takes 12 hours and you have 8 spare hours a week, weekly isn't sustainable.
  2. Pick the cadence you could hold for a year. Spotify's warning about podfading is the point here: a schedule you break in month three does more harm than a slower one you keep.
  3. Build a buffer. Record two or three episodes before launch, and try to stay at least one ahead, as Spotify suggests.
  4. Choose a fixed day. Same day each week or fortnight. Say it in your trailer and show notes.
  5. If you take a break, announce it. For seasons, end the last episode by saying when the next season starts, and publish a short update in the feed if plans change.
  6. Review after three months. If you're always behind, slow down. If you have a growing backlog of great material, consider speeding up.

Example: choosing between weekly and fortnightly

Example (hypothetical numbers, to show the method): a solo host with a full-time job and about 6 hours a week for the show.

  • Each interview episode takes about 9 hours: 1 hour booking and research, 1.5 hours recording, 5 hours editing, 1.5 hours for show notes and two clips.
  • Weekly would need 9 hours a week, 3 more than available. Within a couple of months the host would fall behind.
  • Fortnightly needs 4.5 hours a week, leaving 1.5 hours of slack for bad weeks.
  • The host launches fortnightly on Tuesdays with three episodes already finished, so they're six weeks ahead on day one. After three months, if the buffer is still there, they can consider adding short solo episodes in the off weeks.

Frequency is not the same as retention

Releasing more often won't fix episodes people don't finish. Within each episode, retention comes from structure: a strong opening, a reason to keep listening and a clean ending. Between episodes, it comes from trust that the next one will be worth it and will arrive on time. If your retention graphs show people leaving early, fix the episodes first and the schedule second.

FAQ

Is there a book that helps podcasters keep listeners coming back?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops, re-hooks and endings for podcasts, clips and YouTube, with a 30-day plan in the Bonus Pack.

Is weekly the best schedule for a podcast?

Weekly is common for interview and topic shows, but there's no single best schedule. The best one is the one you can keep.

Does releasing more often grow a podcast faster?

Not on its own. More episodes only help if they're good and you can keep the pace.

Is it bad to take a break between seasons?

No. Season-based shows can be consistent within each season. Tell listeners when you'll be back.

What day should I release episodes?

Pick a fixed day and stick to it. Consistency matters more than the specific day.

What should I do if I miss a release?

Publish as soon as you can, say briefly what happened if it affects listeners, and rebuild your buffer.
