How do I script a "how I would invest £1,000" video so viewers do not leave in the intro?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Open on the plan, not on yourself. In a "how I would invest £1,000" video the viewer clicked for a specific allocation, so the first 30 seconds should show the answer in outline, say who the plan is for in one sentence, and promise the reasoning that follows. Put the risk wording inside the script as a short, honest line rather than a long preamble that pushes the answer back. This is education about video structure, not financial advice.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's own report defines the intro as "what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds" (YouTube Help). For this format, that 30-second number is the first thing to protect.
  • The same help page says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video", so a long preamble shows up as a dip you can see.
  • The FCA says financial promotions "should be fair, clear and not misleading" and should "provide a balanced view of the benefits and risks" (FCA). Balanced wording is a script task, and it can be short and early.
  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded, rather than included" (Dartmouth). Cut the autobiography; keep the plan.

Why the intro of this format is risky

"How I would invest £1,000" is a promise of a list: a split, a reason for each part, and a next step. Creators often open with who they are, why investing matters and a long disclaimer, which delays that list. Viewers who came for the split have no reason to stay through three minutes of context. The retention graph is blunt about it: a dip is a dip, and the first one usually sits in the opening half-minute.

There is also a trust problem specific to money content. Viewers have seen too many "I turned £1,000 into..." videos. A calm, specific opening ("here is the split, here are the assumptions, here is what I am not covering") separates your video from those without promising anything you cannot deliver.

Notice that the risk line sits at the third beat, in the viewer's first 20 seconds, spoken aloud and shown on screen. That keeps it prominent without making it the entire intro. Check the current FCA guidance and, if you are regulated or promoting a regulated product, take proper compliance advice; this page is not legal or financial advice.

How to script it, step by step

  1. Write the allocation first. Before the intro, write the final split on one line. If you cannot state the plan in one sentence, the video is not ready.
  2. State assumptions as a short list. Time horizon, tax wrapper (for example a UK ISA), and whether an emergency fund exists. Assumptions are not padding; they stop the comments saying "but what about debt".
  3. Draft the intro last. Write the body, then lift the clearest sentence into the first five seconds.
  4. Keep each section to one decision. One section per slice of the £1,000. Each section opens with its number ("£400 goes into...") so the viewer always knows where they are.
  5. Show numbers once, then say them. Mayer's redundancy and signalling ideas apply: put the figure on screen and narrate the reason, rather than reading every figure aloud (Dartmouth).
  6. Add a re-hook before each new slice. A single line such as "the next part is where most beginners get the cash question wrong" gives a reason to stay through the transition.
  7. Chapter it. YouTube requires the first timestamp at 00:00, at least three timestamps in ascending order and chapters of at least 10 seconds (YouTube Help). Chapters let viewers jump to the slice they care about without leaving the video.

What to cut from the intro

  • A long personal story. One clause of credibility is enough: what you are, not your life story.
  • "Before we start, like and subscribe." Move it to a natural pause after the first payoff.
  • A stacked disclaimer wall. Say the honest line once, clearly, then show it. Repeat it where you discuss a specific product.
  • Past returns as a hook. Quoting a recent strong year as the opener can look misleading. If you show performance, label the period and the source on screen.
  • Branding animation. A five-second logo sting costs a sixth of the 30-second window that YouTube measures for intro retention.

Example: two openings for the same video

Example: a creator posts "How I would invest £1,000 as a beginner". Version A opens with a 50-second story about their first investing mistake, a subscribe request and a text disclaimer, then gets to the plan at 1:40. Version B opens with the split on screen, one line of assumptions, the risk sentence and the promise, then starts part one at 0:35. Checking the graph after you publish

Look at three things. First, the intro figure in the key moments section. Second, whether the first dip lines up with a specific beat, such as the story or the disclaimer. Third, spikes: YouTube notes spikes can mean viewers rewatched a part, and can also mean "your content isn't clear and your audience had to rewatch a section", so a spike on your allocation graphic may mean it is too dense. Note that YouTube only flags key moments when "your video should also be at least 60 seconds long and have at least 100 views", so give a new upload time before drawing conclusions.

Wistia's marketing data suggests engagement "naturally declines after the five-minute mark" (Wistia). That is general marketing video data, not finance-specific, but it is a reason to give a £1,000 plan clear sections and a re-hook rather than one flat stretch.

How long should the intro be?

Aim to reach the first real step within about 30 to 45 seconds, because 30 seconds is the window YouTube's intro metric measures.

Where should the risk warning go?

Early, short and visible: spoken once in the first 20 seconds and shown on screen, then repeated where a specific product is discussed. Check current FCA guidance for your situation.

Should I show last year's returns in the intro?

Be careful. If you show performance, label the period and source, and keep a balanced view of risk and benefit.

Do I need chapters?

They help. Use at least three timestamps, starting at 00:00, each at least 10 seconds long.
