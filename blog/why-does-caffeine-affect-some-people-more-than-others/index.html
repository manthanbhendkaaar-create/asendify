Why does caffeine affect some people more than others?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Caffeine affects people differently mainly because of genes and the liver. Over 95% of caffeine is broken down by one liver enzyme, CYP1A2, and gene variants make some people fast and others slow at clearing it, so its half-life ranges from about 1.5 to 10 hours in adults. Pregnancy, the contraceptive pill, smoking, age and regular use change it further.

Key takeaways

  • The same coffee can last a few hours in one person and well over a day's worth of hours in another.
  • Genes affect two things: how fast you clear caffeine (CYP1A2) and how strongly your brain responds to it (adenosine receptor variants).
  • Pregnancy and hormonal contraceptives slow caffeine clearance; smoking speeds it up.
  • Regular users build tolerance, so they feel less from the same dose.
  • You cannot see your genes, but you can watch your own response, especially your sleep, and adjust.

This page is general information, not medical advice. If caffeine gives you palpitations, chest pain or severe anxiety, see a doctor or GP.

Reason 1: how fast your liver clears caffeine

The International Society of Sports Nutrition's position stand on caffeine says more than 95% of caffeine is metabolised in the liver by the enzyme CYP1A2. The average half-life, the time it takes for half the caffeine to leave your body, is usually reported as 4 to 6 hours. But it may range from 1.5 to 10 hours in adults.

That is a huge spread. With a 1.5 hour half-life, a morning coffee is mostly gone by lunch. With a 10 hour half-life, half of it is still in you at bedtime.

Part of this is genetic. The ISSN describes people with the AA version of the CYP1A2 gene as fast metabolisers and those with AC or CC as slow metabolisers. A review in Pharmacological Reviews found that this variant, plus another in an enzyme called NAT2, contributes to large differences in how people process caffeine (Nehlig, 2018).

Reason 2: how strongly your brain responds

Clearance is only half the story. Caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors in the brain. Nehlig's review notes that several variants in the adenosine receptor gene affect how people respond to caffeine, and that genetic differences are linked to caffeine's effects on sleep.

So two people with the same caffeine level in their blood can feel very different. One may feel pleasantly alert. The other may feel jittery, anxious or wide awake at midnight. The US Food and Drug Administration describes this as wide variation in both how sensitive people are to caffeine and how fast they metabolise it.

Reason 3: hormones, smoking, age and health

Your genes set a baseline. Other factors shift it.

This is one reason official limits are lower in pregnancy. The European Food Safety Authority says up to 400 mg a day is fine for healthy adults, but advises up to 200 mg a day in pregnancy and while breastfeeding.

It also explains why people who stop smoking sometimes find that their usual coffee suddenly feels much stronger. If you are quitting smoking, it is worth mentioning your caffeine intake to your GP or stop-smoking adviser.

Reason 4: tolerance

If you drink caffeine every day, your body adapts and the same dose feels weaker. Someone who has four coffees a day may barely notice one cup, while someone who rarely drinks caffeine may feel a single cup for hours. Tolerance does not change the amount of caffeine in your blood; it changes how much you notice it.

That is also why people sometimes say caffeine "suddenly" affects them more. After a break, a holiday without coffee or a cut-down, tolerance falls and a normal dose hits harder. Our guide to resetting caffeine tolerance explains how this works.

Example: two people, the same 3 pm coffee

Example: Leah and Omar both drink a 100 mg coffee at 3 pm and go to bed at 11 pm, eight hours later. The numbers below use simple half-life maths and are illustrations, not a test of anyone's body.

Leah (fast, 3 h half-life)Omar (slow, 8 h half-life)
Caffeine at 3 pm100 mg100 mg
After 1 half-life50 mg at 6 pm50 mg at 11 pm
At 11 pm (8 hours)about 16 mgabout 50 mg

Leah has roughly a sixth of the dose left at bedtime. Omar has half of it, the same as drinking half a coffee just before bed. EFSA notes that 100 mg close to bedtime may affect sleep in some adults, so Omar's 3 pm cup may be a real problem for him while Leah barely notices hers.

If Omar moves his last coffee to noon, he has about 23 mg left at 11 pm, much closer to Leah's level.

How to work out your own sensitivity

You cannot see your CYP1A2 gene, but you can run a simple experiment.

  1. Keep a log for a week. Write down every caffeine drink, its time and roughly how much.
  2. Note your sleep. How long did it take to fall asleep? Did you wake in the night? How did you feel in the morning?
  3. Move your last caffeine earlier. Try a cut-off of noon or 1 pm for the next week.
  4. Compare. If your sleep improves a lot, you may be on the slower or more sensitive side.
  5. Check how you feel after one cup. Jitters, a racing heart or anxiety from a small dose suggest high sensitivity. See our page on why coffee makes some people anxious and jittery.

A 2023 meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews found that a standard coffee should be drunk at least 8.8 hours before bed to avoid losing sleep (Gardiner et al., 2023). That is an average. Slow clearers may need longer; our guide on how long caffeine stays in your system goes into the timeline.

FAQ

Am I a slow caffeine metaboliser?

Only a genetic test can say for sure, but strong clues are poor sleep after afternoon coffee and feeling a single cup for many hours. Moving your cut-off earlier for a week is a practical test.

Why does caffeine affect me more than it used to?

Common reasons include lower tolerance after cutting down, starting hormonal contraception, pregnancy, or stopping smoking. If the change is sudden and you have symptoms such as palpitations, see a GP.

Why does coffee not affect some people at all?

They may be fast metabolisers, have adenosine receptor variants that make them less sensitive, or have high tolerance from regular use. Caffeine is still in their body even if they do not feel it.

Does body weight change how caffeine affects you?

Dose per kilogram matters. Sports guidance uses mg per kg, so a lighter person gets a bigger effective dose from the same drink.

Can caffeine sensitivity change over time?

Yes. Tolerance, hormones, age, smoking and health conditions can all change how you respond to caffeine over the years.
