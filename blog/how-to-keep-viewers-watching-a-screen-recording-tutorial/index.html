How do you keep viewers watching a screen-recording tutorial on YouTube?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

To keep viewers watching a screen-recording tutorial, show the finished result in the first seconds, cut every loading screen and hesitation, zoom in on the part of the screen that matters, and split the video into clear chapters so people can find their step. Research on edX videos found students watched only about 2 to 3 minutes of each tutorial regardless of length, and rewatched tutorials more than lectures. So design for skimming and rewatching, not one straight viewing.

Key takeaways

  • In the edX study by Guo, Kim and Rubin, students engaged 1.5x to 2x as long with Khan-style hand-drawn tutorials as with slides or code screencasts (paper PDF).
  • The same study recommends adding "support for re-watching and skimming" to tutorials.
  • Richard Mayer's research summary reports that cues highlighting the structure (signalling) and cutting extraneous material (coherence) both help people learn (Mayer PDF).
  • Chapters, zooms and on-screen labels are the practical versions of those principles.
  • A flat, unedited screen recording is where tutorials most often lose viewers.

Why screen recordings lose viewers

Screen recordings are cheap to make and easy to make badly. Common problems:

  • Waiting. Software loading, files saving, progress bars.
  • Hunting. The cursor wandering while the presenter looks for a menu.
  • Tiny text. A full 4K desktop shrunk onto a phone screen.
  • No destination. The viewer does not know what they are building until the end.
  • One long take. No chapters, so viewers who need step 7 have to scrub.

What research on tutorial videos found

The largest dataset comes from Guo, Kim and Rubin's 2014 study of 6.9 million edX viewing sessions.

These studies are about learning, not YouTube retention specifically, but the two tend to move together in tutorials: a viewer who understands keeps watching.

How to edit a screen-recording tutorial

1. Show the result first

Open with five to ten seconds of the finished thing: the spreadsheet working, the website live, the effect applied. Then say what the viewer will be able to do. This answers "is this the right video?" straight away.

2. Cut every wait

Remove loading bars, saving, rendering and the cursor drifting. Use a jump cut or a quick "a moment later" text if needed. Viewers do not need to watch your computer think.

3. Zoom to the action

Crop and zoom so the menu or field you are using fills the frame. Most viewers watch on phones. If text is not readable on a phone, zoom further.

4. Highlight what to click

Use a box, arrow or cursor highlight for each click. Say the menu path out loud ("File, Export, Media") and show it on screen.

5. Add chapters

Break the tutorial into steps with timestamps in the description. Our guide on adding chapters to YouTube videos explains the format.

6. Keep a human in it

A voice that thinks out loud ("I'm doing this first because...") holds attention better than silent clicking. A small face inset can help some viewers, though not all want it.

7. End with the common mistake

Before the end, show the most common error and how to fix it. Viewers who hit that error will rewatch that part.

Example: re-editing a 25-minute spreadsheet tutorial

A creator's raw recording of building a budget template runs 25 minutes. The retention graph shows a steep early drop and almost nobody past 10 minutes.

  1. Open on the finished budget with charts updating as numbers change (8 seconds).
  2. Cut 6 minutes of typing sample data; replace with a pre-filled sheet and one sentence.
  3. Cut 3 minutes of searching menus and re-doing mistakes.
  4. Zoom every formula to fill the frame; add an on-screen label with the exact formula.
  5. Add eight chapters, one per section of the budget.
  6. Add a final 40-second section on the most common formula error.

The new video runs about 12 minutes and covers the same content. The creator then compares the first 30 seconds and the chapter-by-chapter dips against their typical retention.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Recording at a huge resolution and not zooming.
  • Leaving notifications, bookmarks or personal files visible.
  • Saying "as you can see" when the viewer cannot, because the text is tiny.
  • Explaining what you will do for two minutes before doing anything.

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops, re-hooks and pattern interrupts, with a Bonus Pack including a 30-day plan. Mayer's free research summary is a useful starting point.

How long should a screen-recording tutorial be?

As long as the task needs, with nothing extra. In the edX study, viewers watched only 2-3 minutes of a tutorial on average, so chapters matter.

Should I show my face in a tutorial?

It can help, but it is not essential. A clear voice and readable screen matter more.

What frame rate should I record my screen at?

For most software tutorials, 30fps is enough. Use 60fps if you show fast motion, such as gaming.

Should I add background music?

Keep it very quiet or leave it out. Music that competes with your voice makes instructions harder to follow.

Do chapters hurt watch time?

They let viewers skip, but they also stop viewers leaving to find another video. Judge by your own analytics.
