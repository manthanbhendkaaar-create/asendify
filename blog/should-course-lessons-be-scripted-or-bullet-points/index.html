Should course creators script their lessons word for word or talk from bullet points?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 26 November 2026 · Last updated 26 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Neither extreme is ideal. A full word-for-word script gives tight, caption-friendly lessons but can sound read out. Bullet points sound natural but ramble. Most course creators do best with a loose script: write it out, read it aloud, then record from a short outline of each point.

Key takeaways

  • Guo, Kim and Rubin found lectures planned for online use drew more engagement than reused classroom lectures (problem attempts of 55% versus 41% in the two courses compared), and recommend bringing out the instructor's enthusiasm.
  • A Loom (Atlassian) guide suggests a two-column script, reading it aloud several times and adding pauses and emphasis until it sounds conversational.
  • The University of Oxford says speaking at a normal, steady pace improves automatic caption accuracy and suggests providing scripts or notes where captions are inaccurate.
  • The edX study is correlational and does not test scripted against unscripted lessons directly.

What each approach does

A full script forces you to decide what to say before you record. It shortens lessons, removes repetition and gives you a transcript for captions. The risk is delivery: reading from a page can flatten your voice and remove the energy that holds viewers.

Bullet points do the opposite. You sound like a person talking, but you wander, repeat yourself and forget the example. The edit then takes longer.

The edX study did not compare the two, but its findings point to planning: lessons made for online viewing drew more engagement than recycled live lectures, and the authors recommend investing in pre-production. They also found that engagement often rose with a higher speaking rate, which they link to enthusiasm rather than speed alone.

Steps for a loose script

  1. Write one outcome per lesson. What can the learner do after it?
  2. Write the full text once. Do not worry about style yet.
  3. Use a two-column layout. Narration on one side, screen action on the other, as the Loom guide suggests.
  4. Read it aloud. Change any sentence you stumble on or would not say.
  5. Add pauses and emphasis in the margin where you want them.
  6. Cut it by a quarter. Remove repeats and side points.
  7. Turn each paragraph into a bullet. The bullet becomes your outline.
  8. Record from the outline with the script nearby for numbers and definitions.
  9. Keep a steady pace. Oxford says a normal, steady pace helps captions.
  10. Edit the pauses and false starts and check the captions against your script.

Example: a 6-minute lesson on pricing a service

Example: a creator writes a 900-word script for a lesson on hourly versus fixed pricing. Read aloud, it runs 8 minutes. After cutting repeated points the script is 650 words, about 5 minutes at a conversational pace. The creator turns it into six bullets: the promise, hourly pricing, fixed pricing, a worked example with numbers, a choice rule and the next lesson. The recording takes two takes. Numbers are read from the script. The auto-captions match the script closely enough that the creator only fixes two product names. This scenario uses invented numbers and is not a measured result.

When to script more

Script more for numbers, formulas, legal or safety content, and anything where wrong words matter. Script the first 20 seconds of every lesson even if the rest is from bullets, because the opening decides whether viewers stay.

When to script less

Script less for stories, reactions and opinions, where a natural voice carries the point. Use bullets for these and record more than one take.

Do not read from the screen

If you use a teleprompter or script on the screen, place it close to the camera and rehearse so your eyes do not move across the page. The Loom guide's advice to read aloud several times helps here.

Check your retention graph afterwards. YouTube Help says dips mean viewers are abandoning or skipping at that moment. If dips match robotic stretches, loosen the script there.
