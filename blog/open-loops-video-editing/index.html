Open Loops in Video Editing: How to Keep Viewers Watching

By Manthan Bhendkar · 11 min read · Updated 21 October 2026

Short answer: An open loop is a question, promise or unfinished story you raise early and pay off later, so leaving feels like missing the answer. An open loop is a question, promise or unfinished story that the viewer wants closed. As long as it stays open, leaving the video feels like missing something. Open loops are one of the most reliable tools for holding attention, in long YouTube videos, Shorts, Reels and short ads alike.

What an open loop actually is

An open loop is any moment where you make the viewer curious and deliberately delay the answer. A few common forms:

  • The promise. "There's one mistake almost everyone makes here. I'll show you at the end."
  • The unanswered question. "So why did the second version get ten times the views?" followed by the setup, not the answer.
  • The unfinished story. Start a story, stop at the turning point, and come back to it later.
  • The visual tease. Show a result for a second, then cut away to how you got there.

The key is that the viewer can feel the gap. A loop only works if the answer is something they genuinely want.

Why open loops work

People find unfinished things hard to let go of. A clear question with a delayed answer creates a small, steady pull to keep watching. In editing terms, a good open loop flattens the drop on your retention graph: instead of viewers leaving after the first idea is delivered, they stay for the payoff you promised.

Where to place open loops

  1. In the hook (first 5 to 30 seconds). Open the biggest loop here: the result, the twist or the key lesson the video builds to. Our guide to hooks in the first 30 seconds covers the rest of a strong opening.
  2. Before every slow section. Right before setup, context or explanation, open a smaller loop so the viewer has a reason to sit through it.
  3. At the midpoint. Most videos sag in the middle. A fresh loop ("but this is where it gets interesting") resets attention.
  4. Stacked, not single. Close one loop and open the next in the same breath, so there is always at least one question open.

How to edit open loops in

Open loops are written, but editing decides whether they land:

  • Cold open on the payoff. Pull the most interesting moment from later in the video and put two or three seconds of it at the very start.
  • Cut before the answer. End a section on the question, not on the explanation. Move the answer later in the timeline.
  • Use a visual reminder. A quick on-screen callback ("still coming: the mistake") keeps a long loop alive.
  • Pattern interrupts around the loop. A change of angle, B-roll, zoom or sound effect right after a loop is opened stops the viewer from drifting.
  • Short-form version. In a 20 to 45 second Short or ad, open the loop in the first line and close it in the last second. One loop is usually enough.

Real open-loop lines you can adapt

These are the kinds of lines we use on client videos to open loops. Each one names something specific and says it is coming:

  • "The third one is the one that actually made me money, and it's the opposite of what I expected."
  • "I'll show you the exact trade in a minute, but first you need to understand why the obvious move fails."
  • "There's a catch with this. A big one. I'll get to it."
  • "Keep this number in your head. It comes back at the end."

Notice what they have in common: a concrete payoff (the third one, the exact trade, the catch, the number) and a signal that it will be delivered in this video.

An example script with a timeline

Here is how the loops sit on the timeline of a 10-minute finance explainer titled "3 numbers I check before buying any dividend stock". It follows the interrupt map from Part 7 of our book:

  • 0:00 Cold open on the surprising number. The title's question is the main loop.
  • 0:20 Loop opens: "The third one is the one that matters."
  • 1:50 Loop opens: "There's a catch, in a second."
  • 2:50 Payoff 1 closes (number one, delivered quickly and simply), and loop 2 opens in the same breath.
  • 4:00 The 40% re-hook: the stakes are raised and the visual style changes.
  • 4:30 Loop opens: "The stock that fooled me."
  • 5:00 Payoff 2 closes (number two, more surprising), loop 3 opens.
  • 7:00 Loop opens: "Keep this number in your head."
  • 7:30 Payoff 3 begins, delivered in stages.
  • 8:00 The number comes back.
  • 9:00 Payoff 3 closes, and the full system is summarised in one line.
  • 9:30 Ramp to the next video and the end screen, with no "that's all for today".

At no point between 0:20 and 9:00 is the viewer without an unanswered question, and the best payoff is last. This is the payoff ladder: deliver the parts of the title's promise in rising order of value, so the best thing is always still ahead.

When to open and when to close a loop

  • Open the first loop before 0:30. The title and thumbnail open it; the first line confirms it.
  • Open a loop right before anything slow. Setup, context, a sponsor read. A sponsor read belongs after the first payoff, under 45 seconds, with a loop open going into it.
  • Close a loop only when the next one is ready. The sentence that delivers a payoff should end on the next question.
  • Keep the main loop open until the last 10%. If the title's question is fully answered early and the video keeps going, the graph usually steps down at the answer. Pay it off in stages instead.
  • Use the chapter edges. The last 5 seconds of every chapter open the next loop; the first 5 seconds of a chapter are a mini cold open.

Stacked loops

In longer videos, one loop is not enough. Stacking means running two or three loops at different scales: the main question of the video, the question of the current section, and a short question inside a scene. When one closes, another is still open, so there is never a clean exit point. In the timeline above, from 1:50 to 2:50 three loops are open at once: the title's question, "the third one" and "the catch". Open loops vs clickbait

An open loop is a promise. Clickbait is a broken promise. If you tease something, deliver it, clearly and fully. Viewers who feel tricked leave and do not come back, and your retention and returning-viewer numbers drop. The rule is simple: never open a loop you are not going to close with something worth the wait.

A simple exercise

Take your last video and write down every question a viewer might want answered. Then check your timeline: where is each question raised, and where is it answered? If answers come right after the questions, move them later. If there is a stretch longer than about 30 to 60 seconds with no open question, add one. Then compare the retention graph of your next upload. Our guide to reading your retention graph shows what to look for.

Frequently asked questions

What is an open loop in video editing?

A question, promise or unfinished story the video raises and deliberately answers later, so leaving feels like missing the answer. Editing decides where it opens, how long it stays open and when it pays off.

When should you open and close open loops?

Open the first loop before 0:30, keep at least one loop open at every point until the last 10% of the video, and close each loop with a real payoff while opening the next one in the same breath.

How many open loops should a video have?

Enough that one is always open. In a 10-minute video that usually means a new structural moment (a loop, a re-hook or a question) every 20 to 40 seconds, with two or three loops open at the same time at most.

What is a stacked open loop?

Several loops running at different scales at once: the main question of the video, the question of the current section and short questions inside scenes. When one closes, another is still open.

Are open loops clickbait?

Not if you close them. An open loop is a promise; clickbait is a broken promise. Never open a loop you will not pay off with something worth the wait.
