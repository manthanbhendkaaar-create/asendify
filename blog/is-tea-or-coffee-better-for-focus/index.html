Is tea or coffee better for focus?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 27 October 2026 · Last updated 4 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

For most healthy adults, neither wins outright. Coffee gives a stronger, faster lift for a demanding block of work, while tea gives a gentler lift that is easier to keep steady and less likely to make you jittery. A good default is coffee for your hardest morning task and tea for later sessions, keeping doses moderate and sleep intact.

Key takeaways

  • Coffee usually carries more caffeine per cup; tea carries less, plus some L-theanine.
  • Coffee suits a short, demanding task; tea suits long sessions and afternoons.
  • For focus, moderate doses tend to help most, and bigger doses mainly add jitters and dips.
  • Stop caffeine about 9 hours before bed.
  • Keep within 400 mg a day and 200 mg at once, counting tea too.

This page is education for healthy adults aged 18 and over. It is general information, not medical advice. If you have symptoms or a health condition, speak to a doctor or pharmacist.

The main difference: how much caffeine

The biggest difference between tea and coffee is the dose. The NHS gives rough figures of about 75 mg in a cup of tea and about 100 mg in a cup of instant coffee, and notes that filter and coffee-shop coffee can contain more. The US FDA puts regular brewed coffee at 113 to 247 mg per 12-ounce cup.

That matters because for focus, small to moderate doses tend to help most. Bigger doses mostly add side effects: shaky hands, a racing mind and a sharper dip later. Tea makes it easy to stay in a moderate range. Coffee makes it easy to overshoot.

The second difference: L-theanine

Tea contains L-theanine, an amino acid found naturally in tea leaves. In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in Biological Psychology in 2008 (Haskell and colleagues), 150 mg of caffeine combined with 250 mg of L-theanine improved several measures of attention and reaction time, and reduced headache and tiredness ratings. The authors suggested the combination may act differently from caffeine alone.

Two honest caveats:

  • A normal cup of tea contains far less L-theanine than the amount used in that study, so a cup of tea is not the same as the study drink.
  • Studies like this are small and short, and people respond differently.

So it is fair to say tea's mix of less caffeine plus some L-theanine is a plausible reason many people find it "calmer". It is not proven that tea makes you focus better than coffee.

When coffee is the better choice

  • A short, demanding block of work first thing, such as writing, analysis, revision or coding.
  • After a normal night's sleep, when you want to sharpen up, not mask exhaustion.
  • When you can control the dose, for example a regular mug made at home rather than a large coffee-shop drink.

When tea is the better choice

  • Long sessions where you want steady attention for hours rather than a single spike.
  • If coffee makes you jittery or anxious. A lower dose per cup is often enough to fix it.
  • Afternoons, because less caffeine late in the day is kinder to your sleep.
  • Stressful days such as exams, interviews or presentations, when you already have plenty of adrenaline.

A simple tea-and-coffee focus plan

  1. Plan your hardest task first. Put your most demanding work in the morning.
  2. Coffee 15 to 30 minutes before it starts. One regular coffee is enough for most people.
  3. Tea for the second session. A cup of black or green tea late morning or with lunch.
  4. Water between drinks. It stops you reaching for another coffee out of habit.
  5. Stop caffeine about 9 hours before bed. A 2023 meta-analysis by Gardiner and colleagues (open-access copy) estimated that a normal coffee should be taken at least 8.8 hours before bedtime to avoid losing sleep. If tea is your afternoon drink, keep it early too.
  6. Count the day's total. Add every coffee, tea, cola, energy drink and pre-workout.

The safety lines

The European Food Safety Authority's caffeine guidance says that for healthy adults, single doses up to 200 mg and daily intakes up to 400 mg from all sources do not raise safety concerns. Tea counts towards that total too: five or six strong mugs add up.

Some people should avoid or limit caffeine and ask a GP or pharmacist first: anyone pregnant or breastfeeding (the NHS advises no more than 200 mg a day in pregnancy), people with heart conditions or high blood pressure, people with anxiety or panic disorders, people with sleep problems, anyone taking medicines that interact with caffeine, and anyone under 18.

Never use caffeine powders or highly concentrated liquid caffeine, and never mix caffeine with alcohol. L-theanine supplements are a separate product; if you want to try one, ask a pharmacist first, especially if you take other medicines.

FAQ

Is green tea better than black tea for focus?

Both contain caffeine and L-theanine, and amounts vary by brand and brewing time. Pick the one you enjoy and can drink in moderate amounts.

Is matcha stronger than normal tea?

Matcha is made from whole powdered tea leaves, so a serving can contain more caffeine than a normal cup of tea. Check the label and count it like a coffee.

Can I drink tea all day instead of coffee?

You can, as long as your total caffeine stays within 400 mg and you stop well before bed. Several mugs of strong tea can add up to as much as a couple of coffees.

Does adding milk change the caffeine?

No. Milk changes the taste and calories, not the caffeine content. A large milky coffee can still contain a lot of caffeine.

Should I see a doctor if caffeine makes me anxious?

If even small amounts cause a racing heart, panic or chest pain, stop and speak to a doctor. Some people are very sensitive to caffeine.
