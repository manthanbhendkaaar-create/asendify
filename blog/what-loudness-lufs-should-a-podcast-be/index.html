What loudness (LUFS) should a podcast be for Apple, Spotify and YouTube?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Aim for about −16 LUFS integrated loudness, with true peaks no higher than −1 dBFS. That's Apple Podcasts' published recommendation (with a ±1 dB tolerance), and most podcasters use it everywhere. Spotify and YouTube ask for −14 LUFS, so a −16 file may sit slightly quieter there, but it won't distort. What matters more for keeping listeners is consistency: host, guest, ads and music all at similar levels, so nobody reaches for the volume or leaves.

Key takeaways

  • Apple Podcasts: overall loudness "around -16 dB LKFS, with a +/- 1 dB tolerance" and true peak not above −1 dBFS, measured per ITU-R BS.1770.
  • Podnews: "Spotify and YouTube ask for -14 LUFS"; most podcasters still produce −16.
  • Mono measures louder: a mono file at about −19 LUFS sounds as loud as stereo at −16 (Podnews; Google's guidance cited by WBUR says the same).
  • Broadcast masters at −23 or −24 LUFS are too quiet for podcast apps.
  • Set loudness before encoding to MP3 or AAC, and check the final file.

Why loudness affects retention

WBUR's engineers point out that platforms play episodes, ads and promos from many producers back to back; if levels don't match, listeners keep adjusting the volume. Inside your own episode the same thing happens on a smaller scale: a quiet guest, a loud laugh, a booming music sting. Each one is a small reason to turn the episode off, especially on a commute or in a car where adjusting is awkward. On YouTube, where many people watch podcasts with phone speakers or TV audio, an uneven mix is just as noticeable.

A simple workflow

  1. Level each voice first. Get host and guest to similar speech levels before any master processing. A loudness target on the final mix won't fix a guest who's 6 dB quieter.
  2. Gentle compression on voices so loud and soft words sit closer together.
  3. Match music and ad reads to the voices, not above them.
  4. Master to −16 LUFS integrated with a true-peak limiter at −1 dBFS.
  5. Measure the final file, not just the project, after export. Apple recommends setting loudness before encoding.
  6. For mono files, remember they measure louder; many tools handle this, but check what yours assumes.

For video podcasts, apply the same master to the YouTube upload's audio. See how to edit a video podcast so it doesn't feel static and why viewers drop off a video podcast on YouTube.

Worked example

Example: a two-host show exports at −21 LUFS because one host records on a quiet USB mic. Listeners complain they have to turn it up, then get blasted by the intro music. The editor raises the quieter host by 5 dB, compresses both voices, drops the music bed 4 dB, then masters the mix to −16 LUFS with a −1 dBFS true-peak limit. The exported MP3 measures −16.3 LUFS, within Apple's tolerance. Figures are illustrative.

FAQ

Is there a book on retention editing for video podcasts?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook by Asendify covers pacing and hooks for £1,720 one-time. Apple's and Spotify's creator help pages are free.

Should I master my podcast to −14 or −16 LUFS?

−16 meets Apple's recommendation and is what most podcasters use. −14 matches Spotify and YouTube; either is reasonable if consistent.

What true peak should a podcast have?

No higher than −1 dBFS, per Apple Podcasts.

Why does my mono podcast sound louder?

Mono measures louder; about −19 LUFS mono matches −16 stereo.

Will Spotify or YouTube change my podcast's volume?

Podnews says they target −14 LUFS, so a −16 file may play slightly quieter there.

Is LUFS the same as LKFS?

Yes, effectively. Apple uses LKFS; both measure loudness under ITU-R BS.1770.
