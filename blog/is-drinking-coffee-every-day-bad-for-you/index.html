Is drinking coffee every day bad for you?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 7 November 2026 · Last updated 7 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

For most healthy adults, drinking coffee every day is not bad for you. Up to about 400 mg of caffeine a day, roughly three to four standard cups, is the commonly cited safe limit, and large reviews link moderate coffee drinking with neutral or slightly better health outcomes. It becomes a problem when it hurts your sleep, raises anxiety, or you are pregnant or have certain conditions.

Key takeaways

  • Healthy adults: up to 400 mg of caffeine a day and up to 200 mg in one go are the commonly cited safe limits (FDA, EFSA).
  • A large 2017 review in the BMJ found coffee "seems generally safe within usual levels of intake".
  • Pregnancy is the main exception: the NHS advises no more than 200 mg a day.
  • The most common real harm is worse sleep, even from a single 100 mg dose close to bedtime.
  • Work out your own total, including tea, cola and energy drinks, with the free Caffeine Cutoff Calculator.

This page is general education for healthy adults aged 18 and over. It is not medical advice. If you have a health condition, are pregnant or take medication, check with a doctor or pharmacist.

What the research says about daily coffee

The best single summary is an umbrella review published in the BMJ in 2017 (Poole et al.). The authors pulled together 201 meta-analyses of observational studies covering 67 health outcomes, plus 17 meta-analyses of randomised trials.

Their main findings:

  • Drinking about three cups a day was linked with the largest drop in risk of death from any cause, roughly 17% lower than drinking none.
  • Across most outcomes, coffee was more often linked with benefit than with harm.
  • The authors concluded that coffee "seems generally safe within usual levels of intake".

Two cautions matter. First, most of this research is observational. People who drink coffee may differ from people who don't in ways the studies cannot fully remove, so it shows a link, not proof that coffee causes better health. Second, the review did find harmful links in two groups: pregnant women, and women at higher risk of bone fractures.

So the honest summary is: coffee is very unlikely to be harming a healthy adult who drinks a few cups a day, but you should not start drinking it as a health treatment.

To turn milligrams into drinks, the NHS gives these rough figures: a mug of instant coffee about 100 mg, a mug of tea about 75 mg, a can of cola about 40 mg and a 250 ml energy drink about 80 mg. Filter coffee and coffee-shop drinks can contain more. The FDA notes that even decaf has 2 to 15 mg in an 8 oz cup.

When daily coffee can be bad for you

Daily coffee is a problem when one of these applies:

SituationWhy it mattersWhat to do
You sleep badlyCaffeine lingers for hours; EFSA says 100 mg near bedtime may affect sleepMove your last cup earlier
You feel anxious, jittery or have palpitationsThe FDA lists these as signs of too much caffeineCut your largest single serving first
You are pregnant or trying to beHigher intakes are linked with pregnancy risksStay under 200 mg a day and ask your midwife
You have heart problems, high blood pressure or take certain medicinesCaffeine can interactAsk your GP or pharmacist
You use caffeine powders or many energy drinksEasy to take a huge dose fast; the FDA warns toxic effects such as seizures can occur around 1,200 mg taken quicklyAvoid powders completely

The FDA's list of signs that you have had too much includes a fast heart rate, palpitations, trouble sleeping, anxiety, jitters, an upset stomach, nausea and headache. If you notice these, it is your body's answer to "is coffee bad for me", whatever the averages say. The Mayo Clinic makes the same point: if you get headaches or feel jittery or anxious after caffeine, rethink how much is right for you.

Her total is under 400 mg and no single drink is over 200 mg, so by the published limits she is within the safe range. But she says she lies awake until midnight. The 16:30 cola and 14:00 tea are the most likely culprits for sleep, so the first change is to make her last caffeine of the day before about 14:00 and swap the cola for a caffeine-free drink. She keeps her morning coffee, which is the one she enjoys most. Two weeks later she can judge whether her sleep has improved.

A simple daily coffee check

  1. Add up a normal day, including tea, cola, energy drinks and pre-workout. Coffee is often only part of the total.
  2. Check the two lines: 400 mg across the day and 200 mg in one go (200 mg a day if pregnant).
  3. Check your sleep. If it is poor, move your last caffeine earlier before cutting the total.
  4. Check how you feel. Jitters, palpitations or anxiety mean your personal limit is lower than the average.
  5. Change one thing at a time for two weeks, so you know what helped.

FAQ

How many cups of coffee a day is too many?

For most healthy adults, more than about four standard 8 oz cups (over 400 mg of caffeine) is above the commonly cited limit. Large café drinks can count as two cups or more.

Is it bad to drink coffee on an empty stomach?

There is no strong evidence that it is harmful for most people. Some people get an upset stomach or jitters, in which case having it with food is a simple fix.

Does daily coffee make you dependent?

Regular caffeine users can get withdrawal symptoms, such as headaches and tiredness, if they stop suddenly. Cutting down gradually over a week or two usually avoids this.

Is decaf healthier than regular coffee?

Decaf removes most of the caffeine (2 to 15 mg per 8 oz remains), so it avoids the sleep and jitter problems. Whether it shares coffee's other links with health is less studied.

Is coffee bad for your heart?

For healthy adults, moderate intake has not been shown to harm the heart, but too much caffeine can cause palpitations and a faster heart rate. If you have a heart condition, ask your doctor.

Should I stop drinking coffee if I'm pregnant?

You don't have to stop, but the NHS advises no more than 200 mg of caffeine a day from all sources. Talk to your midwife or GP if you are unsure.
