How do I avoid the afternoon caffeine crash?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 28 October 2026 · Last updated 28 October 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Keep each dose moderate, have your coffee earlier rather than as one big mid-morning hit, eat a proper lunch, and stop caffeine early enough that it doesn't cut into your sleep. Most "crashes" are really short sleep, a large dose wearing off or the normal post-lunch dip, so fixing those works better than adding another coffee at 3pm.

What the afternoon crash actually is

There is no single "caffeine crash" switch. When people describe a slump after lunch, it is usually a mix of these:

  • Sleep debt coming back. Caffeine blocks adenosine, the chemical that builds up while you are awake and makes you feel sleepy. It hides tiredness for a few hours; it does not remove it. When the effect fades, the tiredness you were carrying is still there.
  • A big dose wearing off. A large coffee-shop drink can be well over 200 mg in one go. A big lift is often followed by a more noticeable drop.
  • The normal early-afternoon dip. Many people feel less alert in the early afternoon whatever they drink, and a heavy lunch can make it feel worse.
  • Late caffeine wrecking last night's sleep. If yesterday's 4pm coffee shortened your sleep, today's afternoon feels worse, and you reach for more coffee. That loop is the real crash for many people.

Step 1: Check your total and your biggest single dose

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) says that for healthy adults, daily intakes up to 400 mg from all sources and single doses up to 200 mg do not raise safety concerns. Treat those as ceilings, not targets.

Rough amounts help you count. The NHS gives about 100 mg for a mug of instant coffee, about 75 mg for a mug of tea, about 40 mg for a can of cola and about 80 mg for a 250 ml energy drink. Filter and coffee-shop coffee can contain much more.

  1. Write down every caffeinated drink, tablet and pre-workout for two ordinary days, with the time.
  2. Add up the total and find your largest single serving.
  3. If any one serving is above 200 mg, that is the first thing to change.

Step 2: Split one big coffee into smaller ones

Instead of one large drink at 10am, try two smaller ones: for example a regular mug when you start your hardest work and a smaller cup or a tea around late morning. Smaller doses give a steadier lift and a gentler fade. Keep the day's total the same or lower; the aim is to spread it, not to add to it.

Step 3: Set a cut-off time

A 2023 meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews (Gardiner and colleagues) pooled controlled studies and found caffeine reduced total sleep and deep sleep on average. The authors suggested that a typical cup of coffee should be finished at least about 9 hours before bed, and larger doses such as pre-workouts earlier still.

So if you go to bed at 11pm, a sensible cut-off for a normal coffee is around 2pm. If you need something after that, use decaf, herbal tea or water. Better sleep is the most reliable way to have fewer slumps the next afternoon.

Step 4: Fix lunch and the hour after it

  • Eat a balanced lunch with protein, vegetables and some fibre rather than a very large or very sugary meal.
  • Go outside for 10 to 15 minutes after eating if you can. Daylight and a short walk are simple ways to feel more awake.
  • Drink water. Being mildly dehydrated can make you feel flat, and it is easy to confuse thirst with tiredness.
  • Plan easier tasks for the first hour after lunch, and save your hardest work for the morning when caffeine and alertness are both on your side.

Step 5: Use caffeine-free options when the dip hits

When the slump arrives after your cut-off, try one of these before reaching for caffeine:

  • A brisk 10-minute walk, ideally outside.
  • A short nap of 10 to 20 minutes, if your work allows it and it doesn't stop you sleeping at night.
  • A change of task or environment, such as moving to a brighter room or standing for a call.
  • A glass of cold water and a small snack if lunch was a while ago.

Who should avoid caffeine or ask first

Some people should avoid or limit caffeine and check with a GP or pharmacist first: anyone pregnant or breastfeeding (the NHS advises no more than 200 mg a day in pregnancy), children and teenagers under 18, people with heart conditions or high blood pressure, people with anxiety or panic disorders, people with sleep disorders, and anyone taking medicines that may interact with caffeine.

Never use caffeine powders or highly concentrated liquid caffeine, and never mix caffeine with alcohol.

A worked example

Priya, 29, has a large coffee-shop latte at 10am and another coffee at 3pm, and feels drained by 4pm most days. Using the steps above she:

  • swaps the large latte for a regular mug at 9am and a tea at 11:30am;
  • stops caffeine at 2pm and takes a 10-minute walk after lunch;
  • moves her hardest task to the morning.

This shows how the method works; it is not a promised result, and people respond differently.

Key takeaways

  • The "crash" is usually sleep debt, a big dose fading or the normal post-lunch dip.
  • Keep single doses at or below 200 mg and your daily total at or below 400 mg.
  • Split one large coffee into smaller ones rather than adding more.
  • Stop caffeine about 9 hours before bed for a typical cup.
  • Use daylight, a walk, water and a lighter lunch for the afternoon dip.

FAQ

Is the afternoon crash a sign I'm drinking too much caffeine?

Not always, but it can be. If your largest serving is over 200 mg, your total is near 400 mg, or you are relying on afternoon coffee to get through the day, cutting back and sleeping more usually helps.

Should I drink another coffee to beat the crash?

It works for a short time, but late caffeine can shorten your sleep and make tomorrow's slump worse. A walk, water or a short nap are better options after your cut-off.

Does sugar make the crash worse?

A very sugary drink or lunch can leave you feeling flat later. Many coffee-shop drinks and energy drinks contain a lot of sugar, so check the label.

Is decaf a good afternoon swap?

Usually, yes. Decaf contains a small amount of caffeine, far less than regular coffee, so most people can drink it in the afternoon without trouble.
