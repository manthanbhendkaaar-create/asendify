How do I explain a retention graph dip to a client who wants a longer video?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 25 November 2026 · Last updated 25 November 2026

Show the client the exact second where viewers leave, say what is on screen at that point, and propose one small test instead of arguing about length. A dip says where people left, not that the video is too short or too long.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video," and "spikes appear when more viewers are watching, rewatching, or sharing those parts" (YouTube Help).
  • Its help page also notes that a spike can mean viewers found a part engaging or that they had to rewatch it, so a graph needs interpretation (YouTube Help).
  • The intro metric shows "what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds" (YouTube Help), a number clients can understand quickly.
  • Google Ads Help lists recommended ad lengths such as ":15-:20" for awareness and action and "2:00-3:00" for consideration (Google Ads Help), which shows that the right length depends on the goal.

Why clients ask for longer

A client who wants a longer video usually has a reason behind it: more messages to include, a feeling that a bigger budget should show up as more minutes, or worry that something important will be left out. The retention graph is useful because it turns an opinion into a picture. It does not say longer is wrong. It shows where the viewer stopped, which you can connect to what was on screen at that moment.

The risk is overselling the graph. A dip can come from a slow section, but also from a mismatched title, a poor audience fit or a natural drop that happens in nearly every video. Be clear about what you know and what you are guessing.

Step-by-step: run the conversation

  1. Start with the goal, not the graph. Ask what the video should do: awareness, a sign-up, a purchase.
  2. Open the retention graph with the client. Point to one dip, not five.
  3. Name what is on screen at the dip. A logo sequence, a long explanation or a change of topic are common causes.
  4. Say what the graph cannot prove. The cause is a hypothesis until a test shows otherwise.
  5. Show the intro number. Compare it to the start of the graph so they see how much of the audience the opening kept.
  6. Offer an option, not a refusal. If they want the extra content, propose where it can go, such as a second video, a chapter or the description.
  7. Agree one test. For example, move the main point earlier or cut the dip section and compare the next upload.
  8. Set the measure before the test. Decide in advance which number will count, such as the share still watching at a given point.
  9. Write it down. A short note of what was agreed and what was found avoids repeating the argument.
  10. Report back with the same graph. Show the before and after on the same view.

Example: a 4-minute brand film

Example: an agency edits a four-minute brand film. The client wants to add a customer story near the end and make it six minutes. The editor shows the retention graph: a visible dip at the 50-second mark where the film moves from the product to the company history. The editor proposes moving the customer story to the middle and trimming the company history, and offers a six-minute cut for the website, where viewers have chosen to watch. They agree to compare the next upload with the same measure. This scenario uses invented details and is not a measured result.

When the client is right

Sometimes longer is correct, for instance a detailed demonstration for people who are already interested, or content people choose to watch. Google Ads Help shows that "2:00-3:00" is recommended for consideration goals. If the viewers are warm and the graph holds up through the long parts, say so and agree.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a dip as proof of a cause.
  • Showing the client five problems at once.
  • Arguing about length in the abstract rather than looking at a section.
  • Promising a result from the change instead of proposing a test.

What does a dip in the retention graph mean?

Does a dip mean the video is too long?

Not by itself. It shows where people left; the cause needs checking.

How do I show the client the opening worked?

Use the intro metric, the share still watching after the first 30 seconds.
