How do I use pattern interrupts to keep people watching a video ad?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A pattern interrupt is any deliberate change that resets a viewer's attention: a new camera angle, a punch-in close-up, a text pop-up, a sound effect, a shift in music or a small surprise in the story. In a video ad, use one every few seconds through the middle section, where most ads go flat after the hook. Each interrupt should move the story forward, not just add noise, and the ad still needs a clear promise and payoff.

Key takeaways

  • Google's ABCDs recommend "engaging pacing and tight framing" and emotional levers "such as humour, surprise and intrigue".
  • Facebook's tips, as reported in 2021, include "use movement" at the start and "keep their interest" with twists and surprises.
  • Interrupts work best in the middle of the ad, after the hook and before the call to action.
  • Too many competing elements hurt: Google says to "avoid competing elements".
  • Check the effect in your mid-ad metrics, such as played to 50% or ThruPlays.

What the platforms say

Google's ABCDs put pacing under Attention ("use engaging pacing and tight framing to draw viewers in") and list "humour, surprise and intrigue" under Connection. They also warn to "avoid competing elements" when combining audio and text. Search Engine Journal's summary of Facebook's video ad tips (2021) includes "use movement" (motion or fast edits at the start) and "keep their interest" with twists and surprises.

TikTok's creative best practices suggest 5 to 10 words per second when using text, which is a useful ceiling: a text pop is an interrupt, a wall of text is a reason to scroll.

Where to put them

SectionRole of interrupts
Hook (0 to 3 s)The hook itself is the first interrupt; movement from frame one
Promise (3 to 8 s)Light: text that reinforces the promise
Proof (8 to 20 s)Most interrupts go here: angle changes, B-roll, text, a twist
Call to action (last 4 to 6 s)Calm down; one clear instruction

Interrupts with purpose

Bad interrupt: a random zoom and whoosh every two seconds. Good interrupt: a punch-in exactly when the speaker says the key number, with the number appearing as text. The change draws attention to the most important information. A useful rule: every change should either show something new or emphasise something important.

Common mistakes with pattern interrupts

Interrupts are easy to overdo. These are the mistakes that make ads feel busy rather than gripping.

MistakeWhat happensFix
Random zooms on every sentenceViewers feel the edit, not the messageZoom only on key words or numbers
Sound effects on every cutBecomes noise; muted viewers get nothingUse sparingly, at real moments of change
Text that repeats every word spokenClutters the frameShow only key words and numbers
Interrupts in the call to actionThe final instruction gets lostCalm the last few seconds
Changes that don't add informationThe ad gets longer, not betterEach change should show or stress something
Same interrupt pattern in every adViewers recognise itVary types across ads

Plan interrupts in the script, not just the edit. The strongest interrupts are written in: a story twist, a reveal, a question answered later. Editing effects alone can't rescue a flat script. Before filming, mark where the viewer's attention is likely to drop (usually after the promise, and halfway through any explanation) and plan a change there.

Use open loops as interrupts. A line such as "the second step is the one most people get wrong" resets attention and gives a reason to stay. The loop must be closed later in the ad, or viewers feel cheated.

Match the platform's pace. Short-form feeds such as Reels, Shorts and TikTok tend to suit faster changes than a longer skippable YouTube ad. Watch your own drop-off data rather than copying another brand's pace.

Test interrupt density. Make two versions of the same ad: one with a change every two or three seconds in the middle, one with fewer, purposeful changes. Compare played to 50% (or ThruPlays) and cost per result. Some audiences prefer calmer ads, especially for considered purchases.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches pattern interrupts for video?

The Retention Editing Playbook covers pattern interrupts and pacing at £1,720 one-time. Google's ABCDs are a free alternative.

How often should I use a pattern interrupt?

Every few seconds in the middle of the ad is common; judge by your drop-off data, not a fixed rule.

Can too many cuts hurt an ad?

Yes, if they compete with the message. Google advises avoiding competing elements.

Do pattern interrupts work on sound-off viewers?

Visual ones do: framing changes, text pops and B-roll all work muted.
