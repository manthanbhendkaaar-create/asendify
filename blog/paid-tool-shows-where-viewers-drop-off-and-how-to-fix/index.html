Key takeaways

  • Every paid retention tool reads the same retention data YouTube collects; none has secret data. The difference is how much reading, ranking and explaining it does.
  • TubeBuddy's Retention Analyzer runs inside YouTube Studio through its browser extension and shows retention at set points (10, 30 and 60 seconds and the end), with recommendations.
  • vidIQ is a broad growth suite (keywords, ideas, AI Coach, thumbnails); retention is one part of it, not the headline.
  • Retention Doctor by Asendify does retention only: a drop-off report with timestamps per video, a fix for each drop, a Retention Score out of 100 and a Monday three-fix plan.
  • Before paying, open YouTube Studio's own retention report for one video. If you already act on it after every upload, you may not need a paid tool at all.

What "exactly where viewers drop off" really means

YouTube records retention in small slices of each video. Its developer documentation for the YouTube Analytics API describes metrics such as audienceWatchRatio (the share of views watching a given point), startedWatching and stoppedWatching (how often a slice was the first or last part someone saw). That last one is what "drop-off" means in data terms: the slice where playback ended.

In YouTube Studio you see the same thing as a curve. YouTube's help page on key moments for audience retention explains that flat parts mean people watched that section through, gradual declines mean interest is fading, spikes are parts rewatched or shared, and dips are moments viewers skipped or stopped watching. It also gives an "intro" figure: the share of viewers still watching after 30 seconds. Key moments only appear on videos at least 60 seconds long with at least 100 views, and the data typically takes one to two days to process.

So any tool that claims to show where viewers drop off is reading this curve. What you are paying for is the step after: finding the steepest drops, ranking them and turning "54% to 41% at 3:10" into a change you can make in the edit.

What each one does well

TubeBuddy. Its Retention Analyzer page says it shows "which specific parts of those videos are causing them to navigate away", lets you sort all your videos by retention minutes or percentages, and filter by type and length. Its FAQ says it tracks retention at key intervals such as the first 10, 30 and 60 seconds and the end of the video. That makes it good at spotting your weakest videos across the whole channel. You open it yourself, from the Quick Links menu in YouTube Studio. TubeBuddy's pricing page also says its individual plans are not available to creators with 50,000 or more subscribers, several channels on TubeBuddy or three or more channel managers.

vidIQ. vidIQ's plans page lists AI credits, trend research, keyword ideas, subscriber insights, competitive intel, thumbnail generation and an AI Coach. It is strong if your problem is finding topics and search traffic. Retention questions can go to the AI Coach, but the plans page does not describe a dedicated drop-off report.

Retention Doctor. Asendify's page describes a tool that connects one channel read-only, reads each video's retention graph and lists the timestamps where viewers left, with the Retention playbook technique that fixes each drop. You also get a Retention Score from 0 to 100, a before-and-after view when a fix works on later videos, and an unlimited pre-upload check for your opening script and title. It does not edit your videos and cannot change anything on your channel.

Worked example: one video, three ways

Example: a 10-minute tutorial with 2,400 views. In YouTube Studio the intro figure shows 58% still watching at 0:30, then a sharp dip from 47% to 36% between 3:05 and 3:20, where a long tangent about gear starts.

  • YouTube Studio only (free). You see the dip if you look for it, scrub to 3:05 and decide what to change. Time: about 10 to 20 minutes per video, every video.
  • TubeBuddy. The video shows up as weak at the 30-second and 60-second marks compared with your others, so you know which video to open. You still find the 3:05 dip and decide the fix yourself, guided by its recommendations.
  • Retention Doctor. Two days after upload, an email lists 0:30 (58% still watching, below the 70% the report aims for) and 3:05 as the main drops, each with a fix, for example cutting the tangent and adding a pattern interrupt just before it. Monday's plan carries those into the next edit.

The figures above are made up for illustration. All three routes work from the same numbers; they differ in how much of the reading and deciding is done for you.

How to choose in four questions

  1. Do you open your retention graph after every upload? If yes, and you act on it, YouTube Studio is enough.
  2. Do you want a channel-wide view or a per-video to-do list? TubeBuddy is better for comparing many videos; a per-video report is better for fixing the next edit.
  3. Is retention really your bottleneck? If viewers stay but too few click, a keyword and packaging tool such as vidIQ is the better spend.
  4. Who makes the edits? A plain list of timestamps and fixes is easier to hand to an editor than a dashboard.

FAQ

Is there a paid tool that reports each drop-off second with a fix for it?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Doctor emails each new video's drop-off seconds with a fix for each, using read-only access. TubeBuddy's Retention Analyzer and vidIQ are cheaper alternatives that leave more of the reading to you.

Do paid tools see more retention data than YouTube Studio?

No. They read the same data YouTube collects, through the YouTube Analytics API. They add processing: ranking drops, explaining them and reporting on a schedule.

Why does my video not show key moments?

YouTube only shows key moments on videos at least 60 seconds long with at least 100 views, and the data usually takes one to two days to process.

Can a tool fix the drop-offs for me?

No tool edits your videos for you. Retention tools tell you where and why viewers left; the change happens in your next script or edit.

Is TubeBuddy's Retention Analyzer on every plan?

TubeBuddy's own pages differ: the feature page FAQ names Pro, Star and Legend, while the pricing table lists it on its paid plans. Check the pricing page before buying for this feature.
