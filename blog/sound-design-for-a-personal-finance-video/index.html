What sound design helps a personal finance video without sounding cheesy?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Use quiet, purposeful sounds: a soft tick when a key number appears, a short whoosh on a section change, clean room tone under the voice. Skip cash-register dings, coin clinks and rising "money" stingers, which sound cheesy and can feel like a sales pitch. The voice stays on top, and every sound should point at something the viewer needs to notice. This is education, not financial advice.

Key takeaways

  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded, rather than included", and signalling says cues that "emphasize information that is more important" help (Dartmouth). A sound is a cue; use it for the important thing only.
  • YouTube's retention graph shows whether a change helped: dips "mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help).
  • Sound effects and music need rights too. YouTube warns that claimed music may be "muted or may be entirely unavailable", and says "YouTube's Audio Library has royalty-free music that creators can use" (YouTube Help).
  • The FCA expects communications to be "fair, clear and not misleading" (FCA FG24/1); a hype soundtrack can change how a claim feels.

A simple sound plan

  1. Clean the voice first. Remove noise, even out levels and add a touch of compression. Everything else sits under it.
  2. Add room tone. Where you cut breaths or words, fill with the same quiet background so the cut is not heard.
  3. Pick one tick sound. Use it for numbers only, so viewers learn it means "look here".
  4. Pick one transition sound. Use it for section changes only.
  5. Place sounds slightly ahead of the visual. A tick just before the number appears feels natural.
  6. Leave silence. A short pause after a hard definition is sound design too.
  7. Listen on phone speakers and cheap earbuds. Most viewers do.

Why finance needs restraint

Viewers are deciding whether to trust you with money topics. Over-produced audio can feel like a sales video. Strong stings under claims such as "this saves you thousands" push the claim harder than evidence supports. Keep the sound design supportive and the claims modest.

Example: a budgeting video

Example: a creator makes "How do I build a monthly budget?" In the first edit, a cash-register sound plays every time a number appears and a bass hit marks each tip. In the second edit, one soft tick marks each figure, one whoosh marks each of the four steps, and the room tone is kept clean. The creator compares the retention graph for the first minute and reads the comments for any mention of audio. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Using free sound packs of unknown origin. Check the licence.
  • Putting a sound on every cut. It becomes noise.
  • Letting sounds mask speech. Keep effects well below the voice.
  • Changing the sound set each video. Consistency teaches viewers what to expect.

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, pacing and pattern interrupts with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. It does not include sound effect libraries.

Should I use sound effects at all in finance videos?

A few, used with purpose. One for numbers and one for section changes is plenty.

What is the biggest audio mistake in money videos?

Cliche money sounds and loud music under the voice.

Do I need to worry about sound effect licences?

Yes. Use effects you made, licensed or got from a source whose licence covers YouTube.
