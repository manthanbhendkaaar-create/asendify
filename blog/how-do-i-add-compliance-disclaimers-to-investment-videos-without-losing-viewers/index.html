How do I add compliance disclaimers to investment videos without losing viewers?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Put the warning where the claim is made, in plain words, on screen long enough to read, and keep the rest of the video moving around it. Regulators look at prominence and timing, not just whether a warning exists, so do not hide it in a caption or leave it for the end. Then edit the disclaimer into the story so it reads as part of the video, not as a stop sign. Your compliance team or adviser has the final word.

Key takeaways

  • The UK FCA's social media guidance describes a compliant short-form video as one where "the risk warning is clearly and prominently displayed across the screen throughout the financial promotion", and a non-compliant one as where the warning "is found within the caption of the video" (FCA FG24/1).
  • For long-form video the same guidance says the warning should be "displayed clearly and prominently on the screen for the section of the video involving the communication of the financial promotion", not "at the end of the video rather than when the financial promotion is being communicated" (FCA FG24/1).
  • The FCA tells content creators that promotions must not "emphasise benefits while hiding relevant downsides and risks" and that "consumers are not misled" (FCA).
  • US regulator FINRA has encouraged firms to find ways to make disclosures "more compelling, particularly in electronic media", including "animations, short videos" and the option of a video, audio recording or transcript of the same information (FINRA Regulatory Notice 19-31).
  • YouTube says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). A badly placed disclaimer can create one; a well-placed one need not.

The problem: the disclaimer and the viewer pull in opposite directions

Compliance wants the warning to be seen. The editor wants the viewer to keep watching. The usual compromise fails both: a block of small text over a slide for three seconds, read by nobody and noticed as a pause. Viewers swipe or skip, and a regulator may still ask whether the warning was prominent.

The better approach is to treat the disclaimer as content. It has a place in the structure, a spoken line, a clear design and a reason to be there.

Step-by-step: build the disclaimer into the edit

  1. Get the exact wording from compliance. Do not shorten or reword the required warning yourself.
  2. Find every claim that needs it. A performance figure, a return, a recommendation or an offer; mark them on the timeline.
  3. Place the warning with the claim. On screen at the moment the claim is spoken, not later and not only in the description.
  4. Make it readable on a phone. Large enough, high contrast, on a plain panel or bar, in the safe area so the interface does not cover it.
  5. Give it enough time. Read it aloud at a normal pace; the on-screen time should be at least that long.
  6. Say it as well. A short spoken line ("Remember, your capital is at risk") is natural in the script and helps listening viewers.
  7. Do not compete with it. Do not show a flashing graphic, fast cut or loud sound effect at the same moment.
  8. Keep the claim honest. Show downsides at the same weight as benefits where the rules require; do not make the warning the only fair part of a one-sided video.
  9. Use a consistent design. The same bar, font and position every time, so viewers learn to expect it and it feels part of the format.
  10. Hold the pace around it. Cut the previous and next beats tightly, so the warning is a short pause in a video that moves.
  11. Add captions for the spoken warning.
  12. Re-check for each cutdown. A 30-second clip made from a longer video needs its own warning in the right place.
  13. Get a compliance sign-off on the final export, not the script.
  14. Check the retention graph at the warning. A sharp dip suggests the design or timing needs work, never that the warning should be removed.

Keeping attention without weakening the warning

  • Open with the story, not the warning. Hook the viewer with the topic, then place the warning when the claim is made.
  • Vary the visual. Switch from a talking head to a chart or an animation, and show the warning on the new visual.
  • Make the warning the moment before the point. "Before we look at the numbers, one warning." Then the point.
  • Keep sentences short. Short claims need short warnings.
  • Use chapters on long videos, with a clear section for risks and costs, so viewers can find them.
  • Do not use tricks. Never play the warning at an unreadable speed or on a low-contrast background. It would not help viewers or compliance.

Example: a 6-minute video on a stocks and shares ISA

Example: a UK finance creator makes a 6-minute video on how an ISA works. Version one has a small grey line of text in the description and a 3-second slide at the end. Version two shows a clean bar at the bottom of the screen for the sections where returns are discussed, with the spoken line "Investments can fall as well as rise, and you may get back less than you put in." It cuts the chart section by 20 seconds, adds chapters including "Risks and costs", and gets sign-off from the compliance reviewer on the final export. The creator compares the retention graph at the warning in both versions. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result and not advice on what any rule requires.

What to do if you are unsure

If you do not know which rules apply to you, ask a compliance professional or the relevant regulator before publishing. Rules depend on country, on whether you are an authorised firm, and on the product. This page only covers the editing side: where to put the warning and how to keep the video moving.

Can I put the disclaimer in the description?

Not on its own. The FCA's examples treat a warning only in the caption as non-compliant for short-form video, so show it on screen.

Where should the warning go in a long video?

On screen during the section where the promotion is made, not only at the end.

Should I also say the warning aloud?

A short spoken line is natural and helps listeners, if your compliance team agrees.

Will a disclaimer make people leave?

It can if it is badly placed or hard to read. Clean design and tight pacing around it limit the effect.
