How many trades do you need to know if a strategy works?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 19 November 2026 · Last updated 19 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Usually more than most traders think: often a few hundred. With 50 trades, a measured 55% win rate has a 95% confidence range of roughly 41% to 69%, which includes losing strategies. With 385 trades you can pin a win rate down to within about 5 percentage points either way. The exact number depends on how close your edge is to break-even: the thinner the edge, the more trades you need.

This page is education only, not financial advice. Trading is risky and past results do not guarantee future ones.

Key takeaways

  • A win rate from a small sample is an estimate with a wide margin of error, not a fact about your strategy.
  • The standard formula for the margin of error of a proportion is z × √(p(1−p)/n), as set out by NIST and Penn State's statistics course.
  • At 95% confidence, roughly 97 trades narrow a win rate to ±10 points and 385 trades to ±5 points.
  • What matters is whether the whole range clears your break-even win rate, which depends on your reward-to-risk ratio.
  • Trades only count if they follow the same rules; mixing setups, sizes or markets resets the sample.

The statistics in plain words

Each trade is a win or a loss, so your win rate is a proportion, the same kind of number as a poll result. The NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods gives the most common confidence interval for a proportion:

observed win rate ± z × √(p × (1 − p) / n)

where p is your observed win rate, n is the number of trades and z is 1.96 for 95% confidence. NIST notes that for small samples this simple version may not be accurate enough and recommends the Wilson method, which never gives impossible values below 0%. Penn State's STAT 500 notes on inference for a proportion add that the simple version is suitable when n × p and n × (1 − p) are both above 5.

How the range shrinks as you trade

Using the formula for a strategy showing a 55% win rate:

Trades95% range for the true win rateCould it be a losing 1:1 strategy (below 50%)?
20about 33% to 77%Yes
50about 41% to 69%Yes
100about 45% to 65%Yes
200about 48% to 62%Yes
500about 51% to 59%No

At 1:1 reward-to-risk, a 55% win rate is a real edge. But until roughly 500 trades, the data cannot rule out that it is really a coin flip or worse.

How many trades for a given precision?

Penn State gives the sample-size formula n = (z / E)² × p(1 − p), where E is the margin of error you want. Using the cautious choice p = 0.5, which gives the largest answer:

Margin of error you want (95%)Trades needed
±15 points43
±10 points97
±5 points385
±3 points1,068

Round up, as Penn State advises. NIST's handbook takes a similar approach to sample sizes for proportions: in its example, telling 10% apart from 20% needs about 102 observations.

Why your break-even point matters

You do not need to know your win rate exactly. You need to know it is above break-even. Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + reward-to-risk ratio).

Average win vs average lossBreak-even win rate
1:150%
1.5:140%
2:1about 33%
3:125%

A strategy with a 45% win rate and 2:1 winners clears break-even by 12 points, so a smaller sample can show it. A strategy with a 52% win rate at 1:1 clears break-even by only 2 points, and would need well over a thousand trades to confirm. Average win and average loss are also estimates, so a big edge in the numbers is easier to trust than a thin one.

Rules that keep a sample honest

  • One setup per sample. Combining breakouts and pullbacks gives you one blurred number for two strategies.
  • Same risk per trade, or measure results in R-multiples so size changes do not distort them.
  • Log every trade, including the ones you would rather forget.
  • Do not change the rules mid-sample. Each change starts a new sample.
  • Backtest and forward test separately. Backtests can include hindsight; live or paper trades confirm them.

Example: a hot start

Jess starts a new pullback strategy and wins 18 of her first 30 trades, a 60% win rate, with winners and losers about the same size. She feels ready to double her size.

The formula says otherwise. √(0.6 × 0.4 / 30) is about 0.089; times 1.96 gives about 0.175. Her true win rate is plausibly anywhere from about 42% to 78%. At 1:1, the low end loses money.

She keeps her size the same and logs another 170 trades with identical rules. After 200 trades her win rate is 56%: a range of about 49% to 63%. Still not certain, but now most of the range is profitable, and she has 200 trades of real evidence about drawdowns and losing streaks. She raises her risk gradually from there.

Is 100 trades enough to judge a strategy?

It gives a rough picture: a win rate measured over 100 trades is typically accurate to about ±10 points at 95% confidence. That is enough only if your edge is large.

Do backtested trades count?

They count as evidence, but backtests can include hindsight and unrealistic fills. Confirm with forward-tested or small live trades.

What about losing streaks?

Even a profitable strategy has long losing runs. A larger sample shows you how deep they get, which helps you size positions safely.

Should I measure profit instead of win rate?

Both. Win rate alone ignores the size of wins and losses. Expectancy in R-multiples combines the two and is the better single number.
