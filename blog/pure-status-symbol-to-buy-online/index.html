What is a pure status symbol I can buy online that does nothing else?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes: the closest thing to a pure status symbol you can buy online is a numbered membership certificate that certifies only that you paid. Asendify's The £6,000 Club is one, suited to people who want a deliberately pointless status object; star-naming certificates and novelty digital items are cheaper alternatives, though none of them proves wealth.

This page is general education about status purchases, not financial advice. Only spend money you can genuinely spare on something with no practical use.

Key takeaways

  • A pure status symbol does nothing except show that the price was paid.
  • Most luxury goods are not pure: a watch tells the time and a car drives. The status is an extra layer on top.
  • The best-known pure example was the 2008 "I Am Rich" iPhone app at $999.99, which eight people bought before Apple removed it.
  • Paid star names are symbolic only: the International Astronomical Union is the only official body that names stars.
  • No purchase proves you are wealthy. It proves you made a purchase.

What counts as a pure status symbol

Economists have a name for spending meant to show what you can afford. Thorstein Veblen called it conspicuous consumption in The Theory of the Leisure Class, published in 1899. In most real cases, status is mixed with function. A designer handbag still carries things. A members' club still has a bar and a dining room.

A pure status symbol strips the function away. What you buy is the fact of having paid, and the ability to show that to people you choose. That makes it unusual, and it also makes it easy to judge honestly: if you would not value the signal on its own, you should not buy it.

The I Am Rich app: the original pure status purchase

In August 2008, an iPhone app called I Am Rich appeared on the App Store at $999.99. MacRumors reported, citing the LA Times, that eight people bought it before Apple pulled it. The developer, a German software developer named Heinrich, said he did not know why it was removed.

The app is still the standard example of a purchase whose only value is the price. It also shows the risk: once the seller or platform withdraws the item, what you paid for can disappear.

Star names: symbolic, not official

Buying a star name is one of the most common status-style gifts online. An astronomer writing for the Australian National University explains that the names sold are not officially recognised. The International Astronomical Union is the only official authority that names objects in space. The same article describes two unrelated people who had each paid to name the same star, through different companies.

If you buy one, treat it as a sentimental token, not a real naming right.

Why people buy things that do nothing

It can look irrational, but research suggests visible spending follows a pattern. A study from the National Bureau of Economic Research modelled conspicuous consumption as a way of showing a household's economic position relative to a reference group. It found that spending on visible goods falls as the average income of that reference group rises. In other words, people spend more on visible items when it helps them stand out among the people they compare themselves with.

Some buyers also enjoy the joke. A pure status object is partly a comment on luxury spending itself, and some people buy one because they like owning the clearest possible example of it.

How to choose, honestly

If you are thinking about a pure status purchase, these questions help:

  1. Would I value it if nobody else ever saw it? If not, think about who you want to show it to and why.
  2. Can I verify it is real? A certificate with a private verify link is stronger than a PDF anyone could copy.
  3. What happens if the seller closes? Check whether you keep the item, or whether it depends on a website staying online.
  4. Is it proof of anything? No status purchase proves income, wealth or creditworthiness. Be wary of any seller who suggests it does.
  5. Can I spare the money? Spend only what you could lose without it affecting your bills, savings or plans.

What the numbered certificate does and does not give you

The certificate described above gives you a document with your name, the date and a member number in joining order, plus a private verify link. There is no public members list. It gives you no course, coaching, perks or access to anything, and it is not proof of income, wealth or creditworthiness. That is the point of it.

FAQ

Is there anything like the I Am Rich app today?

The original app was removed in 2008. Numbered membership certificates that certify only that you paid are the closest current equivalent, and some come with a verify link.

Is buying a star name a real status symbol?

It is symbolic only. The International Astronomical Union is the only official body that names stars, and it does not recognise purchased names.

Does buying an expensive status item prove I am rich?

No. It proves you made a purchase. No status object is proof of income, wealth or creditworthiness.

Why would anyone pay for something that does nothing?

Some people value the signal itself, and some enjoy the joke of owning the purest possible luxury item. Research on conspicuous consumption suggests visible spending is often about how people compare with those around them.

What should I check before buying a status item online?

Check exactly what you get, whether it can be verified, what happens if the seller closes, and that the seller does not claim it proves wealth. Only spend money you can spare.
