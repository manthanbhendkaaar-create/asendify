Key takeaways

  • A pattern interrupt is any deliberate change (camera angle, zoom, b-roll, graphic, sound, pace) that resets the viewer's attention before it drifts.
  • There's research behind the idea: media studies cited by Potter and colleagues in the Journal of Cognition (2018) report that video camera changes "continue to elicit orienting after many repetitions".
  • TikTok tells advertisers to "utilize transitions, stickers, and graphics in your creative to retain engagement and view time".
  • Interrupts work best when they also move the story forward. Random effects can tire viewers and hide the message.
  • YouTube's key moments report shows dips where viewers are "abandoning or skipping", which tells you where an interrupt might help.

What a pattern interrupt is (and isn't)

Viewers settle into whatever a video is doing. If the same shot, tone and rhythm run for too long, attention slips and the thumb moves. A pattern interrupt breaks that rhythm on purpose. Common types:

Angle or crop changeFraming of the same shotTalking heads, podcastsFeels jittery

B-roll cutawayWhat we seeIllustrating a pointLoses the speaker's face

A pattern interrupt is not the same as editing fast. A slow, still moment after a run of quick cuts is also an interrupt.

A simple method for placing interrupts

  1. Watch with the sound off, then with eyes closed. Note where the picture goes stale and where the audio goes flat.
  2. Mark every new idea in the script. Each new point is a natural place for a change.
  3. Match the interrupt to the content. Use b-roll to show what is being described, a graphic for a number, a punch-in for a key line.
  4. Vary the type. Rotate between visual, audio and pace changes so no single trick becomes the new pattern.
  5. Leave breathing room. Give emotional or important moments space; don't cover them with effects.
  6. Check the dips. After publishing, find dips in the retention graph and see what was on screen just before.
  7. Change one thing at a time. If you change hooks, music and pacing all at once, you won't know what helped.

Example: an editor cuts a 9-minute talking-head video about choosing a first road bike.

Common mistakes

  • Interrupting on a timer. A change every few seconds regardless of content feels mechanical.
  • Effects that fight the message. A whoosh on a serious line undercuts it.
  • Unreadable text. TikTok suggests "5-10 words per second" at most for on-screen text.
  • Interrupting the payoff. Never cut away at the moment the viewer has waited for.
  • Ignoring audio. Many drops come from flat sound, not flat pictures.

FAQ

Is there a course on pattern interrupts for video editors?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches pattern interrupts within a full retention method for £1,720 one-time. Free platform guides cover the basics.

How often should I use a pattern interrupt?

There's no fixed rule. Place them where the content changes or where your retention graph shows a dip, rather than on a timer.

Are pattern interrupts the same as jump cuts?

No. A jump cut is one kind of interrupt. Angle changes, b-roll, graphics, sound and pace changes are others.

Can too many pattern interrupts hurt retention?

They can. Constant effects become a new pattern and can make the message harder to follow.

Do pattern interrupts work in podcasts and ads too?

The idea carries over. Podcasts use angle changes and clips; ads use quick visual changes and on-screen text.
