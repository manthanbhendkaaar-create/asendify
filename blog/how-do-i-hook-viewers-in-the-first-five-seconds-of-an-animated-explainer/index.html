How do I hook viewers in the first five seconds of an animated explainer?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Show the problem or the finished result in the first frame, say in one plain line what the viewer will get, and start moving at once. In an animated explainer there is no presenter's face to hold attention, so the picture, the first sentence and the first change on screen do all the work. Cut the logo sting, the long intro and any line that delays the point.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's retention report says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). The first seconds are where the first and often biggest dip appears.
  • Wistia says the 1 to 5 minute range is "ideal for explainer videos, product overviews, or quick feature walkthroughs", and that "videos under a minute average a 52% engagement rate" (Wistia). Explainers are short, so a slow opening costs a large share of the runtime.
  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded", and the modality principle says people "learn better when there is voiceover with images or animation" (Dartmouth). Animation with narration is a good fit; clutter is not.
  • The W3C says captions include "meaningful sound effects" as well as dialogue (W3C). Captions help sound-off viewers follow the first line.

Why the first five seconds matter more in animation

A live video has a person on camera: a face, a voice, eye contact. An animated explainer starts with shapes, colours and a voiceover. A viewer who does not recognise the subject in the first moment has little to hold on to, and nothing on screen tells them why this matters to them. The retention graph usually shows it as a steep drop at the start, before the animation has had time to show its strength.

The fix is not louder music or a flashier title. It is to make the point visible and audible at once.

Avoid opening with a logo animation, a company history or "Welcome to our video". They are the least useful frames you have.

Step-by-step: build a five-second opening

  1. Write the one-sentence promise. Finish "After this video, the viewer will..." in plain words.
  2. Choose the most visual moment. Find the frame that shows the problem or the result most clearly. That frame becomes frame one.
  3. Start the voiceover at the first second. No silence, no breath-in, no music-only intro.
  4. Keep the first line under 12 words where you can. Short lines are easier to follow and to caption.
  5. Add on-screen text that repeats the key words of the line. A few words, large, in the same second.
  6. Move something at once. A character enters, a line draws, an object changes. Give the eye a reason to stay.
  7. Cut the logo to the end card. Brand the video in the last seconds, not the first.
  8. Delay the backstory. Give the problem first; the history can come after the viewer is in.
  9. Check the audio. Voice clear, music low enough that the first words are easy to hear on a phone.
  10. Add captions and check that the first line reads correctly with the sound off.
  11. Watch the first five seconds on a phone, once. Is the subject clear? If you had to explain it, change it.
  12. Compare two versions in an unlisted test if you can, or compare the first 30 seconds of retention between videos.

Keeping the first sentence honest

A hook that promises more than the explainer delivers will lose the viewer a little later and teach them not to trust the next one. If the video explains how a tool works, do not open with a dramatic claim about changing the world. Say what it does, show it, and let the animation do the work. If you use a figure or a quote in the opening, check it and give the source in the description.

Voice, picture and text together

Animation lets you choose what goes on the screen and what goes in the ears. Following the modality and coherence ideas from learning research, keep the voiceover as the main carrier of the explanation, use the picture to show what is being described, and keep on-screen text to a few key words rather than a repeated paragraph. Remove decorative movement that does not point to the subject.

Example: a 90-second explainer for an invoicing tool

Example: a studio makes a 90-second animated explainer for an invoicing app. Version one opens with a five-second logo animation, then "Welcome to Billo, the smart way to manage your business". Version two opens on a pile of paper invoices with one stamped "overdue", and the line "Chasing late invoices takes hours; here is how to stop". The logo moves to the end card, captions run from the first word, and the voiceover starts in the first second. The studio compares the retention graph at 5 and 30 seconds for both versions. What to check after publishing

Look at the retention graph at the 5-second and 30-second marks. A steep early dip means the opening is still not clear; a gentle slope means viewers stayed to see the point. Compare with your other explainers of a similar length rather than with a general figure.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on hooks for explainer videos?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, open loops and pacing with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. It is not animation-specific and does not promise results.

Should an animated explainer start with the logo?

Usually not. Put the problem or result first and move the logo to the end card.

How long should the first line be?

Short: under about 12 words where you can, so it is easy to follow and caption.

Do I need music in the opening?

A soft bed is fine, but the voice must be clear from the first second.

Should I add captions to an explainer?

Yes; they help sound-off and deaf or hard of hearing viewers follow from the first word.
