How fast should the cuts be in explainer videos?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

There is no fixed cut speed for explainer videos. A good rule is that something new should reach the viewer every few seconds (a new visual, label, step or idea), while each idea stays on screen long enough to be understood. Fast cuts suit the hook and simple points; slower holds suit diagrams and key concepts. Your retention graph shows whether you've got the balance right.

Key takeaways

  • Explainers teach, so understanding matters more than speed. Wistia found viewers typically watch "over 50% of an educational video and a tutorial video" in the 1-5 minute range, with many dropping off around halfway.
  • Google recommends "engaging pacing and tight framing" and warns to "avoid competing elements".
  • If text is on screen, TikTok's guidance of "5-10 words per second" is an upper limit, not a target.
  • YouTube spikes can mean viewers "had to rewatch a section". In explainers, that often means a cut came too fast.

Cut speed by section

SectionPaceWhy
Hook (0-10 s)FastShow the problem and the promise quickly
SetupMediumGive just enough context
Core conceptSlower holdsLet diagrams and key terms land
Steps or examplesMedium, one visual per stepStructure through cuts
RecapFastViewers already know it
Call to actionSteadyClear and calm

How to judge cut speed

  1. Count ideas, not cuts. If a shot carries a new idea, it can stay. If nothing new is happening, cut or change the visual.
  2. Read every text label aloud. If it disappears before you finish, hold it longer.
  3. Watch at 1x on a phone. Fast cuts that look fine on a monitor can feel frantic on a small screen.
  4. Check the graph. Dips suggest boredom; spikes in dense sections suggest confusion.

Common mistakes

  • Cutting on a timer. A cut every two seconds regardless of content feels mechanical.
  • Rushing the key diagram. The most important visual needs the longest hold.
  • Text and voice saying different things. Viewers can't follow both.
  • Slow intros. Explainers often waste the first 10 seconds on logos.

Planning cut speed before the edit

Cut speed is easier to get right when it's planned in the script. Before animation or editing starts, mark each line of the script with one of three labels: "fast" (hook, recap, simple transitions), "hold" (a key concept, diagram or number) or "step" (one visual per point). This gives the animator or editor a map, and stops the common problem of a uniform speed that rushes the hard parts and drags the easy ones.

Script line typeLabelTypical treatment
Problem statementFast2-4 quick shots
New term or conceptHoldOne visual, labels added in sequence
Process with stagesStepOne shot per stage
Benefit summaryFastShort montage
Price or offerHoldClear, steady frame

Two more checks help. First, ask someone outside the project to watch once and explain the product back to you. If they can't, the issue is usually a rushed concept, not a slow video. Second, compare versions on the same platform. On YouTube, typical retention lets you compare a new explainer with your last ten videos of similar length, which is fairer than comparing with a viral video in another niche.

Explainers placed on landing pages behave differently from those in feeds. Landing-page viewers have already chosen to watch, so they tolerate slightly longer holds; feed viewers need the faster hook. If one explainer serves both, consider a shorter feed cut that opens on the problem and a fuller landing-page version.

Common questions from clients about pace

Clients sometimes ask for "faster" or "more dynamic" explainers after seeing a competitor's video. Before speeding everything up, ask what they mean: often the issue is a slow opening or a long section on one feature, not the overall rhythm. Show them where the retention graph dips, if the video is already live, or watch the cut together and mark where attention drifts. Speeding up only those sections usually gives the energy they want without rushing the concept the explainer exists to teach.

FAQ

Is there a guide on pacing for explainer videos?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers pacing with templates for £1,720 one-time; Wistia's free length research is a good companion.

How many cuts per minute should an explainer have?

There's no fixed number. Aim for a new visual or idea every few seconds, with longer holds on key concepts.

Are fast cuts bad for explainers?

Not in the hook or recap. They become a problem when they rush the core idea.

Should explainers be under 2 minutes?

Many marketing explainers are, but length should match what needs explaining.

How do I know if my cuts are too fast?

Look for spikes in retention where viewers rewound, and test with someone new to the topic.

Do animated explainers need different pacing from live action?

Animation lets you control every frame, so it's easy to over-animate. Give key diagrams time to settle, just as you would hold a live-action shot.
