Should I charge per minute, per video or a monthly retainer for short-form video editing?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

For short-form, charging per finished minute rarely works: a 30-second Short can take as long as a 10-minute video because the work is in finding the hook and cutting tight, not in the runtime. Most short-form editors price per video (with a set number of revision rounds) for one-off work, and a monthly retainer for a fixed number of Shorts a month. Per minute of raw footage or hourly suits open-ended jobs where the scope is unclear.

Key takeaways

  • Price the work, not the runtime. Short-form time goes into picking the moment, the hook, captions and pacing.
  • Fixed prices need a fixed scope. Upwork's guidance says fixed-price contracts work best when the project is clearly defined from the start, while hourly suits work where "the scope may evolve".
  • Agree payment terms in writing. If you don't, UK law treats a business payment as late 30 days after the customer gets the invoice or the work, whichever is later.
  • Watch the VAT line. UK businesses must register once taxable turnover goes above £90,000 in a rolling 12 months, which changes what you charge business and consumer clients.
  • Retainers give both sides predictability; per-video pricing is easier to start with.

What to put in the price

  1. Number of deliverables. "8 vertical Shorts, 20 to 45 seconds each."
  2. What's included. Hook selection, captions, music from a licensed library, thumbnail frame, platform versions.
  3. Revision rounds. Two rounds per video is common; extra rounds billed.
  4. Turnaround. For example, 3 working days from footage received.
  5. Footage rules. Who supplies it, by when, in what format.
  6. Payment terms. Retainers paid in advance at the start of each month; one-off projects with a deposit.
  7. Unused capacity. Whether unused Shorts roll over, and for how long.

Example: a podcast clipping job

Example: a podcast sends one 60-minute episode a week and wants 5 clips per episode. Per finished minute would pay almost nothing (5 clips of 40 seconds is about 3.5 minutes). Hourly makes the client nervous. The editor offers a package: 20 clips a month from 4 episodes, two revision rounds per clip, captions and a licensed music bed included, 4 working days turnaround, paid monthly in advance. Picking the right moments takes time, so the editor prices on about 1.5 hours per clip, including reviewing the full episode once. When the client later asks for longer YouTube versions of two segments, those are quoted separately per video. Details and figures are illustrative.

Signs you're using the wrong model

  • You're working 3 hours on Shorts you price by the minute.
  • Revision rounds keep growing on fixed per-video jobs. Cap them.
  • Your retainer client sends footage late every month. Add a footage deadline.
  • The client argues about hours. Move to fixed per-video pricing with a clear scope.

Selling on retention, not hours

Clients who run ads or depend on views care about results. Showing how your edits held viewers (stayed to watch, average view duration) makes a higher per-video price easier to justify than listing hours.

FAQ

Is there a book that helps editors show clients retention results?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook includes a tracker and spreadsheet for logging results. YouTube Studio reports are the free alternative.

Is per-minute pricing ever right for short-form?

Per minute of raw footage can work for clipping long recordings. Per finished minute rarely works for Shorts.

How many revisions should a per-video price include?

Two rounds is a common default. Bill extra rounds so scope doesn't creep.

Should retainers be paid in advance?

Usually yes, at the start of each month, with a footage deadline so the editor can plan.

Do I charge VAT on editing?

Only once you are VAT-registered. In the UK you must register when taxable turnover goes over £90,000 in 12 months.
