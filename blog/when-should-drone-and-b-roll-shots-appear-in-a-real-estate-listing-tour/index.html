When should drone and b-roll shots appear in a real estate listing tour?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Use the drone shot briefly at the start to give context, then move inside and let the rooms carry the tour. Use b-roll (details, the street, nearby places) to cover cuts and break up long room shots, and keep each shot short. A viewer deciding whether to book a viewing wants to see the home, not a long aerial sequence.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's retention report says "intro tells you what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds" (YouTube Help). A listing tour that opens with a long aerial loses viewers before the home appears.
  • The same report says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help). Mark where in your tour viewers leave and check what shot is on screen.
  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded, rather than included" (Dartmouth). Cut shots that do not help the buyer judge the home.
  • Wistia says "engagement naturally declines after the five-minute mark" (Wistia). A tour should usually be well under that.

What each shot is for

ShotPurposeBest placementLength guide
Drone exteriorShows size, plot, settingOpening, once3 to 6 seconds
Drone area viewShows nearby transport, parks, shopsNear the end or in the area section3 to 5 seconds
Street b-rollShows the approach and neighbourhood feelAfter the exterior2 to 4 seconds
Detail b-rollKitchen worktop, fittings, view from a windowBetween rooms1 to 3 seconds
CutawayCovers a jump or a long pauseAs needed1 to 2 seconds

These lengths are practical guides, not rules. Test them against your graph.

Step-by-step: place the shots

  1. Decide the viewer's question. A buyer asks "what is it like, and is it worth seeing?" Order the tour to answer that.
  2. Open with one short exterior shot. A drone shot of the whole property with a line such as "Three-bed semi, south-facing garden." Keep it under six seconds.
  3. Move into the first room by cutting on the front door. Do not linger outside.
  4. Walk the tour in the order a visitor would. Hall, living room, kitchen, bedrooms, bathrooms, garden.
  5. Use detail b-roll between rooms. One or two seconds of a feature (a fireplace, a fitted wardrobe) gives variety.
  6. Use cutaways to hide dull moves. A hallway walk or a door opening can be covered with a detail.
  7. Hold the best room a little longer. Where the home is strongest, let the shot breathe.
  8. Place the area drone shot where it adds information. Put it near the end with a line about transport or schools, only if accurate.
  9. Avoid repeating the exterior. Once at the start, perhaps once in a closing frame.
  10. Check drone rules before filming. In the UK, drone flying is governed by the Civil Aviation Authority; check current rules and permissions before you fly.
  11. Compare with the graph. See whether viewers drop at the opening aerial or at the first room.

Common patterns compared

PatternEffectWhen it suits

Accuracy and fairness

A listing video should show the property fairly. Do not use a lens or angle that misleads about size, and make sure nearby places named in the voiceover are real and the distances correct. Aerial views can hide a road or a railway line; do not edit them out if that misleads. This is editing guidance, not legal advice, and local advertising rules for property apply.

Example: a 90-second three-bedroom listing

Example: an agent films a three-bedroom house. First cut: 25 seconds of drone footage with music, then the door. The graph shows many viewers leave before the hall. Second cut: a 5-second drone shot with "Three-bed, south-facing garden", a 2-second street shot, then the front door at second 8. Between rooms the editor adds 1-second details: the kitchen worktop, the stair window. The garden is the strongest feature, so it gets 10 seconds. A 4-second area drone shot near the end shows the park. The agent compares the intro measure and the first 30 seconds with the first cut. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

What to avoid

  • A long aerial intro. It delays the thing viewers came for.
  • Overusing b-roll. Details should serve the tour, not pad it.
  • Misleading angles. Show true size and surroundings.
  • Unlicensed music. Use music you have rights to.
  • Flying without checking the rules. Confirm permissions first.

After publishing

Check where viewers leave. A dip at the start suggests the aerial is too long; a dip mid-tour suggests a slow room or too little variety. YouTube notes spikes show moments that were rewatched, which in a tour often means a feature people want to look at again.

How long should the drone shot be?

Usually 3 to 6 seconds at the start, then into the home.

Where should b-roll go?

Between rooms and over cuts, in 1 to 3 second details.

Should I show the area?

Yes, if location sells the home and the facts are accurate; place it near the end.

Do I need permission to fly a drone?

Check current Civil Aviation Authority rules and permissions before flying in the UK.
