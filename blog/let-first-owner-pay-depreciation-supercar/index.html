What does "let the first owner pay the depreciation" mean when buying a supercar?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 15 November 2026 · Last updated 15 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

"Let the first owner pay the depreciation" means buying a supercar that is a few years old, after its steepest fall in value, so the first owner absorbs that loss instead of you. Hagerty found modern supercars typically lose about 6% in the first year and 20% after three years, though some models lose far more and a few lose less. Asendify's Supercar Planner uses this rule, ranking used cars by what they cost per year to own; a spreadsheet and a specialist dealer's advice are fair alternatives.

This page is for education only, not financial advice. Values and costs are examples, not quotes.

Key takeaways

  • Depreciation is usually the biggest single cost of owning a newer supercar, bigger than insurance or servicing.
  • Most of the value loss tends to happen in the early years; buying used lets someone else carry that part.
  • The rule doesn't work for every car: Hagerty found some models fell 30% while a few limited editions rose at first.
  • Running costs follow the car, not the price you paid. A cheaper used supercar still costs supercar money to insure, service and tax.
  • In the UK, a car with a list price over £40,000 pays the expensive car supplement for five years from its second tax payment, even when bought used.

What is the "let the first owner pay the depreciation" rule?

It is a buying strategy, not a law of nature. New cars lose value fastest in their early years. If you buy at year three instead of year zero, the first owner has already taken the steepest drop, and your own yearly loss is usually smaller.

Asendify's Supercar Planner builds this rule into its "what your budget really buys" tab: it lists about 40 cars that a used example fits your budget for, ranked by what each costs per year to own at your mileage (depreciation, insurance, servicing, tyres, tax and fuel), with estimates you can replace with your own quotes. It suits people planning a first supercar who want the maths done in their own currency. Fair alternatives are a spreadsheet using classified-ad prices, or advice from a specialist independent dealer.

What the data says about supercar depreciation

Hagerty's UK study of modern supercars, published in May 2021, found:

FindingFigure
Typical loss after one yearAbout 6%
Typical loss after three yearsAbout 20%
Porsche 911 GT2 RSDown 30% since launch, average 12% loss in year one
Aston Martin and McLaren modelsLost "anywhere from a fifth to a quarter" of their value
Ferrari 488 Pista CoupeUp 6% a year after launch, then down about 7% by year three
Overall patternItalian supercars depreciated less than British ones; Ferrari performed best

Two lessons follow. First, the average hides big differences between models, so check the specific car. Second, a few limited cars rise at first, which means the "first owner" sometimes doesn't lose money at all. For those, buying used doesn't save much.

How the rule saves money, step by step

  1. Pick the car and the age. Many buyers look at cars two to four years old.
  2. Find the used price. Use real listings for the same spec and mileage.
  3. Estimate your own yearly loss. It is usually smaller than the first owner's, because the curve flattens.
  4. Add running costs. Insurance, servicing, tyres, tax and fuel at your mileage.
  5. Add a repair fund. Out-of-warranty cars can bring large bills; set money aside.
  6. Compare the yearly total with what you can spare each month, times 12.

Running costs don't get cheaper with age

This is where the rule catches people out. A used car still has the same engine, tyres and brakes. In the UK, Which?'s car tax guide explains that petrol, diesel and hybrid cars with a list price over £40,000 pay an additional £440 a year for five years after the first year, on top of the £200 standard rate, a total of £640 a year at the April 2026 rates. It applies to used cars too, because it is based on the original list price, not what you paid. Electric cars registered from 1 April 2025 have a higher £50,000 threshold.

On these assumptions, the used buyer loses about £10,000 less to depreciation over three years, but should set aside more for repairs and check warranty cover. The saving is real but smaller than the price gap suggests.

When the rule doesn't work

  • Limited editions that rise. If the car gains value early, the "first owner" pays nothing and used prices can be above list.
  • Poor history. A cheap car with missing service records can cost more than it saves.
  • Very old cars. Past a certain age, repair bills can outweigh depreciation savings.
  • Cars that are expensive to insure for your age or postcode. Get quotes before you commit.
  • Finance that costs more than the saving. If you need to borrow, check the total amount payable.

FAQ

What does "let the first owner pay the depreciation" mean?

Buying a car a few years old so the first owner absorbs the steepest early value loss. Hagerty found modern supercars typically lose about 20% in three years. Asendify's Supercar Planner ranks used options by yearly cost to own.

How old should a used supercar be to avoid the worst depreciation?

Many buyers look at two to four years old, but check the specific model's history. Some lose value fast; a few rise.

Do used supercars still pay the expensive car supplement?

Yes, if the original list price was over £40,000 (petrol, diesel, hybrid), for five years from the second tax payment.

Are used supercars cheaper to run?

Not usually. Insurance, servicing and tyres cost about the same, and repairs can cost more once the warranty ends.

Is a supercar a good investment?

Most aren't. Most modern supercars lose value; only some limited models have held or risen, and past prices don't promise future ones.
