Should finance creators use a talking head or a screen recording for earnings breakdowns?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Use both. Put your face on screen for the opening, the verdict and the context, and show the filing or chart while you explain a figure, switching back to your face between points. Research on course videos found that mixing an instructor's face with slides held attention better than slides alone, though it was not about finance. This is education, not financial advice.

Key takeaways

  • An edX study found "videos that intersperse an instructor's talking head with slides are more engaging than slides alone", and recommended "post-production editing to display the instructor's head at opportune times" (Guo, Kim and Rubin, 2014). It studied course videos, not earnings content.
  • Mayer's signalling principle says cues that "emphasize information that is more important" help learning (Dartmouth); on a filing page, that means highlighting the figure.
  • YouTube's retention graph shows what works for your audience: dips "mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help).
  • Verdicts about a company can be promotions in some cases; the FCA expects communications to be "fair, clear and not misleading" (FCA FG24/1).

A simple structure for an earnings video

  1. Hook with the question the viewer has: "Did the company beat expectations?"
  2. Face on camera for the headline. One or two sentences, plain words.
  3. Cut to the source. Show the press release or table, with the figure highlighted.
  4. Return to your face for meaning. What does the number imply?
  5. Repeat per key figure. Three to five beats usually covers a quarter.
  6. Risks and unknowns on camera. It signals honesty.
  7. Close with what to watch next, not a prediction.

Making screen sections watchable

  • Zoom in on the line you are reading so text is legible on a phone.
  • Highlight with a box or underline as you speak.
  • Trim mouse wandering. Use a smooth zoom and pan instead.
  • Dim the rest of the page so the figure stands out.
  • Show the source and date on screen.
  • Do not read the whole table. Pick the lines that matter.

Example: a quarterly results video

Example: a creator covers a retailer's results in 11 minutes. The first edit stays on a screen recording of the results PDF for eight minutes with the creator's voice over it. The second edit opens on camera with the headline, cuts to the PDF for each of four figures with the number highlighted, returns to the camera for the interpretation, and ends on camera with the risks. The creator compares the retention graph in the screen-only section with the mixed sections. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Reading the filing aloud. Summarise and point.
  • Using a company logo that implies endorsement. Be careful with branding.
  • Giving a buy or sell call as fact. Say it is your view and not advice, and take advice on promotion rules.
  • Using screenshots without source and date. Viewers can't check them.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on structuring earnings breakdown videos?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, pacing and pattern interrupts with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. It does not teach financial analysis.

Is a talking head always better than a screen recording?

No. Use the face for meaning and the screen for proof, and let your graph decide the balance.

How long should I stay on a screen recording?

Until the figure has been seen and explained, then return to your face.

Can I show a company's filing in my video?

Public filings are generally fine to show, but check the terms for any logos or paid data you use.

Should I give a verdict at the end?

You can give your view, labelled as opinion and not advice.
