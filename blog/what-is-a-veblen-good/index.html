What is a Veblen good?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A Veblen good is a luxury item that some people want more as its price goes up, because the high price is part of what they are buying. It signals that the owner could afford it. The idea is named after the economist Thorstein Veblen, who described "conspicuous consumption" in 1899; Harvey Leibenstein later called the price-driven part of demand the "Veblen effect".

This page is general education about economics and consumer behaviour, not financial advice.

Key takeaways

  • With most goods, a higher price means fewer buyers. With a Veblen good, a higher price can mean more buyers, up to a point.
  • The price works as a signal: it tells other people the owner could afford it.
  • The term comes from Thorstein Veblen's 1899 book The Theory of the Leisure Class.
  • Harvey Leibenstein separated the "Veblen effect" from bandwagon and snob effects in a 1950 paper.
  • Veblen goods are not the same as Giffen goods, which are cheap staples whose demand rises with price for a different reason.

Where the idea comes from

Thorstein Veblen was an American economist and sociologist. In The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899), he argued that wealthy people spend in visible ways to show their position, and he called this conspicuous consumption. Expensive goods, in his account, are valued partly because they are expensive: they prove that the buyer can waste money without worry.

Veblen did not use the phrase "Veblen good" himself. That label came later, as economists tried to fit his idea into standard demand theory.

The key step was a 1950 paper by Harvey Leibenstein in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, titled Bandwagon, Snob, and Veblen Effects in the Theory of Consumers' Demand. Leibenstein split demand into "functional" demand, for what a product does, and "nonfunctional" demand, which comes from other people's behaviour and from the price itself. The Veblen effect is the part of demand that depends on the price being high, or being known to be high.

Veblen effect, snob effect and bandwagon effect

Leibenstein described three effects that are often confused. The table puts them side by side.

EffectWhat drives demandWhat happens when more people buyEveryday example
Bandwagon effectWanting what others haveDemand risesA trainer brand everyone at school wears
Snob effectWanting what others do not haveDemand fallsA limited run that loses appeal once it is common
Veblen effectThe price itself, as a signalDepends on price, not numbersA luxury item that sells better after a price rise
Normal demandWhat the product does, and costNo direct effectBuying a cheaper loaf of bread

The snob and Veblen effects often appear together, but they are different. The snob effect is about how many other people own the item. The Veblen effect is about how much it costs.

How a Veblen good breaks the normal rule

The usual law of demand says that when a price rises, people buy less. For a Veblen good, that relationship can bend. If the main reason to buy something is to show you could afford it, a lower price weakens the signal. A cheaper version can make the item less desirable to the people it is aimed at.

This does not mean demand rises for ever. At some point, the price is too high even for the target buyers. The Veblen effect describes a range where a higher price helps sales, not a rule that prices can go up without limit.

Real-world evidence for status spending

The idea that people spend to show their position has been tested with data. A study published by the National Bureau of Economic Research looked at spending on visible goods: clothing, jewellery and cars. The authors built a model of status seeking in which "conspicuous consumption is used to reflect a household's economic position relative to a reference group". Its key prediction, that visible spending should fall as the average income of a household's reference group rises, was "strongly borne out in the data".

In plain terms, people spend more on visible items when that spending helps them stand out from the people around them. That is the behaviour Veblen described, measured more than a century later.

A clear modern case is the "I Am Rich" iPhone app. In 2008 it sold for $999.99, and its whole point was the price. MacRumors reported, citing the LA Times, that eight people bought it before Apple removed it from the App Store. The only thing the buyers got was the fact that they had paid.

Veblen goods vs Giffen goods

Both break the normal law of demand, so they are often mixed up. They work in opposite ways.

Veblen goodGiffen good
Typical itemLuxury, status itemCheap staple food
Who buys more when price risesWealthier buyers seeking statusPoorer households
WhyThe price is part of the appealThey can no longer afford dearer foods, so buy more of the staple
What the buyer getsA signal of wealthEnough calories on a tight budget

A Veblen good is about showing wealth. A Giffen good is about being short of it.

Example: a price rise that increases sales

Example: imagine a small leather goods maker selling a handbag for £400. It sells 100 a month. The bag is well made but known as a mid-range brand.

The maker repositions the bag, raises the price to £1,200 and limits supply. Some old customers leave because it is now too dear. But a new group of buyers wants it precisely because it costs £1,200 and fewer people have one. Sales settle at 70 a month.

In real life, many brands try this and simply lose customers, because a high price only works as a signal if people already see the brand as desirable.

Common examples of Veblen goods

Economists usually point to items where the brand and price matter as much as the function:

  • Luxury watches, where a cheaper watch keeps better time
  • Designer handbags and clothing
  • Supercars and rare cars
  • Fine wine and spirits at the top end
  • Exclusive club memberships, where the fee itself is part of the status

None of these is a pure Veblen good. A luxury watch still tells the time and a supercar still drives. The Veblen part is the extra amount people pay above what the function alone is worth.

A Veblen good with no function at all

The purest version of a Veblen good would do nothing except show the price was paid, like the I Am Rich app. Asendify's The £6,000 Club is built on that idea: a numbered certificate with your name, the date and a member number, a private verify link, and no perks, course or access. It suits someone who finds the concept interesting and wants a deliberately pointless status object; it is not proof of wealth, and anyone wanting something useful should spend elsewhere.

FAQ

What is a Veblen good in simple terms?

It is a luxury item that becomes more attractive to some buyers when its price rises, because the high price shows they could afford it.

Who named Veblen goods?

They are named after Thorstein Veblen, who wrote about conspicuous consumption in 1899. Harvey Leibenstein formalised the "Veblen effect" in a 1950 economics paper.

Is a Rolex a Veblen good?

It is often given as an example, because part of what buyers pay for is the brand and what it signals. It still works as a watch, so it is not a pure Veblen good.

What is the opposite of a Veblen good?

There is no single opposite, but a normal good, where demand falls as price rises, is the standard case. An inferior good, which people buy less of as their income rises, is another contrast.

What is the difference between a Veblen good and a Giffen good?

Demand for both can rise with price. Veblen goods are luxuries bought for status, while Giffen goods are cheap staples that poor households buy more of when prices rise because they cannot afford alternatives.
