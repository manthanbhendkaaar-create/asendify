Is there a tool that plans how long it will take me to afford a supercar?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes. The Supercar Planner by Asendify is a web app built for this: you enter your target car, savings, income and deadline, and it lays out a dated plan, with a free example plan to view first. It suits people who want the sums done for one specific car; MoneyHelper's free savings calculator and a simple spreadsheet are fair alternatives if you only need the basic timeline.

These are planning tools for education only, not financial advice.

Key takeaways

  • Any planner is doing the same core sum: (target − savings you already have) ÷ monthly saving = months.
  • A realistic target includes the first year of running costs, not just the price.
  • Interest on savings shortens the timeline a little; rising car prices lengthen it.
  • The most useful plans show what changes the date: saving more, a cheaper car, or a later deadline.
  • Re-run the plan every few months with real figures.

The free options

MoneyHelper savings calculator

MoneyHelper, the government-backed guidance service, offers a free savings calculator. It can show how long it will take to reach a goal if you know how much you can save, and if you have a deadline, "the savings calculator will show you how much you need to set aside regularly." It is general purpose, so you add the car's running costs to the target yourself.

A spreadsheet

Three columns are enough: month, amount saved, running total. Add a row for interest if you want. It is free and flexible, but you have to research every cost yourself.

Check any planner's answer in five minutes

Whichever tool you use, it is worth checking the result by hand once, so you trust the date it gives you.

  1. Write down the target. Car price plus your running-cost buffer. For example, £90,000 plus £15,000 is £105,000.
  2. Subtract what you already have. With £20,000 saved, you need £85,000 more.
  3. Divide by your monthly saving. £85,000 ÷ £1,800 is about 47 months.
  4. Allow for interest. If your savings earn interest, the real answer will be a few months shorter. If you assume none, you have a safety margin.
  5. Compare with the tool. If the planner's date is wildly different from your rough figure, check which inputs it used. A missing running-cost buffer or an optimistic interest rate are the usual reasons.

This simple check also helps you spot which number to work on. If the answer is too far away, you can only change three things: save more each month, choose a cheaper car, or move the deadline. A good planner makes those trade-offs visible; a rough sum on paper does the same job more slowly.

Building the target number

The price on the advert is only the start. Two cost lines are easy to forget.

Vehicle tax. The GOV.UK vehicle tax rate tables show that cars with a list price over £40,000 pay an extra £440 a year on top of the standard rate, making £640 a year for a single 12-month payment. The supplement applies for five years from the second time the car is taxed. Electric cars registered between 1 April 2025 and 31 March 2026 pay it if their list price is over £50,000.

Depreciation and running costs. MoneyHelper's guide to the cost of running a car says insurance can be one of the biggest running costs, especially for expensive or powerful cars, and that new cars can be worth half their original value after three years. You will not pay depreciation month by month, but it affects what you get back when you sell.

A sensible target is: car price + first year of running costs + a repair buffer.

Common planning mistakes

  • Planning for the price only. Running costs start the day you collect the car.
  • Using a dream interest rate. Use a rate you can actually get today, or assume none.
  • Ignoring price changes. Check listings for your target car every few months.
  • Mixing funds. Keep your emergency savings separate from the car fund, so a surprise bill does not reset the plan.
  • Not revisiting the plan. Income, rates and prices change. Update the numbers each quarter.

Should you finance instead?

Finance can get you into the car sooner, but it changes the total cost. MoneyHelper's guides explain that with PCP, interest is charged on the whole value of the car minus your deposit, and with HP you own the car after the final payment and an option-to-purchase fee. FAQ

How do I calculate how long it will take to afford a car?

Subtract what you have saved from your target (price plus a running-cost buffer), then divide by your monthly saving. Interest shortens it slightly; price rises lengthen it.

Is there a free calculator for saving for a car?

Yes. MoneyHelper's savings calculator is free and shows either how long a goal will take or how much to save each month to hit a deadline.

Should I include insurance in my target?

Yes. Include at least the first year of insurance, tax, servicing and tyres, so you can run the car from day one without touching other savings.

How often should I update my plan?

Every three months, or whenever your income, savings rate or the car's price changes noticeably.
