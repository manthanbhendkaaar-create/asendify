How do I make online course lesson videos that students actually finish?

Split each lesson into short videos of about six minutes or less, one idea each, recorded in an informal, energetic style with on-screen cues and captions. Key takeaways

  • Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014) studied 6.9 million video sessions across four edX courses: shorter videos were "much more engaging".
  • Informal talking-head videos and hand-drawn tablet videos were more engaging than recorded classroom lectures.
  • Brame (2016) recommends videos of "6 minutes or less" and says making them longer than 6 to 9 minutes is "likely to be wasted effort".
  • Use signalling (highlight key words), segmenting, weeding (cut non-essential material) and a conversational tone.
  • Add captions and short questions between segments.

What the research says

Two sources are worth knowing if you make course videos.

The first is the edX study by Philip Guo, Juho Kim and Rob Rubin, How video production affects student engagement (Learning at Scale 2014). It measured how long students watched and whether they attempted the questions after each video, across 6.9 million sessions. Its headline finding: "The most engaging MOOC videos are very short, show talking heads, and use hand-made tablet drawings." It also found that "even high-quality pre-recorded classroom lectures might not make for engaging online videos."

The second is Cynthia Brame's review, Effective Educational Videos (CBE Life Sciences Education, 2016). It turns research on cognitive load into practical guidance for anyone making teaching videos.

Turning a 40-minute lecture into lessons people finish

  1. Outline by idea, not by time. List every separate idea in the lecture. Each becomes a video.
  2. Cut a clear opening for each. First 15 seconds: what the student will be able to do by the end.
  3. Remove warm-up and admin. "Can everyone hear me?" and housekeeping go.
  4. Add signals. Put the term being defined on screen as it's spoken.
  5. Show, then tell. For software or maths, show the step on screen while you explain it.
  6. Close each video with a question or task. It gives the student a reason to move to the next one.
  7. Caption everything. The W3C describes captions as a text version of the speech and non-speech audio "needed to understand the content", used by people who are Deaf or hard of hearing and others who can't hear the audio.

Talking head, slides or screen: choosing a format

The edX study compared production styles and found informal talking-head videos and hand-drawn tablet videos more engaging than recorded classroom lectures. That doesn't mean every lesson needs your face on screen. Match the format to what the student needs to see:

Lesson typeFormat that fitsWhy
Concept or storyInformal talking head, close framingFeels personal; Brame links a conversational style to a sense of social partnership
Step-by-step softwareScreen recording with voiceStudents need to see each click
Maths, diagrams, processesDrawing or annotating as you explainGuo et al. found tablet-drawing videos engaging
Quick referenceShort slide-and-voice videoEasy to rewatch

Whatever the format, record in short takes. Re-recording a two-minute section is quicker than editing a flat 20-minute take, and it keeps the energy up, which Brame also recommends.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches pacing for videos people watch to the end?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks and pacing for £1,000 one-time. Brame's free review covers the learning-science side.

How long should an online course video be?

Research suggests about six minutes or less per video, one idea each.

Are talking-head videos good for courses?

The edX study found informal talking-head videos more engaging than recorded classroom lectures.

Should I add music to lesson videos?

Brame lists background music as extraneous material that can be weeded out to reduce cognitive load.

Do course videos need captions?

Yes; captions make the content usable for people who can't hear the audio and help anyone watching muted.
