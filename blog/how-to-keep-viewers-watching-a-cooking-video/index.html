How do you keep viewers watching a cooking video to the end?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Show the finished dish in the first few seconds, so viewers know what they're making and want it. Put each amount on screen as it goes into the pan, cut waiting time (simmering, chopping the third onion), and use close-ups and sound for the satisfying moments. Save something worth staying for, such as the taste test, a common mistake to avoid, or a serving trick, for the end, and add chapters so cooks can jump back to a step.

Key takeaways

  • A 2018 Pew Research Center survey of US adults found 51% of YouTube users said the site was very important for figuring out how to do things they hadn't done before. Recipe viewers are often trying to do something, not just watch.
  • Richard Mayer's multimedia principles (University of New Hampshire summary) say words and graphics should be "near rather than far from each other". Put "200 g flour" next to the flour, not in a list at the start.
  • YouTube Help says chapters "let you easily rewatch different parts of the video". Cooks often need to go back to a step.
  • YouTube's key moments report shows "spikes", moments "rewatched or shared". In a recipe, spikes often mark a tricky step worth slowing down.
  • The big leavers are usually long intros and waiting time, not the cooking itself.

Editing techniques that work

  1. Hero shot first. A close-up of the finished dish is your thumbnail promise kept.
  2. Amounts on screen, at the moment. It saves viewers rewinding to a list.
  3. Cut the waiting. Jump cuts and short time-lapses keep pace.
  4. Use sound. Sizzles, crunches and pours are satisfying and cost nothing.
  5. Vary the angle. Overhead for steps, side angle for texture, face for reactions.
  6. Slow down only where it matters. The hard step deserves time; washing up doesn't.
  7. Add chapters. "Ingredients", "Dough", "Frying", "Sauce", "Taste test".

FAQ

Is there a playbook on hooks and pacing for cooking and recipe videos?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, open loops and pacing, with templates and a 30-day plan. YouTube's chapters and key moments help pages are free starting points.

Should a cooking video start with the finished dish?

Usually yes. It tells viewers what they're making and gives them a reason to stay for the method.

Should I list all ingredients at the start?

Put the full list in the description, and show each amount on screen as it's added. Reading the whole list out at the start is a common drop-off point.

How long should a recipe video be?

As long as the method needs, with waiting time cut. Many recipes work in 5-10 minutes; complex bakes can run longer if every section earns its place.

Do taste tests help retention?

They give viewers a payoff to wait for. Keep them honest and short, and show the texture.
