How do I analyse my YouTube analytics to know what to make next?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 2 November 2026 · Last updated 2 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Read your YouTube Studio reports in a fixed order: impressions and click-through rate (did people choose the video?), average view duration and retention (did it deliver?), traffic sources (where did viewers come from?) and returning viewers (did it build an audience?). Compare each video with your own recent videos rather than with other channels, then make the next video from the one that did best at the stage your channel is weakest at. The free YouTube Studio is enough to do all of this.

Step 1: Pick a fair comparison set

Before you look at any number, choose what you are comparing against. Single numbers mean little on their own.

  • Take your last 10 to 15 long-form videos and leave Shorts out, because they are found and watched differently.
  • Give each video at least two to four weeks so the early surge has settled. YouTube says data usually takes one to two days to process, and a video that is three days old is still being tested.
  • Use the same date range for each video, for example its first 28 days.
  • Keep a simple sheet: title, topic, length, impressions, CTR, views, average view duration, average percentage viewed and main traffic source.

Step 2: Impressions and click-through rate, the packaging test

In the Reach tab, impressions count how often YouTube showed your thumbnail, and impressions click-through rate (CTR) is how often those showings turned into a view. YouTube's own help page says about half of all channels and videos sit between 2% and 10%, and that new channels and videos with very few views vary more.

How to read it:

  • High impressions, low CTR: YouTube was willing to show the video, but the title and thumbnail did not persuade people. The topic may still be right; the packaging probably was not.
  • Low impressions, high CTR: a small, loyal audience clicked, but YouTube did not widen the reach. YouTube notes that videos with fewer impressions often have higher CTR for exactly this reason.
  • Do not react to small daily swings. YouTube advises comparing CTR across videos over the longer term and allowing for different traffic sources.

Step 3: Average view duration and retention, the delivery test

In the Engagement tab, average view duration is the average length of a view, and the audience retention graph shows which parts held attention. YouTube's help page on key moments explains the shapes:

  • Intro: the share of viewers still watching after the first 30 seconds. A low figure suggests the opening did not match the title and thumbnail.
  • Dips: places where people skipped or left.
  • Spikes: parts that were rewatched or shared. These can mean strong content, or a confusing bit people had to replay.
  • Top moments: stretches where almost nobody left. If these come late, move that kind of material earlier.

The report also compares a video with your 10 most recent videos of similar length, which is the comparison that matters. Key moments only appear for videos of at least 60 seconds with at least 100 views. YouTube also warns that high CTR with low average view duration can be a sign of clickbait, which it is less likely to recommend.

Step 4: Traffic sources, the discovery test

Still in Reach, the traffic source report tells you how viewers found each video:

  • Browse features (home page, subscriptions feed) means YouTube chose to put it in front of people.
  • Suggested videos means it was shown beside or after other videos. The "Traffic source: Suggested videos" card names those videos, which tells you what your audience was already watching.
  • YouTube search means people looked for it. The "Traffic source: YouTube Search" card lists the actual search terms, which are some of the best next-video ideas you will get.
  • External and direct or unknown cover links from other sites, apps, bookmarks and signed-out viewers.

A topic that pulls steady search traffic is a candidate for a follow-up or a series. A video that took off through browse or suggested is worth repeating in format and packaging.

Step 5: Returning viewers, the loyalty test

In the Audience tab, YouTube splits your viewers into new, casual and regular. Casual and regular viewers together are your returning audience. YouTube says viewers who come back regularly are more likely to be recommended your future videos, and suggests consistent topics, a familiar format and community features to build that group. It also says a very low regular share, even below 1%, is common for newer channels.

Look at which videos brought in mostly new viewers (good for growth) and which ones your returning viewers watched most (good for loyalty).

A worked example

Here is an invented cooking channel, used only to show the method. The numbers are not real data.

VideoImpressions CTRAverage % viewedMain traffic source
5 quick weeknight pastas7.8%31%Browse features
How to sharpen a kitchen knife4.1%52%YouTube search
My kitchen tour3.0%24%Subscriptions
Cheap meal prep for a week6.5%46%Suggested videos

Reading it in order:

  • Packaging: the pasta and meal prep videos won the click.
  • Delivery: the knife and meal prep videos held people best. The pasta video's retention fell away after the first recipe, so the click was earned but the promise was not kept for long.
  • Discovery: the knife video lives on search, and its search terms include questions about whetstones and honing.
  • Loyalty: the kitchen tour reached mostly existing viewers and held few of them.

Next video: a second meal prep video (strong at every stage), and a follow-up answering the whetstone question found in search terms. The kitchen tour format is paused.

Common mistakes

  • Judging a video in its first few days. Early numbers swing a lot.
  • Chasing CTR alone. A high click rate with poor retention is a broken promise.
  • Comparing with other channels. Your own recent videos of similar length are the only fair benchmark.
  • Ignoring the search terms card. It is your audience telling you what they typed.
  • Changing title, thumbnail and topic at once. You will not know which change mattered.

Key takeaways

  • Read the reports in order: CTR, then retention, then traffic sources, then returning viewers.
  • Compare each video with your own recent videos of similar length.
  • Make more of what did well at your weakest stage, and use search terms for direct ideas.
  • Wait a few weeks before judging, and change one thing at a time.

FAQ

What is a good click-through rate on YouTube?

YouTube says half of channels and videos have an impressions CTR between about 2% and 10%. What matters more is how a video compares with your own recent videos from similar traffic sources.

Which matters more, CTR or retention?

Both, in that order. CTR decides whether people start watching; retention shows whether the video kept its promise. A video needs both to keep being recommended.

How long should I wait before analysing a new video?

Give it at least two to four weeks and compare like-for-like periods, such as each video's first 28 days. Studio data usually takes one to two days to process.

Do I need a paid tool to analyse my analytics?

No. Everything in this method is in the free YouTube Studio. Paid tools mainly save time or explain the numbers in plain language.
