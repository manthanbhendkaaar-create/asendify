Should a recorded conference talk show the slides full screen or picture-in-picture?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Switch between them rather than picking one layout for the whole talk. Show slides full screen when viewers need to read a chart, code or detail; show the speaker full frame for stories, jokes and key points; and use picture-in-picture only when slides are light. Research on lecture videos found mixing speaker and slides was more engaging than slides alone.

Key takeaways

  • In the edX study by Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014), "Videos that intersperse an instructor's talking head with slides are more engaging than slides alone."
  • The same study found "Even high quality pre-recorded classroom lectures are not as engaging when chopped up for a MOOC". A single wide shot of a stage rarely holds online viewers.
  • An eye-tracking study cited in Kizilcec, Bailenson and Gomez found "learners spent 41% of their time looking at the face when it was shown in the video". A speaker box pulls eyes away from the slide.
  • In their field studies, "35% watched videos without the face for self-reported reasons including avoiding distraction."
  • YouTube's audience retention report shows dips where viewers are "abandoning or skipping". Check whether dips line up with unreadable slides.

Why one layout for the whole talk loses viewers

In the room, people choose where to look. They glance at the slide, then at the speaker. Online, the editor makes that choice for them. If the layout never changes, the viewer cannot read the detail (if it is a wide shot) or loses the person (if it is slides only). Picture-in-picture throughout seems like a safe middle ground, but it shrinks both and, on a phone, often makes the slide unreadable.

Changing layouts with the content also creates natural visual changes every 20-60 seconds, which helps attention without adding effects.

How to edit a talk with switching layouts

  1. Get the slide deck as a file. Ask the speaker for the original deck, or record the slide feed directly. Filming the projector screen gives washed-out, keystoned slides.
  2. Record the speaker on a separate camera. A medium shot, chest up, is enough.
  3. Mark each slide by type. Detail slides (read me), image slides (look at me) and title slides (transition).
  4. Apply a rule. Detail slides: full screen, held as long as the speaker discusses them. Image or title slides: speaker full frame or picture-in-picture. Stories and key lines: speaker full frame.
  5. Keep cuts calm. A conference audience expects a steady pace, not quick-fire edits. Change layout when the content changes.
  6. Leave room for a speaker box. If the deck has an empty corner, picture-in-picture can sit there without covering text.
  7. Add lower thirds once. Speaker name and talk title early on, then not again.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on editing conference talks for online viewers?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, pacing and pattern interrupts with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. YouTube's retention help is a free companion.

Is picture-in-picture bad?

No, but it suits light slides. On detailed charts or code, the speaker box covers content and splits attention, especially on phones.

Do I need two cameras?

It helps a lot. One on the speaker plus the slide feed is the minimum for switching layouts well.

Should I keep the audience applause?

Keep a second or two at the start and end for atmosphere. Cut long applause and walk-ons.

What if I only have a wide shot?

Ask for the slide deck and overlay slides full screen during detail sections. It still beats an unreadable projector screen.
