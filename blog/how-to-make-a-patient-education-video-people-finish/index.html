How should a clinic edit a patient-education video so patients watch to the end?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 3 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Answer the question in the first 10 seconds, then explain in the order the patient will experience it. A patient-education video (what to expect at an appointment, how to prepare, aftercare) loses people when it starts with background or jargon. Keep one topic per video, use plain language and simple visuals, show progress ("step 2 of 4"), add chapters to anything over a couple of minutes, and have a clinician review every word.

This page is about video structure and editing. It isn't medical advice: the clinical content of any patient video must come from, and be checked by, qualified clinicians.

Key takeaways

  • Patients watch to reduce uncertainty, so the first line should state what they'll know by the end.
  • Chronological order (before, on the day, after) is easier to follow than a topic-by-topic lecture.
  • One video per question beats one long video that covers everything.
  • On YouTube, the retention report's Intro figure shows how many viewers were still watching after the first 30 seconds, and dips show where they skip or leave.
  • Calm visuals, plain words and captions matter more than polish.

A structure for a three-minute "what to expect" video

  1. 0:00 The promise. "This video explains what happens at your first physiotherapy appointment, from arriving to leaving."
  2. 0:10 Before you come. What to bring, what to wear, how long it takes.
  3. 0:45 When you arrive. Check-in, waiting area, who will call you.
  4. 1:15 During the appointment. The steps, shown in the actual room.
  5. 2:15 Afterwards. What happens next, follow-ups, who to contact with questions.
  6. 2:45 Recap. Three bullet points on screen.

Use chapters so patients can jump to the part they need; YouTube chapters need 00:00 first, at least three timestamps and at least 10 seconds each.

Editing techniques that help understanding

  • Progress markers. "Step 2 of 4" on screen gives people a reason to stay.
  • Signposting. "Next, what happens on the day" before each section.
  • Simple graphics. A labelled diagram beats a paragraph of narration.
  • Steady pace. Patient education should feel calm; avoid fast jump cuts and loud music.
  • Captions and clear audio. Many viewers have hearing loss or watch muted; accurate captions help everyone.
  • Consistent presenter. One familiar face across a series builds trust.

Privacy and consent

If real patients appear, get written consent before filming and before publishing. UK medical guidance, for example the GMC's guidance on recordings for public media, says you "must get the patient's consent, which should usually be in writing". Actors or staff volunteers avoid most of the problem.

FAQ

Is there a book on making explainer videos people finish?

The Retention Editing Playbook (£1,720 one-time) teaches hooks, pacing and structure; it's YouTube-focused and doesn't cover clinical content.

How long should a patient-education video be?

As short as the topic allows, often two to four minutes, one topic per video.

Should patient videos have music?

Light background music is fine if it never competes with speech. Many work well without it.

Can we film real patients?

Only with informed, usually written, consent, and with a clear plan for how the footage will be used.

Who should approve the script?

A qualified clinician should approve all clinical content before filming and before publishing.
