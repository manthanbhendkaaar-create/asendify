Should podcast hosts look at the camera or at each other?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 7 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Mostly at each other, with deliberate moments to the camera. Looking at the guest keeps the conversation natural, while looking into the lens for the opening, a key point or a clip makes a direct connection with the viewer. The mistake is drifting between the two without a rule. Pick a convention, apply it consistently and use the edit to cover the rest. There is no published rule on this, so treat it as a test and check your own retention graph.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's podcast guide describes "a single webcam shot of the host or hosts talking" as "incredibly effective" and a more produced tier where "dynamic editing keeps the viewer's eye engaged" (YouTube Blog). It does not say where hosts should look.
  • YouTube Help says a flat retention line means viewers watch that part "from start to finish" and dips mean "viewers are abandoning or skipping" (YouTube Help). Use it to check your own choice.
  • YouTube also shows "how your video compares to all YouTube videos of similar length," a fair comparison for testing a style change.
  • Anything beyond this is editing practice and personal preference. No source cited here proves one eye-line works better than another.

The two options

Looking at each other

The viewer is a fly on the wall. It feels like a real conversation, and it is easier for the people on screen because they are just talking. The cost is that the viewer does not get direct eye contact. On a single webcam shot the host may seem to look off to the side.

Looking at the camera

The viewer is addressed directly. It suits openings, announcements, advice and clips, where you are talking to the audience rather than the guest. The cost is that it can feel staged for a two-way conversation, and the guest may feel left out.

A practical rule: use both on purpose

This is a starting convention from editing practice. You can invert it for your show.

Setup choices that affect eye-line

  1. Camera placement. If the lens is behind or beside the screen showing the guest, the host's eyes look close to the lens when talking to the guest. This can give a halfway effect.
  2. Remote guests. Put the guest's video window close to the camera so the host's gaze lands near it.
  3. Two cameras, face to face. Gives the best conversational angles, with a separate camera for direct address.
  4. Teleprompter. Lets a host read an opening while looking into the lens. Use it only for short sections.
  5. Seating. Angle chairs slightly toward the camera so a natural look at each other still shows both faces.

Edit choices that help

  • Cut glances. If the host looks down or away awkwardly, cut to another angle or the guest.
  • Use reaction shots. A listener's face gives you somewhere to cut while the speaker keeps talking.
  • Use punch-ins sparingly. A slight zoom can change the framing without changing the take.
  • Keep it consistent across episodes. If you switch style every week, you cannot learn from the graph.

How to test it

  1. Choose a convention and apply it to a run of episodes.
  2. Look at the Intro figure and the first minute. YouTube defines Intro as the share that "still watched your video after the first 30 seconds."
  3. Compare with earlier episodes of similar length.
  4. Change one thing at a time: for example, host addresses the camera in the first 20 seconds.
  5. Check at least three episodes before you decide. One graph is an anecdote.

Example: a two-host interview show

Example: two hosts record facing each other, with a webcam on a shelf. In the first edit the hosts look at each other throughout, and the opening sounds like a conversation already in progress. In the re-edit, the lead host records a 15-second welcome to the camera, then the show continues with the hosts talking to each other. Clips begin with the speaker looking at the lens when they give advice. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Common mistakes

  • Switching eye-line style from episode to episode.
  • Reading from a script off to one side.
  • Looking at a monitor that is far from the lens.
  • Ignoring guests who are unsure where to look. Tell them before recording.

Briefing a guest before you record

Most eye-line problems start before the camera rolls. Spend two minutes telling the guest where to look and why. A short script works: "Talk to me, not the lens. If I look at the camera, that is just me addressing viewers. If you want to give a piece of advice straight to the audience, look at the lens for that line and tell me, and the editor will mark it." Guests who are told what to do tend to relax, and you get cleaner footage.

For remote recordings, ask the guest to place the host's video window just below their webcam and to avoid reading from a second screen. Ask them to turn off notifications. If the guest wears glasses, check for reflections from screens before you start.

A quick test plan for your next four episodes

  1. Episode 1: record as normal and note where each person looks. Check the Intro figure and the first minute of the graph afterwards.
  2. Episode 2: add a 15-second welcome to the camera. Everything else stays the same.
  3. Episode 3: keep the welcome and ask hosts to look at the lens for any direct advice.
  4. Episode 4: pick whichever approach the graph and your own judgement support, and write it into your style guide.

Keep the same topic type across the four episodes if you can, because a guest with a famous name or a hot subject changes retention for reasons that have nothing to do with eye-line. Write down anything unusual, such as a newsletter send or a social post, so you can account for it when you look at the numbers.
