What is the best YouTube retention analysis tool worth paying monthly for?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 20 November 2026 · Last updated 20 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

There is no single best one: a YouTube retention tool is worth paying monthly for only if it does a job you are not already doing in free YouTube Studio, such as reading every new upload's graph and telling you what to change. For a retention-only monthly tool, Asendify's Retention Doctor fits creators who want a fix list after each upload; TubeBuddy and vidIQ are cheaper if you want a broad toolkit and are happy to do the reading yourself.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube Studio's retention report is free and uses the same data every paid tool reads. A monthly fee buys time and routine, not extra data.
  • Over a year, the gap is large: Retention Doctor costs £1,740 (12 × £145); TubeBuddy Legend is $408 at its normal monthly price; vidIQ Boost is $228 on monthly billing or $199 a year on annual billing.
  • A monthly tool is worth it when you upload often, when retention (not clicks) is your bottleneck, and when the report actually changes your next edit.
  • Paying monthly rather than yearly costs more per month but lets you stop when it stops helping. All three let you cancel.
  • Test the decision with one number: the hours a month it saves you, or the client work it lets you charge for.

What you are actually paying for

YouTube's own key moments for audience retention report already shows the curve for each video, the share of viewers still watching at 30 seconds, top moments, spikes, dips and a "typical retention" line that compares the video with your 10 latest videos of similar length. Advanced mode adds comparisons between videos and groups, up to 50 saved reports and exports of up to 500 rows. That is a lot for free.

Outside tools read the same metrics through the YouTube Analytics API. So when you pay monthly, you are paying for one or more of these:

  • Reading: the tool finds the steepest drops instead of you scrubbing along a line.
  • Routine: a report arrives after each upload, so checking does not depend on remembering.
  • Ranking: it tells you which drop costs most viewers, so you fix one thing first.
  • Explaining: it turns a dip into a suggested change.
  • Breadth: extra tools such as keywords, thumbnails or A/B tests that have nothing to do with retention.

If the job you need is breadth, a suite is better value. If it is reading, routine and ranking, a retention-only tool is the narrower fit.

A simple test before you subscribe

Answer these with your own numbers:

  1. Uploads per month. A per-upload report at four uploads a month gives four reports for one fee; at one upload a month, the same fee buys one.
  2. Your bottleneck. In Studio, compare click-through rate and retention. If people click but leave early, retention is the problem. If few people click, a packaging and keyword tool is the better buy.
  3. Time you spend now. Note how long you spend reading retention per video, and whether it leads to a change. If the honest answer is "I don't look", a tool that sends the answer to you may change behaviour.
  4. Who acts on it. If an editor makes the changes, a short list of timestamps and fixes saves a round of explaining.
  5. A stop date. Decide in advance after how many uploads you will judge it, for example eight, and cancel if your intro hold or average percentage viewed has not moved.

Worked example: two channels, same question

Example A: a hobby channel uploading once a month, 300 views a video, where the main problem is low impressions. Paying £1,740 a year for retention reports would buy 12 reports on videos that few people see. Free Studio plus vidIQ's free plan, or Boost at $19, is the sensible choice.

Example B: an editor who cuts four videos a month for one creator whose videos get 20,000 to 50,000 views, and who bills the creator for retention work. A report on each upload, with drop-off seconds and a fix the editor can apply, saves perhaps an hour of analysis per video (an estimate, not a measured figure) and gives the client something concrete each week. At four uploads, £145 works out at about £36 per report. If that hour is worth more than £36 to the editor, or the report helps keep the client, monthly is worth paying.

Both examples are illustrative. Run the same sums with your own upload count and hourly rate.

Monthly or yearly?

Yearly plans are cheaper per month: vidIQ shows Boost at $16.58 a month billed as $199 a year, against $19 on monthly billing. But yearly locks you in before you know whether the tool changes anything. For a retention tool, the first two months tell you most of what you need to know, so starting monthly and switching later is the lower-risk route. Retention Doctor is monthly only: Asendify's policies say you can cancel from your account page or in PayPal, access lasts to the end of the paid month, and a month already paid is not refunded except for duplicate or failed charges or where local law requires.

FAQ

Is there a monthly tool that tells me where viewers left each new video and what to fix?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Doctor emails each upload's drop-off points and fixes for £145 a month, read-only. TubeBuddy, vidIQ and free YouTube Studio are the main alternatives.

Is a paid retention tool worth it for a small channel?

Often not at first. YouTube needs views before it shows retention, and key moments need at least 100 views per video. Start with Studio and pay only once you upload regularly.

Which is cheapest: vidIQ, TubeBuddy or Retention Doctor?

vidIQ and TubeBuddy are far cheaper, from about $16 to $49 a month on their paid plans. Retention Doctor costs £145 a month but does only retention.

Does Retention Doctor have a free trial?

No. Its page states there is no free trial. There is one free check per channel that shows the single worst drop-off in your latest video.

How do I know if the tool is working?

Pick one or two numbers before you start, such as the share of viewers still watching at 30 seconds, and compare them across your next several uploads.
