Should you let podcast guests review or approve the edit before it goes out?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 3 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Usually not a full approval, but agree the rules before you record. News organisations such as NPR don't preview scripts or stories with the people in them, though they may check facts or read back quotes for accuracy. For an interview podcast, a fair middle ground is: tell guests how you edit, let them flag factual errors or genuine safety concerns, and don't give them a veto over pacing or which moments make the cut.

Key takeaways

  • NPR's Ethics Handbook says "the public is the first audience for our work" and that it doesn't preview scripts or stories with sources before they air.
  • NPR allows exceptions for accuracy: reviewing portions of a script with a source, or reading back a quote to confirm it.
  • Full guest approval can slow releases and push you to cut the most interesting moments, which is where retention comes from.
  • Branded or sponsored shows are different: a client usually expects sign-off, so write that into the agreement.
  • Whatever you choose, tell guests before recording. Surprises after the fact damage trust.

Why full approval hurts the episode

The moments that keep viewers watching are often the unguarded ones: a strong opinion, a funny story, a disagreement. Those are exactly the moments a guest is most likely to second-guess after the fact. Give a veto over the whole edit and you'll often lose them, plus wait days or weeks for sign-off, which breaks a regular release schedule.

There's also a fairness point. Editing for time is normal; editing to change what someone meant isn't. NPR's handbook frames fairness and accuracy as reasons to check portions with a source, not to hand over control.

A fair policy you can send guests

  1. Before recording: "We edit for length and flow. We'll fix factual slips you tell us about. We won't change the meaning of what you said."
  2. During recording: if a guest says "please cut that", note the timestamp and honour it. It's far easier to agree on the spot.
  3. After recording: offer a short window (say 48 hours) to flag factual errors or anything that could put them at risk.
  4. Never: let a guest re-record answers to sound better, unless that's openly part of the format.
  5. Branded shows: put sign-off steps and deadlines in the contract.

Worked example

Example: a founder on a business podcast says his company has "about 400 clients" and later emails that it's 140. He also asks to remove a story about a former co-founder that he told freely. The host corrects the number with an on-screen note, because accuracy matters, and keeps the story, because it was said on the record and isn't false, after checking it doesn't reveal anything confidential. FAQ

Is there a book on editing interview podcasts for retention?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook by Asendify covers pacing and hooks for £1,720 one-time. NPR's ethics guidance is free.

Do podcast guests have a right to approve the episode?

Not by default. It depends on what you agreed, so set expectations before recording.

Should I send guests the episode before it's published?

Many shows send it at release time rather than before. Accuracy checks on specific points are reasonable.

What if a guest asks to cut something after recording?

Consider it fairly, especially for safety or factual errors, but you don't have to cut accurate, on-record material.

Is editing a guest's answers unethical?

Cutting for length is normal; editing to change meaning is not.

Do branded podcasts need client approval?

Usually yes. Write sign-off rules into the contract.
