Can you learn YouTube video editing for free?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 19 November 2026 · Last updated 19 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes. You can learn the core skills of YouTube video editing for free: DaVinci Resolve has a free version with free official training books and online exams, and YouTube's Audio Library gives you free music you can use in your videos. What free resources rarely give you is structure, feedback and a plan tied to your own channel, which is where most free learners stall.

Key takeaways

  • Blackmagic Design lists DaVinci Resolve as free for Mac, Windows and Linux, with training guides that link to online exams.
  • Blackmagic's beginner's guide for Resolve 19 was released free in March 2025, covering editing, colour, audio, effects and export.
  • YouTube's Audio Library offers royalty-free music and sound effects you can use, including in monetised videos if you are in the Partner Program.
  • In a large study of free Harvard and MIT online courses, only 3.13% of all learners completed in 2017-18, so the challenge with free learning is finishing.
  • The skills that grow a channel, such as hooks, pacing and retention, are learned by editing your own videos and reading their data.

Blackmagic Design's DaVinci Resolve training page lists Resolve as free and offers training books, each linked to a "Complete Online Exam", plus training videos. Digital Camera World reported that Blackmagic released "a free, step-by-step training guide" for Resolve 19 in March 2025, covering editing, colour correction, audio, Fusion titles and export settings for social media.

For music, YouTube's Audio Library help page says it offers "royalty-free production music and sound effects to use in your videos", that Partner Program channels can monetise videos using them, and that downloaded tracks won't be claimed through Content ID. Some tracks need a credit in the description.

Where free learning runs out

The tools are free; the hard part is sticking with it. Justin Reich and José Ruipérez-Valiente's study of HarvardX and MITx courses, published in Science in 2019, found that completion among all participants was 3.13% in 2017-18, against 15.42% among those who intended to finish and 46.02% among paying learners (authors' postprint). The study does not prove payment causes completion, but it shows how easily free learning drifts.

Free resources also tend to teach software, not YouTube. A guide will show you how to use a blade tool; it will not tell you why viewers left your video at 1:40.

The plan costs nothing but time. Its strength is that every week produces a real edit and a number.

When paying for learning makes sense

Free is enough for many people. Paying tends to make sense only in specific situations:

SituationFree is usually enoughPaying may help
Learning the softwareYes: official guides and videosRarely needed
Getting a first video outYesNo
Plateaued after 10+ videos with the same retention dipsSometimesA structured course on retention can save months of trial and error
Need deadlines to stay on trackOnly if you set your ownPaid or scheduled programmes add commitment
Editing for clients professionallyPartlySpecialised training can speed up skills clients pay for

Before you pay for anything, use the free tools for at least a month. You will know exactly which gap you are paying to close, and you will get far more from any course you choose.

Worked example: a free learner's first two videos

Example: a student starts a study-tips channel with no budget.

  • Video 1: edited in Resolve after two weeks of the beginner's guide. Music from the Audio Library. Retention two days later: 48% still watching at 30 seconds; the biggest dip at 0:20, during a long introduction.
  • Video 2: the student cuts the introduction to one line and shows the first tip at 0:06. Retention: 59% at 30 seconds.

Total spend: £0. The improvement came from reading the data, not from better software. The figures are illustrative.
