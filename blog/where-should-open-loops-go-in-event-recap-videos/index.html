Where should open loops go in an event recap video?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Put the first open loop in the opening 5 seconds, by teasing the event's best moment without showing how it ends, and add a smaller second loop around the halfway point. Pay both off in the last third, just before the call to action. A recap without loops is a montage, and Wistia notes that in 1 to 5 minute videos most viewers drop off around the halfway point, which is exactly where a second loop earns its place.

Key takeaways

  • Most event recaps are a sequence of nice shots with music. An open loop gives them a reason to be watched in order.
  • Loop 1 (0 to 5 s): tease the peak moment: the reveal, the award, the crowd reaction, the speaker's best line, cut off before the payoff.
  • Loop 2 (around the middle): a smaller question ("and then the power went out...") to carry viewers past the usual mid-video drop.
  • Captions matter for recaps shared on social. Meta's creator team notes captions grab the attention of people watching with sound off.
  • Check the YouTube retention report: dips mean viewers are abandoning or skipping at that point. A dip after a loop closes means the next one is too late.

Why recaps lose viewers

People who were at the event watch to spot themselves. Everyone else (future attendees, sponsors, colleagues) needs a reason to keep going. Typical recaps give them:

  • A drone shot of the venue.
  • Lanyards, coffee and people networking.
  • A speaker on stage, no audio.
  • Applause.
  • Logo and "See you next year".

Every shot is pleasant, but nothing makes the viewer wonder what comes next. That is the job of an open loop.

What an open loop looks like in a recap

Loop typeHow it looksWhere it pays off
Teased peakCrowd gasps; cut away before we see whyFinal third, full moment shown
Unfinished quoteSpeaker: "The one thing nobody in this room expected was..." (cut)When the talk clip returns
Countdown to a momentText: "6 hours until the launch"The launch itself
A person's questionAttendee: "I came here to find a co-founder."Later: did they?
Problem on the day"Then the main stage lost power."How the team saved it

All of these must be real moments from the day. Don't stage a crisis or suggest an outcome that didn't happen.

Where loops go in a 2-minute recap

TimeSectionLoop status
0:00 to 0:05Teased peak moment, cut short; event name on screenLoop 1 opened
0:05 to 0:30Arrival, scale, atmosphere, real voicesLoop 1 held
0:30 to 0:55Best talk moments, captionedLoop 2 opened at about 0:50
0:55 to 1:25Workshops, networking, sponsor momentsLoop 2 held

For a 30 to 60 second social cut, keep only loop 1 and pay it off by around 70% of the runtime.

Ending and end screens

If the recap lives on YouTube, the end screen can appear only in the last 5 to 20 seconds of a video at least 25 seconds long. Close your loops before then, so viewers aren't still waiting for a payoff when the end screen elements cover the picture. Related: how to make an event recap video people watch to the end and what shots to get at an event so the recap holds attention.

Plan loops before the event

Open loops depend on capturing the right footage. Before the day:

  • Ask organisers when the likely peak moments are and make sure a camera is on them.
  • Pick one or two attendees to follow from morning to evening (with their consent).
  • Record a short question to camera early and the answer late.
  • Capture the moment before each reveal as well as the reveal itself.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches open loops for event and recap videos?

The Retention Editing Playbook teaches open loops and cold opens for £1,720 one-time; it is written for YouTube and applies to recaps published there.

How many open loops should an event recap have?

One for a short social cut, two for a 2 to 3 minute recap. More can feel like a trailer that never delivers.

Where should the first loop go?

In the first 5 seconds: tease the peak moment, then cut away before the payoff.

Is it misleading to tease a moment?

Not if the moment is real and you show it later. Never imply an outcome that didn't happen.

Should loops close before the call to action?

Yes. Pay them off first, then give one clear next step, such as the date of the next event.
