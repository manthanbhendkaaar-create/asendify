Do worked examples or lectures keep learners watching longer in course videos?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 26 November 2026 · Last updated 26 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

There is no study that settles watch time for worked examples versus lecture, but the research points one way for novices: worked examples usually teach more than problem-solving, and shorter segments hold people longer. A mix works best: a brief explanation, a worked example, then a chance to try.

Key takeaways

  • A 2010 Educational Psychology Review paper says studying worked examples generally produces better learning than conventional problem solving for novices, and often takes less time. The effect can reverse for learners with more prior knowledge.
  • Mayer's multimedia principles include segmenting (median effect size 0.67 in 7 of 7 tests) and signaling (0.70 in 26 of 28 tests).
  • Guo, Kim and Rubin found that in edX videos median engagement was at most 6 minutes, and that tutorial videos were watched for about 2 to 3 minutes whatever their length.
  • Learning and watch time are different measures. Neither study tests worked examples against lecture on watch time directly.

What the question is really asking

Course creators ask this because their retention graph shows students leaving halfway through a lesson. They want to know whether to talk less and show more. The honest answer has two parts. For learning, the evidence favours worked examples for beginners. For watching, the evidence favours short, well-signposted segments. A worked example is usually also a better video: it has something to see and a clear end.

What the evidence says

The Springer paper reports that novices who study worked examples often learn better than those who solve problems unaided, because problem-solving pushes beginners into weak strategies that use up working memory. It also reports an expertise-reversal effect: help that suits novices can be less useful or harmful for experienced learners, so guidance should fade as they improve.

Mayer's principles add video-specific advice. Segmenting means presenting a lesson in learner-paced chunks. Signaling means adding cues such as headings or arrows that show how the material is organised. Coherence means leaving out extras that do not serve the lesson.

The edX study found that tutorials were watched for only about 2 to 3 minutes whatever their length, and were re-watched more than lectures. The authors suggest tutorials should support skimming and re-watching.

Steps to build the lesson

  1. Decide who the learner is. Beginner or experienced? The Springer paper suggests fading support as people improve.
  2. State the goal in one sentence at the start. Tell viewers what they will be able to do.
  3. Give a short explanation. Keep it to the minimum needed to follow the example.
  4. Show one worked example in full. Use real numbers and show each step on screen.
  5. Signal the steps. Number them on screen, as Mayer's signaling principle suggests.
  6. Cut the dead time. Remove pauses while you type or calculate.
  7. Offer a second example with some steps left blank. This fades support gradually.
  8. Split the lesson into segments. One idea per video where you can.
  9. Mark practice as optional for experienced learners. Put it in a separate lesson or a chapter.
  10. Check the graph. If viewers leave during the example, check pace and length.

Where lecture still wins

Lecture is fine for motivation, for context and for ideas that need reasoning rather than steps. The mistake is a long lecture before any example. A short lecture followed by a worked example is more watchable and, by the research above, more likely to teach beginners.

Limits of the evidence

The studies measure different things in different settings. The edX study is correlational and its engagement numbers come from specific courses. The worked-example research concerns learning, not watching. Use both as guidance, then use your own course analytics as the test.

Are worked examples always better than lecture?

No. The Springer paper reports that the benefit can shrink or reverse for learners with more prior knowledge.

How long should a worked example video be?

Keep it as short as the steps allow. The edX study found tutorials were watched for about 2 to 3 minutes whatever their length, so split long ones.

What is segmenting?

Mayer's term for presenting a lesson in learner-paced chunks rather than one continuous unit.

Should I cut the lecture entirely?

No. Keep a short explanation, then show an example.

Do worked examples increase watch time?

None of the sources checked tests that directly. Compare the graphs of your own versions.
