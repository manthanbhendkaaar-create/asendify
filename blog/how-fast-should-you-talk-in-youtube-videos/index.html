How fast should you talk in YouTube videos?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

There is no single correct speed, but most clear speakers land somewhere around 150 to 170 words per minute, and talking a little faster than feels natural usually helps. The largest study of educational video engagement, covering 6.9 million viewing sessions on edX, found instructors spoke between 48 and 254 words per minute and that students "generally engaged more with videos where instructors spoke faster." Speed only helps if every word is still clear, so cut pauses and filler before you try to talk faster.

Key takeaways

  • Guo, Kim and Rubin's 2014 study of 862 edX videos found speaking rates from 48 to 254 words per minute, with a mean of 156 (paper PDF).
  • Within videos of similar length, engagement often rose by up to 2x as speaking rate increased.
  • Their advice: reassure presenters that speaking fast is fine, and edit out filler words and pauses.
  • Pace comes from editing as much as from talking: tight cuts make a calm speaker feel quick.
  • Clarity beats speed. Poor audio or mumbling makes viewers rate content and speakers worse, as research from USC and ANU found.

What the research says

The study most often quoted on video engagement is "How Video Production Affects Student Engagement" by Philip Guo, Juho Kim and Rob Rubin, presented at the ACM Learning at Scale conference in 2014. They analysed 6,902,358 viewing sessions from 127,839 students across four edX courses.

On speaking rate, they found:

FindingDetail
Range of speeds48 to 254 words per minute
Average156 words per minute (standard deviation 31)
Main patternEngagement usually rose, by up to 2x, as speaking rate increased within a length bucket
ExceptionFor 6-12 minute videos, the 145-165 wpm bucket dipped below slower speakers
RecommendationReassure instructors speaking fast is fine; edit out filler and pauses

The authors were careful: faster speakers may also have been more enthusiastic, and enthusiasm is part of the effect. These were educational videos, not entertainment, so treat it as a strong pointer rather than a rule for every niche.

Why faster usually works on YouTube

  • Less waiting. Viewers came for the payoff. Slow delivery delays it.
  • More energy. A brisk pace tends to sound more confident and engaged.
  • Viewers can slow you down. YouTube lets people change playback speed. A viewer who finds you too fast can switch to 0.75x; a viewer who finds you too slow may just leave.

When slower is better

  • Step-by-step tutorials. If viewers need to copy what you do, give each step time on screen.
  • Complex numbers or terms. Pause slightly before and after a key figure so it lands.
  • Emotional or serious topics. A rushed delivery can feel careless.
  • Non-native audiences. If much of your audience watches in a second language, clarity matters more than pace.

How to measure your speaking rate

  1. Pick a typical two-minute section of a recent video.
  2. Turn on captions or export the transcript from YouTube Studio.
  3. Count the words and divide by 2.
  4. Compare your rate with your retention graph for that section.

A rough guide:

Words per minuteHow it tends to feel
Under 130Slow; fine for careful tutorials, risky elsewhere
130-160Relaxed conversational pace
160-190Brisk and energetic; common for commentary
Over 190Very fast; needs excellent diction and audio

These bands are a practical starting point, not research-backed cut-offs.

Talk faster, or edit tighter?

Most creators get more from editing than from forcing their voice. You can make a natural speaker feel much quicker by:

  • Cutting breaths, "um", "so" and false starts.
  • Removing repeated sentences when you said something twice to get it right.
  • Starting each cut on the first important word, not the run-up.
  • Keeping visuals changing so the pace feels lively even when speech is calm.

Our page on why viewers leave in the first 30 seconds applies the same idea to openings, and how loud YouTube audio should be covers making faster speech easy to hear.

Example

A creator records a 10-minute explainer. The transcript has 1,350 words, so the raw rate is 135 words per minute, with many pauses. In the edit, they cut filler and dead air, which brings the video to 8 minutes 10 seconds. The same 1,350 words now run at about 165 words per minute, without the creator speaking any faster on camera. They then compare the first-minute retention against their typical retention in YouTube Studio for the next three uploads to see whether the tighter pace helped their audience.

Tips for speaking faster without losing clarity

  • Warm up your voice before recording; tongue twisters help.
  • Use a good microphone close to your mouth, so quick speech stays crisp.
  • Write short sentences. Long sentences force you to slow down or stumble.
  • Emphasise the key word in each sentence.
  • Record in short sections and re-record anything you rushed.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches named retention editing techniques, with a 30-day plan?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops, re-hooks and pattern interrupts, with a Bonus Pack including a 30-day plan. The free Guo et al. study is a useful read on pacing.

What is a normal speaking rate?

In the edX study, instructors averaged 156 words per minute, with most within about 30 words either side.

Should I speed up my footage to 1.1x?

It can work for some voices but often sounds unnatural. Cutting pauses usually achieves more with less risk.

Do viewers watch YouTube at faster playback speeds?

Some do, and YouTube offers the option. That is another reason not to pad your delivery.

Does talking fast hurt understanding?

Only when words become unclear. Good diction, short sentences and clean audio matter more than raw speed.

How fast should I talk in Shorts?

Shorts usually suit a brisk pace, but every word must still be clear without sound or with captions.
