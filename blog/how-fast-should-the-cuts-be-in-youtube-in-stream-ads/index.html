How fast should the cuts be in a YouTube in-stream ad?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

There is no proven ideal cut rate, so cut as soon as each shot has done its job. In practice that often means quick shots of about 1 to 2 seconds in the first 5 seconds, before viewers can skip, then 2 to 4 seconds per shot once the story or demo is running. Slow down for anything the viewer must read or understand, such as a price, a screen or a product detail.

Key takeaways

  • Google's ABCDs recommend "engaging pacing and tight framing", not a specific number of cuts.
  • Film shots have shortened over decades: researchers measured a fall from about 10 seconds per shot in 1950 to about 4 seconds in 2010. That's a useful reference for "normal" pace, not a target.
  • The same researchers say cuts "trigger eye movements", so every cut asks for a little attention. Too many and the message gets lost.
  • The first 5 seconds can run faster because they must earn the right to the next 25.
  • Measure, don't guess: compare the "video played to" 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% figures, which Google defines as the share of viewers still watching at each point.

What "fast" means in an in-stream ad

In-stream ads are usually horizontal, play with sound, and often appear on large screens including TVs. That affects pacing:

  • Sound carries the thread. A steady voiceover or music track lets you cut visuals faster without confusion.
  • Bigger screens show more. A wide shot on a TV reads in a second; on a phone it may need longer.
  • Lean-back viewing. Viewers who chose a 20-minute video are in a different mood from someone scrolling a feed. Frantic cuts can feel out of place.

Cut on new information, not on a timer

The most reliable rule is: cut when the shot has delivered its information and the next shot adds something new. A cut that changes nothing (same subject, same angle, same message) just adds noise. A shot held after its point is made invites the skip.

Ask of every shot:

  1. What does the viewer learn from this?
  2. Have they learned it yet?
  3. Does the next shot add something?

If the answers are "nothing", "yes" or "no", cut or remove it.

Pacing without more cuts

You can raise energy without shortening every shot:

  • Movement inside the shot: a walking presenter, a product turning, a push-in.
  • Punch-ins: a quick scale change on a talking head.
  • Sound design: a whoosh, a click, a music change at a key moment.
  • Text animation: words appearing in time with the voiceover.
  • Pattern interrupts: a sudden change in setting, colour or angle. See pattern interrupts in video ads.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches pacing for video ads?

The Retention Editing Playbook teaches pacing and pattern interrupts for £1,720 one-time; it is YouTube-first. In the Blink of an Eye by Walter Murch is a classic on cutting.

Is there an ideal number of cuts per second?

No. Google recommends engaging pacing without a number. Cut when a shot has done its job.

Should the first 5 seconds be faster?

Usually, yes. They must show the problem, product and brand before the skip button appears.

Can cuts be too fast?

Yes. If viewers can't follow the message or read the text, faster cuts lower results.

Do TV placements need slower cuts?

Often slightly. Test a version with a little more time on each shot for connected TV.
