How can an agency make client video ads hold attention more consistently?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

An agency makes client video ads hold attention consistently by turning good instincts into a system: a brief that names one viewer and one promise, a fixed opening structure, a shared editing checklist, planned hook tests on every campaign, and a weekly loop where results go back to the editors. Consistency comes from process, not from relying on your best editor being on every job.

Key takeaways

  • Most inconsistency starts before the edit. In a BetterBriefs/IPA study, 80% of marketers said they write good briefs, but only 10% of agencies agreed.
  • Standardise the first 3 seconds; that is where most viewers decide.
  • Test several openings per campaign. TikTok recommends 3 to 5 different creatives per ad group.
  • Use emotion on purpose: research summarised by Harvard Business School found surprise is best at grabbing attention and joy at holding it.
  • Close the loop: share what won and why with every editor, every week.

Where consistency breaks down

When one client's ads hold viewers and another's don't, the cause is rarely the software or the editor's talent. It is usually one of four gaps:

  1. The brief. Nobody wrote down who the ad is for or the single thing it promises.
  2. The opening. Each editor starts ads their own way, so quality depends on who got the job.
  3. Testing. One version goes live, so there is nothing to learn from.
  4. Feedback. Results sit in the media buyer's dashboard and never reach the edit suite.

The brief problem is well documented. The BetterBriefs study published by the IPA surveyed more than 1,700 marketers and agency staff in over 70 countries. Marketers estimated that 33% of marketing budget goes to waste through poor briefs and misdirected work, and 78% of marketers said their briefs gave clear strategic direction, against 5% of agencies. If the brief is vague, the edit has to guess.

1. A brief the editor can use

Ask the client for five things in plain words: who the viewer is, the problem they have, the one promise, the proof, and the action you want. Anything else is background. An editor who knows the single promise can judge every shot against it.

2. A fixed opening structure

Give every ad the same first 3 seconds: a visual hook, a short text promise, and a line that raises a question the ad will answer. TikTok's creative best practices advise introducing the proposition in the first 3 seconds and putting the hook in the first 6. Google's ABCDs of effective video ads say the same thing in different words: get to the core of the story faster, with engaging pacing and tight framing.

3. Emotion as a tool, not an accident

Harvard Business School's Working Knowledge reported on research by Thales Teixeira with Michel Wedel and Rik Pieters in which 58 adults watched 30-second ads and could skip them with the space bar while cameras tracked their faces and eyes. Surprise was most effective at capturing attention, and joy was best for holding it; several short bursts of joy kept attention better than one long stretch. A practical reading for editors: open on something unexpected, then keep small moments of reward coming.

4. Test openings, not whole ads

Keep the body and call to action fixed and swap only the first 3 seconds. TikTok recommends testing 3 to 5 different creatives per ad group, preferring versions with large differences, and refreshing creative when performance steadily declines. On YouTube, Google Ads video experiments let you run creative as a single variable in a two-armed test, with conclusive results at 95% confidence.

5. A weekly results review

Once a week, the creative lead shows the team which openings held viewers and which didn't, with the ads side by side. Add a line to the checklist whenever the same fault shows up twice.

Worked example

Example: an agency runs ads for six local gyms. Four editors cut them, and results vary widely by editor. The agency introduces the system.

  • Week 1: one-page brief for each gym; every ad gets the same 3-second opening template.
  • Week 2: each campaign launches with four openings on the same 20-second body: a result shot, a member's question, a surprising class moment, and a price-led line.
  • Week 3: the media buyer shares which openings held viewers past the first few seconds. The creative lead plays the winners in the team meeting and the checklist gains one line ("first frame shows a person moving, not an empty gym").

Within a month, every editor is working from the same opening, the same checklist and the same evidence. The numbers will still differ by gym, but the gap caused by who cut the ad shrinks, because the faults that caused it are caught before launch.

FAQ

Is there a system an agency can use to make client ads hold attention?

Yes: a one-page brief, a fixed opening, a shared checklist, planned hook tests and a weekly results review. A playbook such as Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook can supply the shared rules and templates.

Why do some editors' ads hold viewers better than others?

Usually because of habits, not talent: how they open, how fast they reach the promise and how much they cut. Writing those habits down lets everyone use them.

How many versions of a client ad should we test?

TikTok recommends 3 to 5 creatives per ad group. Keep the differences large and change one part, usually the opening.

How often should creative be refreshed?

When performance steadily declines, according to TikTok's guidance. Watch the trend rather than refreshing on a fixed calendar.

Should clients see the checklist?

Yes, if it helps. Sharing it explains why you push back on a logo intro or a crowded script, and makes approvals faster.
