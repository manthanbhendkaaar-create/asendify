How do I make a portfolio company showcase video for a venture capital website?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Let the founder tell one specific story in under two minutes: what the company does in plain words in the first ten seconds, who it helps, one moment that shows the product working, and why the investor backed it, kept honest and approved by both sides. A showcase video on a venture capital website has two audiences, founders deciding whether this firm is a good partner and visitors checking what the portfolio actually is. Both leave quickly if the video is a logo reel with music and no explanation.

Key takeaways

  • Wistia's data suggests "<1 min" for brand and social videos and "1-5 mins" for videos that "educate or explain", and says "engagement naturally declines after the five-minute mark" (Wistia). A showcase that explains a company sits in the first two ranges.
  • Google's video guidance says to "jump in", "feature people to help you tell a better product story" and keep messaging simple (Google Ads Help). It is advice for ads, but its principles suit showcase films.
  • A LinkedIn marketer writes in IAB UK that "79% of videos are watched on mute" on LinkedIn (LinkedIn's own analysis) and that subtitles are "table stakes" for speech (IAB UK). Many VC videos are shared on LinkedIn.
  • The FCA says financial promotions "must be clear, fair and not misleading regardless of the media type" (FCA). Whether a given showcase counts as a financial promotion depends on its content and audience; take advice.

What the video is for

A VC website visitor is usually one of four people: a founder looking at the firm's taste and style, a potential co-investor or LP checking the portfolio, a journalist or analyst, or a candidate. A good showcase video helps the first and last most, because they want to understand the company and the working relationship. Investors and analysts want facts, which belong in text and data rather than a video.

That suggests the video should be a human introduction to the company: what it does, who it serves and what it is like to build it. It is not a pitch deck on screen.

Structure that works for a 60 to 120 second showcase

Keep the partnership beat honest and specific ("they helped us with our first enterprise hire") rather than generic praise. Generic praise from founders about their investor sounds like marketing, because it is.

Step-by-step production

  1. Agree the purpose and the approvals. Confirm with the founder and the firm who signs off the final cut and what claims may be made. Get written permission to use names, logos, footage and any numbers.
  2. Pick facts you can verify. Use statements the company is happy to stand behind and that are current. Avoid unsupported superlatives ("the fastest growing...").
  3. Prepare three questions, not a script. What do you do? Who needs it? What is the hardest part? Natural answers are easier to watch than read lines.
  4. Film in the company's real setting. Office, warehouse, lab or customer site, with good light and clean sound. Record audio carefully; poor sound loses viewers faster than modest visuals.
  5. Capture product footage. Screen recordings or real use, at readable size. Check small text on mobile; many viewers will watch on phones.
  6. Cut for the first ten seconds. Open on the clearest sentence, not on a logo or a drone shot.
  7. Add captions. Check names, numbers and technical terms for accuracy.
  8. Add a short end card. One link, one action.
  9. Review for claims. Read the final cut for anything that could be read as a promise or investment inducement and get review from the right person.
  10. Check the graph after publishing. YouTube's retention report shows an intro figure for "the first 30 seconds"; for a 90-second video that is a third of the film.

What to leave out

  • Funding rounds and valuation talk unless the company agrees and it is accurate and appropriate for the audience.
  • Predictions of returns or growth. Do not suggest outcomes for investors.
  • Competitor comparisons you cannot document.
  • Jargon-heavy voiceover. If the first ten seconds need a glossary, rewrite.
  • A montage of logos with music. It tells viewers nothing.

Format variants

  • Founder portrait (60 to 90 seconds). One person, one story. The most flexible format.
  • Product-in-use (45 to 75 seconds). Customer or user shown with the product. Needs permission from the people filmed.
  • Short vertical clip (20 to 40 seconds). For LinkedIn and Shorts; strong first line, captions, one idea.
  • Annual portfolio reel (2 to 3 minutes). Several companies in a few seconds each; keep a consistent template and a clear label for each company.

Example: a 90-second founder showcase

Example: a seed-stage VC firm features a logistics software company. The first cut opens with a drone shot of a warehouse and a logo sting, then a founder talking for 20 seconds about the company's mission. The re-cut opens with the founder saying "We help small warehouses plan pick routes in minutes instead of hours" over a screen recording, shows a dispatcher using the product, and gives one concrete example of how the firm helped with hiring. The firm checks the intro percentage and the viewer drop-off at the product demonstration. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Measure and iterate

On your own site, use whatever video analytics your host gives you (play rate, average watch time). On YouTube, read the retention graph: "flat" means viewers watch "from start to finish", "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping" (YouTube Help). Compare across several portfolio videos rather than judging one.

How long should a showcase video be?

60 to 120 seconds is a practical range; shorter for social.

Who should speak?

A founder or customer, with one honest story.

Do I need approvals?

Yes, from the company and the firm, for claims, names and footage.

Should I include numbers?
