How do I structure a how-to video so viewers stay for step 5?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Show the finished result first, tell viewers how many steps there are, keep each step short and visible, and give a small reason to reach the next one. By step five, people have decided whether the payoff is worth the effort, so the structure has to keep reminding them where they are and what they will get. Chapters, on-screen progress markers and a clear result at the start do more than a longer intro or louder music.

Key takeaways

  • Mayer's segmenting principle says "people learn better when the information is presented in user-paced segments, rather than in one continuous unit" (Dartmouth). Numbered steps and chapters are segments viewers can control.
  • The signalling principle says "people learn better when there are cues that emphasize information that is more important" (same source). On-screen step labels and highlights are cues.
  • YouTube's retention report describes dips as viewers "abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" and flat lines as viewers watching "from start to finish" (YouTube Help). In a how-to video, dips often sit where a step starts slowly.
  • YouTube chapters need the first timestamp at 00:00, at least three timestamps and at least 10 seconds each (YouTube Help). A step-by-step video fits this naturally.
  • Wistia says "engagement naturally declines after the five-minute mark" (Wistia); a five-step video can easily run past it.

Why step 5 is where people leave

Early steps benefit from the intro's energy. By the middle, effort is real and the finish is still far away. Viewers who only wanted one step skip to it, and viewers who are lost give up. In a how-to video the graph often looks like a slope with small dips at each step start, then a drop around the last third.

Three things cause it. The payoff is not in view, so the effort feels abstract. The steps run at the same pace, so nothing marks progress. And the later steps are the hardest, which is where the viewer's confidence is lowest.

Step by step: build the structure

  1. Write the result in one sentence. If you cannot, the video lacks a clear payoff.
  2. Cut the steps to what is needed. Combine or drop any step that is optional; mention extras at the end.
  3. Name the steps with verbs. "Cut the board" beats "Part 2".
  4. Order by dependency and by reward. Put steps that give a visible change early, as long as the order still works.
  5. Open on the result. Show it for a few seconds before explaining anything.
  6. State the count. "Five steps, about twelve minutes" sets expectations honestly.
  7. Add chapters. Use the step names; first timestamp at 00:00, at least three in all, at least 10 seconds each.
  8. Show a progress label. A small "Step 3 of 5" in a corner works as a signal.
  9. Trim each step to action. Cut false starts and repeated explanations; keep the part that shows the hands or screen.
  10. Re-hook at the middle. Show a quick flash of the result and say what the next two steps add.
  11. Give step 5 extra care. Show the check that proves it worked; do not rush it because you are tired of recording.
  12. Close the loop. Show the finished result again and say what to do if it did not work as expected.
  13. Check the graph. After publishing, look for dips at step boundaries and shorten those steps.

Example: a five-step video on fitting a shelf

Example: a DIY creator makes a 12-minute video on fitting a floating shelf. The first version starts with a 90-second intro about tools and then runs five steps at the same pace. The second opens on the finished shelf with books on it, says "five steps, about 12 minutes", and lists them on screen. Step 1 (find the studs) and step 2 (mark the line) are each under a minute. At step 3 the creator flashes the finished shelf and says steps 4 and 5 cover drilling and the final check. A "Step 4 of 5" label appears in a corner. Step 5 shows the level test and what to do if it tilts. Chapters carry the step names. After publishing, the creator compares the graph at each step start with the first version. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

What to avoid

  • A long intro before the result. Show the payoff first.
  • Steps of equal length no matter what. Short steps for easy actions; more time for hard ones.
  • Hiding the count. Honest numbers help viewers decide to stay.
  • Skipping the check at the end. Step 5 is where people need confirmation.
  • Overpromising speed. If it takes 12 minutes, say so.

Safety note

If the how-to involves tools, electricity, chemicals or anything risky, say so on screen and follow the maker's instructions. This page covers editing structure, not safety advice.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on keeping viewers through a tutorial?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, open loops and pacing with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. It does not promise results.

Should I show the result first?

Usually yes. A few seconds of the finished result gives the steps a purpose.

How many steps is too many?

Cut to what is needed; if you have more than seven, consider grouping them or splitting the video.

Do chapters hurt retention?

They let people skip, but they also help viewers find the step they need and return later.

What if a step needs waiting time?

Cut or speed it up, and use the pause to explain why the wait matters.
