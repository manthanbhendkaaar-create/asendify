How do I use text overlays in a UGC ad without clutter?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Use text overlays to add what the picture and voice do not already say: a hook line, a label for the key proof point, and a final action. Keep each overlay to a few large words, inside the safe margins of the screen, timed to the speech, and remove any that merely repeat the voiceover. If an overlay does not make the ad clearer in sound-off viewing, delete it.

Key takeaways

  • TikTok's creative guidance says to "use captions or text overlays to provide context to your ad" and recommends "5-10 words per second when using text" (TikTok for Business). Treat 10 words a second as a ceiling, not a target.
  • Mayer's redundancy principle says "people learn better using on-screen graphics with voiceover", and that adding on-screen text on top of both can overwhelm learners (Dartmouth). Word-for-word text over narration can hurt rather than help.
  • Mayer's signalling principle says "people learn better when there are cues that emphasize information that is more important" (same source). Short, targeted text that highlights the key point is a signal, not clutter.
  • The WCAG explanation of captions says they convey "not only the content of spoken dialogue" but also non-speech information such as sound effects and speaker identification (W3C). Captions and overlays are different jobs, and it helps to keep them apart.

Overlay or caption?

Many UGC ads mix two kinds of text, and clutter often comes from confusing them:

TypeJobStyle
CaptionsShow what is said, for sound-off viewers and accessibilityLower-middle, consistent, readable, in sync
OverlaysAdd information not in the speech: hook, label, price, actionLarger, short, placed deliberately

If both appear at the same time in different places, the screen can become busy. Decide which one leads at each moment.

The three overlays most UGC ads need

  1. The hook line. A few words in the first second or two that name the problem or promise.
  2. The proof label. A short tag at the moment of proof: "Dried in 5 minutes", but only if true and shown.
  3. The action line. One short line at the end: "Link below" or the offer, kept accurate.

Beyond those, add overlays only where the voice cannot carry the information, such as a spelling, a number or a name.

Rules that reduce clutter

  1. One overlay at a time. Never stack two on screen together.
  2. Few words. Aim for about three to six words per overlay. This is a practice guide.
  3. Large size. Readable on a small phone without zooming.
  4. High contrast. Use a consistent outline, shadow or background that works over any footage.
  5. Safe margins. Keep text away from the edges and from areas covered by app interface elements.
  6. Sync with speech. The text appears as the idea is spoken, not before and not long after.
  7. Short on-screen time, long enough to read. At least about one second for two words, longer for more, and do not hold past the moment.
  8. Consistent style. One font, one or two colours, one animation style across the ad.
  9. No text over the product's key feature. Do not hide what you are showing.
  10. Remove duplicates. If the caption already shows it, drop the overlay.

Where to place text

Frame contentGood text position
Face centredAbove the head or lower third, not over the face
Product at the bottomText near the top
Demo in the centreText at the top or bottom edge in margins
Split screenText on the dividing line only if short

Always check the frame on an actual phone with the app's own interface overlaid, if you can.

Example: a skincare UGC ad

Example: an agency edits a 25-second skincare ad. Version one has large animated captions word by word, a hook title, a price badge at the top, a sticker saying "NEW" and a bottom banner with a discount code, all within the first ten seconds. The editor strips it back. The hook line "Dry skin by 3pm?" appears for the first two seconds, simple captions follow the voice at the lower middle, one proof label appears as the creator shows her skin in daylight, and one action line closes the ad. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result. The agency tests both versions on the same audience and compares early view-through and completion.

Testing overlays

Change one thing: for example, remove all overlays except the hook, and compare. Watch whether early drop-off changes and whether viewers who stay take the action. Remember that many viewers watch muted, so test with sound off. Be cautious about concluding from small numbers, and note that creative fatigue affects later results.

Mistakes

  • Reading the voiceover aloud and showing it in full in a large font.
  • Different fonts and colours for each overlay.
  • Overlays covering the face or the product.
  • Text so small or fast it cannot be read.
  • Stickers, arrows and emojis added "for energy" without purpose.
  • Claims in text that the video does not support. Claims in ads must be true and substantiated; check your local advertising rules.

Checklist

  • Hook, proof and action overlays only, unless needed.
  • One overlay at a time.
  • Large, high contrast, inside margins.
  • Timed to speech.
  • Nothing hiding the product.
  • Works with sound off.

Keeping a simple log

Whatever you change, write it down. A table with the date, the video, the single change you made, the metric you watched and what happened takes two minutes to update and saves a great deal of guessing later. Include a column for what else was different, such as a bigger topic, a holiday week or a different upload time, because those confound results. After five or six entries you will see which changes helped repeatedly and which only looked good once. Treat any single result as a hint rather than proof, and only promote a change to your standard routine once it has held up on more than one video.

How many words can an overlay have?

Should captions and overlays both appear?
