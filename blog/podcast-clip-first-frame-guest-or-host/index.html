Should podcast clips use the guest's face or the host's face in the first frame?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Start on whoever is saying the hook line, which is usually the guest, with the first word already under way. Use the host's face only when the host's question is the hook. The first frame should show a person speaking with their mouth moving and eyes engaged, not a pause, a wide shot or a title card. There is no published rule that one face performs better, so this is a test for your own show: try both and read your Shorts and Reels numbers.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's podcast guide advises: "Edit these into vertical videos and post them as YouTube Shorts" (YouTube Blog). It does not say which face to show first.
  • Meta says "build for vertical" and "put your key messages within the Reels safe zone" (Meta for Developers). Faces and captions in the first frame must sit inside that zone.
  • TikTok's ad guidance says "introduce your content proposition in the first 3 seconds" and "prioritize your hook in the first 6 seconds" (TikTok Ads Help). It is for ads, but it points the same way: say the hook early.
  • YouTube Help defines dips as "viewers are abandoning or skipping" (YouTube Help). Shorts analytics show a comparable viewed-versus-swiped measure; check how your platform describes it.

What the first frame has to do

A clip appears in a feed of other videos. In the first moments, the viewer asks: who is this, and why should I watch? The first frame answers visually; the first line answers verbally. The face matters less than whether the frame feels alive:

  1. A face already speaking. No lead-in silence.
  2. Close enough to read emotion. A tight or medium crop, not a wide shot.
  3. Inside the safe zone. Faces and captions clear of the interface.
  4. A caption of the hook line, so the point lands without sound.

Step by step

  1. Choose the hook line. The first sentence that makes someone want the next one.
  2. Find the speaker. Whoever says it owns the first frame, unless the question is the hook.
  3. Trim to the first word. Start the clip on the breath before the word, not before it.
  4. Crop for vertical. Centre the face with the eyes about a third down, and leave room for captions.
  5. Add the caption. Show the hook line as text, reviewed for accuracy.
  6. Decide the second shot. Cut to the other person or a cutaway after a beat to keep movement.
  7. Check the safe zone. Preview in the app with the interface overlay if you can.
  8. Export a version with the other face first and test.

Common first-frame mistakes

  • Starting with a title card and no face.
  • A wide shot where both people look tiny.
  • A pause or "so" before the line.
  • Faces too close to the top or bottom of the frame.
  • Starting on the host nodding and the guest not yet speaking.

How to test

  1. Pick five clips with a clear guest hook and make two versions each: guest first, host first.
  2. Post them with similar timing and the same captions.
  3. Compare retention and swipe-away figures in the platform's analytics.
  4. Look at differences across all five clips rather than one.
  5. Record the finding in your style guide.

Keep other variables stable, since titles, thumbnails and posting time also affect results.

Example: a business podcast clip

Example: a host asks a founder, "What was the biggest mistake in year one?" and the founder answers, "We hired before we had a customer." The editor makes two versions: one starting on the host's question, and one starting on the founder saying the answer, with the first words already in progress and the line captioned. After a week, the team compares the two. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

  • Judging from one clip.
  • Ignoring that different topics perform differently.

Framing details for vertical clips

When you reframe a horizontal conversation for vertical, track the speaker's face rather than centring the frame. Most editors have an auto-reframe tool, but check it by eye, because it can drift to the wrong person during overlap. Leave space above the head and keep the face out of the top and bottom edges, where app buttons and captions sit. Place captions in the lower middle, inside the safe zone, and avoid covering the mouth.

For two-person clips, consider a split screen with each face in half the frame, or a fast cut between speakers. A split screen lets the viewer see both reactions, but faces become small, so test it on a phone. If you use a title or topic label, keep it short and put it away from the first frame's face.

Building a simple test log

Keep a small spreadsheet with one row per clip: topic, hook line, first-frame face, crop, caption style, platform, date and the viewed versus swiped figure or equivalent. After twenty clips you will see patterns that no general rule can give you, such as which guests, topics and caption styles work for your audience. Review the log monthly and update your clip style guide with one or two findings at a time.
