Retention Doctor vs vidIQ: which is better for fixing audience retention?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 19 November 2026 · Last updated 19 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

For fixing audience retention specifically, Retention Doctor is the narrower fit: it reads each new upload's retention data and tells you the second viewers left and what to change. vidIQ is the better choice if you want a broad, cheaper growth suite for keywords, trends, ideas and an AI coach, with retention as one part of a bigger toolkit.

Key takeaways

  • vidIQ is a general YouTube growth tool: its plans page lists AI credits, trend research, keyword ideas, competitive intel, an AI Coach and thumbnail generation.
  • vidIQ's US list prices are $0 (Free), $19 a month (Boost) and $49 a month (MAX), with annual discounts; it is much cheaper than Retention Doctor.
  • Retention Doctor does one job: it connects read-only to one channel and reports where viewers left each new video, with a fix for each drop, a Retention Score from 0 to 100 and a Monday three-fix plan.
  • Both rely on the same underlying retention data YouTube shows in Studio, which typically takes one to two days to process.
  • If your main problem is views from search and ideas, start with vidIQ or YouTube Studio. If your main problem is viewers leaving, a retention-only tool is more focused.

What each tool is built for

vidIQ's plans page describes a broad toolkit. The free plan includes 150 AI credits a month, niche trends, content ideas and growth tips from its AI Coach. Boost adds 2,000 or more AI credits, unlimited trend research and subscriber insights, keyword ideas, optimisation suggestions, competitive intel and thumbnail generation. MAX adds more credits, more AI Coach conversations and a "Max Mode" described as suited to channel audits. Retention is not the headline feature on that page.

vidIQ's own guide to audience retention (updated June 2026) sends readers to YouTube Studio for the curve: "Open the Analytics tab and scroll to the Audience retention graph". It also gives a rule of thumb that "a good audience retention rate is roughly 30% average percentage viewed for most long-form videos" and to aim to keep at least 60% of viewers past 30 seconds. That is useful guidance, but it is vidIQ's benchmark, not an official YouTube figure.

Retention Doctor by Asendify is built only around the retention graph. It reads your own data with read-only access, finds where viewers dropped on each new upload about 48 hours after it goes live, and gives a fix for each drop, a Retention Score out of 100 and a three-fix plan every Monday.

How the data behind both works

Neither tool can see more than YouTube shares. YouTube's key moments for audience retention guide explains the four shapes on the chart: flat lines mean viewers watch that part through, gradual declines mean interest fades, spikes are moments rewatched or shared, and dips mean viewers are abandoning or skipping. The intro figure shows what share of viewers was still watching after 30 seconds. Key moments only appear on videos at least 60 seconds long with at least 100 views, and data typically takes one to two days to process.

So the difference between tools is not access to secret data. It is how much reading, prioritising and explaining the tool does for you, and whether retention is its focus.

Worked example: choosing for two channels

Example one: a cooking channel with 2,000 subscribers gets 40% of views from search, but most videos stall at 300 views. Its retention is fine (around 45% at 30 seconds), but titles and topics miss what people search. vidIQ Boost at $19 a month, or the free plan plus YouTube Studio, is the sensible spend. A retention tool would mostly report "no big problems".

Example two: a commentary channel with 8,000 subscribers gets plenty of browse impressions and a healthy click-through rate, but viewers leave in the first minute. On a 12-minute video, retention falls from 100% to 52% by 0:45, with a sharp dip at 0:20 where a sponsor read starts. Here the money problem is retention. A retention-only tool that flags the 0:20 dip on every upload, and tracks whether moving the sponsor read helped, is the better fit. These figures are illustrative.

When you need neither

If you upload once a month and are happy to spend 20 minutes per video in YouTube Studio, the free tools can be enough. Open the retention report two days after upload, note the intro figure and the biggest dip, watch the 15 seconds before the dip, and write down one change for the next video. Check the typical retention line, which compares the video with your own 10 latest videos of similar length. Paid tools save time and add structure; they do not unlock data you cannot already see.

Questions to ask before paying for either

  1. What is my actual bottleneck? Impressions, click-through rate or retention. Studio's reach and engagement tabs will tell you.
  2. How often do I upload? A weekly report has more value at one upload a week than at one a month.
  3. Who reads the analytics? If an editor makes the changes, a plain-English list of drops is easier to hand over than a full dashboard.
  4. What access does the tool ask for? Prefer read-only scopes, and check what you can revoke in your Google account.
  5. Can I cancel? Both offer monthly options; check the terms on each site.

FAQ

Is there a tool that only reads my retention graph and tells me what to fix?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Doctor reads one channel read-only and flags each drop-off with a fix. vidIQ covers broader growth, and YouTube Studio is free.

Is vidIQ cheaper than Retention Doctor?

Yes. vidIQ lists a free plan, Boost at $19 a month and MAX at $49 a month (US prices). Retention Doctor is £145 a month.

Does vidIQ show audience retention?

vidIQ mentions retention in its channel audit features, but its own retention guide points creators to YouTube Studio's Analytics tab for the full curve.

Can I use both?

Yes. Some creators use vidIQ for topics and keywords and a retention tool for editing feedback. Only pay for both if both problems are real.

What is a good audience retention rate?

There is no official YouTube benchmark. vidIQ suggests roughly 30% average percentage viewed for long-form videos; your own recent videos are the fairest baseline.
