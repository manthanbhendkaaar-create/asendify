Can I use a bold claim like "the best" as the hook in a client's video ad?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Only if the client already holds evidence for it, or if it's such an obvious exaggeration that nobody would take it literally. Under the UK CAP Code, any claim viewers are likely to read as fact, like "the fastest", "No.1" or "cuts bills by 30%", needs documentary evidence before the ad runs. A testimonial or "users say" framing doesn't get round that. A hook built on a specific, provable result usually holds attention just as well, without the risk.

Key takeaways

  • The ASA says that "before distributing or submitting a marketing communication for publication, marketers must hold documentary evidence" for objective claims (CAP rule 3.7).
  • What counts is how viewers are likely to read the claim, not what the client meant.
  • Testimonials "alone are unlikely to be considered sufficient to substantiate objective claims".
  • Obvious exaggeration ("puffery") generally doesn't need evidence, but calling a factual claim an opinion doesn't make it one (ASA on puffery).
  • The ASA's six tips on substantiation add that stronger claims need stronger evidence, and results must be described accurately.

Why editors reach for bold claims

The first seconds of an ad decide whether people stay, and a big claim is a fast way to make a promise. "The best coffee in London" or "No.1 rated app" feels like a strong hook. The problem is that the bolder and more specific it sounds, the more likely viewers treat it as fact, and the more evidence the client needs.

The pre-edit claims checklist

  1. List every claim in the script, captions, voiceover and end card, including on-screen text.
  2. Mark each one objective (provable fact) or obvious puffery.
  3. Ask the client for the evidence file for every objective claim before editing starts.
  4. Check comparisons ("better than", "cheaper than") cover the competitors you name or imply.
  5. Match the claim to the evidence. If the study says "up to 30%", don't say "30%".
  6. Keep testimonials honest. A real quote can't carry a factual claim the client can't prove.
  7. Save the file with the project so the agency can respond quickly if the ASA asks.

Example: a cleaning-product ad

Example: a client wants the hook "Kills 100% of germs" for a 15-second TikTok ad. The editor asks for the evidence. The lab report says "kills 99.9% of bacteria in a 5-minute contact test" and says nothing about viruses.

Who's responsible?

The advertiser (the client) is primarily responsible, but agencies and others involved in making an ad can also be named in ASA rulings. Building a claims check into your edit process protects both of you, and saves re-edits when an ad is pulled.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches strong hooks without risky claims?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks and pacing for £1,720 one-time. The ASA's free guidance covers claims.

Can I say "the best" in an ad?

Sometimes, if it reads as opinion or obvious puffery. If viewers would read it as a factual comparison, you need evidence.

Does a customer review count as evidence?

Not for objective claims. The ASA says testimonials alone are unlikely to be enough.

Do these rules apply to TikTok and Instagram ads?

Yes. The CAP Code covers non-broadcast ads, including paid social and influencer ads in the UK.

What if the client insists on an unprovable claim?

Explain the risk in writing, suggest a provable alternative, and let the client decide with that on record.
