How do I write a first frame for a stock market explainer video that stops the scroll?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Make the first frame the exact question or number the viewer clicked for, shown large on screen with a person or chart already in motion. Skip the logo, the channel intro and the "hey guys". The first 30 seconds should deliver what the title and thumbnail promised, because YouTube treats that as the sign of a good opening. This is education, not financial advice.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's retention report says the "Intro" figure shows "what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds", and links a high figure to content that "matched the viewer's expectation of the video's thumbnail and title" (YouTube Help).
  • Dips on the graph "mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video", so the first frame is where you see the first one.
  • YouTube's spam policy bans "maliciously misleading titles, thumbnails, descriptions or imagery to trick users into clicking" (YouTube Help). A dramatic first frame must be true.
  • The FCA expects financial promotions to be "fair, clear and not misleading", with risk warnings that are clear and prominent (FCA FG24/1).
  • Extra material that does not help the point can hurt: Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded" (Dartmouth).

What makes a good first frame for a stock explainer

A stock explainer asks viewers to care about a company or a market move they may only half understand. The first frame has to say "this is the thing you wondered about" in under a second. That means one idea, readable on a phone, with no setup.

Write the first frame and first line together

The frame and the first sentence work as a pair. Write both at once.

  1. State the question as a viewer would type it. If the title is "Why did Nvidia fall 10% in a day?", the first frame shows that move and the first line names the day.
  2. Show evidence, not decoration. A real earnings page or chart beats a stock image of a trader.
  3. Put the date on anything with a number. Prices and ratios go stale, and a dated figure is honest.
  4. Keep the frame uncluttered. One chart, one label, one face at most.
  5. Check the frame against the thumbnail. If the thumbnail promises a crash, the first frame should show the crash, not a studio.
  6. Do not promise outcomes. Avoid "this stock will 10x". It is misleading and a compliance problem.

Where the risk warning goes

If you discuss specific investments, UK rules around promotions can apply, especially if you are paid or you are a firm. The FCA's social media guidance says, for long-form video, a risk warning should be "displayed clearly and prominently on the screen for the section of the video" it relates to, and for short-form video "clearly and prominently displayed across the screen throughout" the promotion. A small line of grey text at the end will not do. Check the guidance and take advice if your video could count as a promotion; this page cannot tell you whether yours does.

For general education, a short on-screen note at the start, such as "education only, not advice", is honest and does not need to dominate the first frame.

Example: a first frame for a stock explainer

Example: a creator is making "Why did Nvidia stock fall 10% in one day?" for YouTube. Version A starts with a three-second channel animation, then "Hey guys, welcome back". Version B opens on a one-day price chart with the drop circled and the date shown, and the presenter says, "On this day, the share price fell sharply. Here is what actually happened." The creator checks the retention graph for both after a week. If the figure after the first 30 seconds is better on B, the opening is doing its job. Common mistakes

  • Opening with your own results. "I made 40% this year" is a claim that needs evidence and may breach promotion rules.
  • Using a dramatic crash image that is not about the topic. It breaks the thumbnail promise.
  • Starting with a disclaimer wall. Keep the first frame about the viewer's question and place warnings where the rules need them.
  • Tiny chart labels. They are unreadable on a phone.

Should a stock explainer start with the price?

Only if the price is the answer to the title question. Otherwise start with the question and bring the number in within the first line, dated.

Can I use a dramatic crash thumbnail and first frame?

Only if the video is about that crash. YouTube's policy targets titles and thumbnails that trick viewers into clicking.

Do I need a risk warning on a finance video?

It depends on whether the video is a financial promotion under FCA rules. If it is, the warning must be clear and prominent. This is general information, not legal or financial advice.

How do I know if my first frame works?

Open the retention report and read the figure for viewers still watching after 30 seconds, then compare it across your videos.
