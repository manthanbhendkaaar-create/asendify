How should captions look on a video ad so people can read them at a glance?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Captions on a video ad should be short, big and easy to read in one glance: one or two lines at most, around 42 characters per line or fewer, a plain bold font, light text on a dark box or with a strong outline, placed inside the platform's safe area and away from the bottom buttons, and on screen long enough to read. Broadcasters' subtitle guidance suggests about 160 to 180 words per minute as a comfortable reading speed, so if your voiceover runs faster, trim the words rather than cram them. Animated word-by-word captions can work in feeds, but only if each word is still readable; style should never cost legibility.

Key takeaways

  • The BBC's subtitle guidelines recommend 160 to 180 words per minute, two lines (three only if no important picture is hidden), and lines no wider than 68% of a 16:9 frame.
  • Netflix suggests keeping lines to around 42 characters to avoid display problems near the edge of the frame.
  • Burned-in (open) captions always show; uploaded caption files can be switched off and may not display in every ad placement.
  • Contrast and placement matter more than the font you choose.
  • Captions that are hard to read make people rewatch or scroll, which is the opposite of what you want.

What broadcast guidance says

Subtitle rules from broadcasters were written for exactly this problem: text people must read while watching moving pictures.

Ads are watched on phones, often in a fast-moving feed, so treat these as upper limits. In a 9:16 ad the frame is narrow, which usually means fewer characters per line than 42.

Rules that keep captions readable

  1. Keep lines short. Break at natural pauses in speech, not mid-phrase. "Stop losing viewers / in the first three seconds" reads better than "Stop losing viewers in the / first three seconds".
  2. Two lines at most. One line is often better in vertical ads.
  3. High contrast. White or yellow text with a dark outline, shadow or semi-transparent box. Avoid thin white text on bright footage.
  4. Big enough for a phone. If you squint at it on your own phone at arm's length, it's too small.
  5. Inside the safe area. Keep captions above the bottom bar of buttons and captions on TikTok, Reels and Shorts, and away from the right-hand icons.
  6. Pace to the speaker, but never faster than people can read. If the script is too fast, cut words from the caption or the script.
  7. Check spelling and names. Auto-captions often get brand and product names wrong.
  8. Don't cover the face or product. Move captions when something important is on screen.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches on-screen text for video?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook (Asendify) covers pacing and on-screen text for £1,720 one-time. Broadcaster subtitle guidelines are free alternatives.

Should captions be burned in or uploaded?

For feed ads, burned in, so they always show. Add a caption file as well where the platform supports it.

What's the best caption colour?

There's no single best. White or yellow with a dark outline or box is readable on most footage.

Are word-by-word captions better?

Not proven. They suit some fast creator-style ads; test them against phrase captions.

How many words per caption?

Aim for a short phrase that reads in a glance, roughly one or two short lines.
