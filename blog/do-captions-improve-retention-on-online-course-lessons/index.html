Do captions improve retention on online course lessons, and what style works best?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes, accurate captions usually help course lessons: a 2015 review of more than 100 studies found captions improve comprehension of video, and they are essential for deaf and hard-of-hearing learners. The best style for lessons is plain, accurate closed captions that learners can turn on, not flashy word-by-word animated captions. On-screen text that copies the narration exactly can add mental load.

Key takeaways

  • Morton Ann Gernsbacher's review, Video Captions Benefit Everyone (2015), says "More than 100 empirical studies document that captioning a video improves comprehension of" the video, and that captions particularly help people watching in a non-native language.
  • A critique by Learning and the Brain notes much of that evidence comes from foreign-language viewers, college students and self-report, so "benefits everyone" may overstate it for every group.
  • The W3C's accessibility guidance defines captions as "a text version of the speech and non-speech audio information needed to understand the content", and says they must be synchronised with the audio.
  • Yue, Bjork and Bjork (2013) found on-screen text identical to narration impaired learning compared with slightly reduced text. Burned-in, always-on transcripts may not be best for every lesson.
  • Mayer's redundancy principle, summarised by Dartmouth: "People learn better using on-screen graphics with voiceover."

A strong default: accurate closed captions on every lesson, key-term callouts for the most important ideas, and a downloadable transcript.

Why "accurate and optional" beats "flashy and always on"

The research points in two directions, and both are useful. Captions help many learners understand and follow a lesson, especially those watching in a second language, in noisy places, or with hearing loss. That supports adding captions to everything.

But on-screen text that exactly duplicates what's said can split attention between reading and watching. Yue and colleagues found that slightly reduced text beat exact text for learning, even though learners preferred the exact version. That is why closed captions, which learners choose to switch on, and key-term callouts, which highlight rather than duplicate, suit teaching better than always-on, word-by-word captions borrowed from short-form video.

Accuracy matters most. Auto-generated captions often get technical terms, names and numbers wrong, which is exactly the content learners need. Always review them.

Captions on course lessons vs course promo clips

The same course often needs two caption styles. Lessons are watched by paying learners who want to understand, so plain, accurate, optional captions fit. Promo clips for Reels, Shorts or ads compete in muted feeds, so bold burned-in captions help them get noticed and understood without sound. Mixing the styles up is a common mistake: flashy captions make lessons harder to follow, and plain, small captions make promo clips easy to scroll past.

How to caption a course lesson well

  1. Start from an accurate transcript. Use auto-captions as a draft, then correct technical terms, names and numbers.
  2. Sync carefully. Captions should appear with the words, not seconds before or after.
  3. Include meaningful sounds. If a sound matters ("[alarm beeps]", "[click]"), caption it.
  4. Keep lines short. Two lines at most, broken at natural phrase breaks.
  5. Avoid covering the key visual. Make sure captions don't sit over the code, formula or button you're explaining.
  6. Add key-term callouts separately. Highlight new vocabulary or steps on screen in a consistent position.
  7. Offer a transcript. Learners use it to revise and search.

Example: captioning a coding course

Example: an online coding course has 40 lessons with auto-generated captions. Learners report that captions mangle function names and that the captions cover the code at the bottom of the screen.
