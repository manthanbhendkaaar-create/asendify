How do I use the end screen on a travel vlog to send viewers to the next episode?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

End the day's story first, then use the final 10 to 15 seconds to set up the next episode with something real, such as the next location or an unresolved problem, while the end-screen video element for that episode sits on screen. Put your travel episodes in a playlist so that viewers who do not click still roll into the next one.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube says "End screens can be added to the last 5–20 seconds of a video", the video "has to be at least 25 seconds long", and you can add "up to four elements" on a 16:9 video (YouTube Help).
  • Element types include video or playlist, subscribe, channel and link; external links need YouTube Partner Program membership, and end screens are not available on videos made for kids (same page).
  • YouTube's retention report shows an "Intro" figure for the first 30 seconds, and dips where "viewers are abandoning or skipping" (YouTube Help). The last seconds of the vlog are where a handoff succeeds or fails.
  • YouTube advises that titles "accurately represent the video" (YouTube Help). The next episode's title should match what you tease.

Why vlogs are well suited to episode handoffs

A travel vlog is a story in sequence: a journey with places, problems and people. Viewers who liked day three are natural candidates for day four. The risk is that your ending is a trail-off: a sunset shot and "see you in the next one". That gives the viewer no reason to choose your next video over whatever the app suggests.

A good handoff keeps the story moving. It uses a real thread from the day, such as tomorrow's destination, a missed bus, a promise of a hike.

Step 1: Finish the day's story

Before the end-screen window, close the episode's arc. If the vlog promised "will we get to the summit?", answer it. A vlog that ends on an unanswered question from its own opening leaves viewers annoyed; a vlog that ends on a new question from the next day leaves them curious.

Step 2: Choose a real teaser

Use something from your footage that is true and specific.

Teaser typeExampleRisk
Next location"Tomorrow, the night train to the coast."Low
Unresolved problem"We still have no booking for tonight."Low if real
Promise of an experience"Tomorrow is the market I came here for."Low if delivered
Manufactured cliffhanger"You will not believe what happened."High: viewers feel misled

Avoid exaggerated teasers. If the next episode delivers less than you imply, viewers learn to ignore your handoffs.

Step 3: Time the end screen

YouTube lets you place end screens in the last 5 to 20 seconds, with a minimum video length of 25 seconds. For a vlog, 12 to 15 seconds is usually enough: a one-line recap, a one-line teaser and the click. Use a calm background shot with no important action. Leave the area where elements will sit free of captions, logos and text.

Step 4: Choose the elements

  • Next episode video element as the primary choice, large and central.
  • Playlist element for the whole trip, for viewers who want to start from the beginning.
  • Subscribe only if the viewer is new; a returning viewer may have subscribed already.
  • Link to a gear list or itinerary if you are in the Partner Program; otherwise use the description.

Four is the limit on 16:9; two is cleaner. Check on a phone, since layouts shift on small screens.

Step 5: Build the playlist order

If your episodes are in a playlist in trip order, autoplay can carry viewers who do not click. Name episodes clearly with the number and place: "Japan Day 4: Kyoto temples and a missed train". Use consistent naming so viewers can find where they left off.

Step 6: Say the handoff aloud, in your voice

Do not rely on the visual elements alone. Say it: "The next video is right here." Viewers watching on TV or in a lean-back mode may not notice small elements; a spoken line helps. Keep it brief and natural.

Step 7: Check the retention graph

Open the last 30 seconds of the vlog. If viewers drop well before the end, the story has lost them before the handoff. If they stay until the end then drop off, your handoff is the weak part. Change one element: teaser line, element layout or tail length, and compare with the next vlog.

Example: a three-episode trip

Example: a creator publishes a three-day trip. Episode 1 ends on the sunset with "thanks for watching" and an end screen with four elements. Episode 2 ends the day's story, then says, "Tomorrow we take the night train, if we can get tickets," with an end screen showing episode 3 and the trip playlist only. Step 8: Think about where else viewers go

Pinned comment with the next episode link, a description line at the top, and a card mid-video are other tools. Do not overdo them. For a series, consistency matters more than variety: viewers learn where to look.

Common mistakes

  • Trailing off with a generic goodbye.
  • A manufactured cliffhanger that the next episode does not deliver.
  • Too many elements. Pick the next episode first.
  • Captions over the elements.
  • Wrong playlist order. Autoplay sends people to the wrong day.
  • Never checking the last 30 seconds of the graph.

FAQ

How long should a travel vlog end screen be?

YouTube allows the last 5 to 20 seconds. Around 12 to 15 seconds is usually enough for a recap, a teaser and the click.

Should I use a cliffhanger?

Only a real one, taken from your footage. A manufactured cliffhanger that is not delivered teaches viewers to ignore you.

Which elements should I include?

The next episode and the trip playlist are the most useful. Add subscribe or a link only if needed.

Do I need a playlist?

It helps: viewers who do not click can still roll into the next episode in order.
