When should b-roll of ingredients appear in a cooking video?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Show ingredient b-roll at the moment the viewer needs it: when you name the ingredient, when it changes the dish or when it is used in a step. Give each shot only as long as it takes to recognise it, and put one clean overview of the ingredients near the start rather than a long parade before any cooking happens.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's retention report says "Intro tells you what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds", and that dips mean viewers are "abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video" (YouTube Help).
  • Mayer's signalling principle says "people learn better when there are cues that emphasize information that is more important" (Dartmouth); an ingredient shot at the moment it is named is a cue.
  • The coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded" (same page); long ingredient montages that do not support a step are extraneous.
  • Spatial contiguity says "corresponding images and text are placed close together" (same page), so put any ingredient label next to the ingredient.
  • YouTube advises that titles "accurately represent the video" (YouTube Help); an opening that shows the finished dish matching the title helps the first 30 seconds.

What ingredient b-roll is for

In a cooking video, ingredient b-roll does three jobs. It tells the viewer what they need (a shopping list in pictures), it shows what something looks like (what "ripe" or "finely chopped" means) and it adds rhythm between talking and cooking. When it does none of those, it is decoration, and viewers feel the delay.

The rule of thumb is that b-roll should answer a question the viewer has at that moment.

Step 1: Open with the finished dish, not the pantry

The first 30 seconds decide whether a viewer stays. The title makes a promise: "Creamy mushroom pasta". The first frame should show it. An opening on a bag of flour followed by ten seconds of other ingredients makes the viewer wait for what they came to see. Show the finished dish for two or three seconds, say what makes it worth making, then move into the ingredients.

Step 2: Give one clean ingredient overview

A short overview helps viewers decide whether they can cook this tonight. Show the ingredients arranged together, in one shot, for three to five seconds, with a simple text list or an on-screen label. Put the full quantities in the description. This replaces the long montage of each item being picked up.

Step 3: Time b-roll to the narration

Place each ingredient shot when it is spoken or used.

  1. Name, then show. When you say "smoked paprika", cut to the jar or the spoon of it as you say it, not before.
  2. Show the change. If an ingredient changes the dish, such as lemon zest brightening a sauce, show it at that moment.
  3. Show texture or state when it matters: "diced, not minced", "golden, not brown".
  4. Show quantity when the viewer might misjudge: a tablespoon of oil, a pinch of salt.
  5. Do not show what the viewer already sees. If your hands are holding the onion, you do not need a separate onion cutaway.

Step 4: Keep shots short

Most ingredient shots need one to three seconds to be recognised. Longer shots feel slow unless they are beautiful and you want that pause. A common approach is to vary: quick cuts for common items, a slightly longer shot for a hero ingredient, and a close-up for texture. These are guides; test on your own audience.

Step 5: Use text and graphics sparingly

Put ingredient names or quantities as short labels near the ingredient, not across the frame. Spatial contiguity supports placing labels close to what they describe. Avoid covering the pan with text. If you use units, be consistent with the description and say whether measures are metric or cups.

Step 6: Keep the cooking continuous

A frequent mistake is cutting away from the pan to b-roll at the moment the viewer wants to see what happens. When food is cooking, show the cooking. Use ingredient b-roll in the gaps: while something simmers, in the prep stage or at the start of a new step.

Step 7: Check the graph

Compare your retention graph for the first minute and for each section. If viewers leave during the ingredient introduction, shorten it or move the shots into the steps. If they rewatch a spike at a technique, consider adding a closer shot next time. YouTube suggests reviewing dips to understand why audiences lost interest in a segment; apply that to the ingredient section first.

Example: a 10-minute pasta video

Example: a creator opens with a three-second shot of the finished pasta, says in one sentence what makes the sauce quick, then shows an overhead of eight ingredients for four seconds with a short text label. In the steps, a close shot of the garlic appears when it is named, and a shot of the grated cheese appears when the cheese goes in. A long montage of picking up each item, used in an earlier video, is gone. The creator compares the first-minute graph of the two videos. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Step 8: Be accurate about ingredients

If a recipe involves allergens or a substitution that changes the result, say so. Show the ingredient the recipe actually uses and not a similar-looking one. This is part of being honest with viewers; it also reduces comments asking what you used.

Common mistakes

  • Opening with raw ingredients instead of the finished dish.
  • Long montage of every item.
  • B-roll over the pan at a key moment.
  • Labels covering the food.
  • Showing a different ingredient from the one used.
  • Skipping the retention check.

FAQ

Should I show all ingredients before cooking?

A short overview is useful, but a long parade slows the start. Open on the finished dish first.

How long should each ingredient shot be?

Usually a few seconds, long enough to recognise the item. Test on your own videos.

Where do I put the quantities?

In the description, and on screen only when the viewer might misjudge.

Can b-roll cover mistakes in the main footage?

It can hide a cut, but do not use it to misrepresent what was used or how long something took.
