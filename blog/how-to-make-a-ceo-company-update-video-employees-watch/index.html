How do I make a CEO or company update video that employees actually watch?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Open with the single thing that changes for employees ("From January, everyone gets..." or "We missed our target, and here's what that means for you"), keep the video to a few minutes, film it plainly at a desk, and post a short written summary and captions with it. If you have more than one or two topics, split them into separate short videos rather than one long all-hands recording. Employees watch internal updates for one reason: to find out what it means for them. A video that makes them wait for that answer gets skipped.

Key takeaways

  • First 15 seconds: what's changing and who it affects. Context comes after.
  • One topic per video; a few minutes each.
  • Informal and direct beats polished; a laptop camera is enough.
  • Be honest about bad news early. Burying it at minute nine damages trust.
  • Always add captions and a written summary with links; many people will read instead of watch.
  • Ask whether video is even the right format; some updates are better as a short written note.

What the guidance and evidence say

SourceWhat it says

Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014)In 6.9 million edX sessions, median engagement time was at most about six minutes whatever the video length, and informal office-setting videos held viewers better than studio productions
Wistia on video lengthEngagement "naturally declines after the five-minute mark"

The edX and Wistia data come from learning and marketing videos, not internal comms, but they point the same way: shorter, plainer and more direct holds attention.

Why employees skip company update videos

  • The warm-up: two minutes of thanks, weather and "exciting times" before any news.
  • Everything in one recording: results, reorganisation, office move, benefits and a product demo in a 40-minute all-hands.
  • Corporate language: "We're leaning into synergies across our go-to-market motion."
  • Over-production: a studio set and autocue that make the CEO sound like a press release.
  • Bad news buried after good news, which people notice.

A structure for a 3-minute update

TimeWhat to sayExample
0:00 to 0:15The headline and who it affects"We're changing how bonuses work from April. It affects everyone, and most people will get more certainty."
0:15 to 1:00WhyThe real reason, in plain words
1:00 to 2:00What it means in practiceTwo or three concrete examples for different teams
2:00 to 2:40What happens next and whenDates, who to ask, where the details are
2:40 to 3:00How to ask questionsQ&A session, channel or form; then stop

Example lines are illustrative. Don't promise outcomes the company hasn't committed to.

Delivering bad news on video

  • Say it in the first sentence. People can tell when they're being warmed up.
  • Be specific: which teams, how many people, by when, and what support exists.
  • Don't let the video replace conversations that should be one to one. Anyone directly affected by redundancy or role changes should hear it from their manager, and in the UK that has to follow a proper consultation process.
  • Give a clear place for questions and answer them visibly.

Filming and editing tips

  • Film at a desk or in a normal room. It reads as honest and is easy to repeat weekly.
  • Speak from bullet points, not a script. The edX study found more energetic delivery held viewers longer.
  • Cut the start. Begin on the first sentence of news, not "is this recording?"
  • Use simple visuals only where they help: one chart, one slide with the key date.
  • Add chapters or timestamps if a video must run longer than a few minutes.
  • Post the written summary first, with the video underneath for people who want tone and detail.
  • Check the captions for names and product terms.

Splitting an all-hands into watchable pieces

If you hold a live all-hands, record it, but don't just post the 50-minute file. Cut it into topic clips:

ClipLength
CEO headline update3 to 4 minutes
Quarter results explained3 to 5 minutes
Team spotlight or customer story2 to 3 minutes
Q&A highlights, by question1 to 2 minutes each

Post the full recording too, for people who want it.

Example: replacing a monthly 45-minute recording

Example: the founder of an 80-person company posts the full monthly all-hands recording. Analytics on the internal video tool show most people stop within the first few minutes, and the same questions come up in Slack every month.

From the next month, she records a 3-minute video on the day of the all-hands: the headline (a new client won and two hires approved), what it means for each team, and the next month's priority. The all-hands is cut into four topic clips with titles. A five-line written summary goes in the company channel with links. Company and details are illustrative.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches how to keep viewers watching to the end?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks and pacing for £1,720 one-time. GitLab's free handbook covers internal and async video.

How long should a CEO update video be?

A few minutes per topic. If it needs longer, split it or add chapters.

Should I post the full all-hands recording?

Post it, but also cut short topic clips and a written summary; most people won't watch the full file.

Should company update videos be professionally produced?

Usually not. A plain, direct desk recording is easier to repeat and often feels more honest.

Is video better than an email for company updates?

Not always. Ask whether written, spoken or video is the best format for each message, and pair video with a written summary.
