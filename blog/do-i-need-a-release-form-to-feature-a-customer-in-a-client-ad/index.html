Do I need a release form to put a customer in a client's video ad?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 4 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

In practice, yes. UK ad rules say an ad must not feature a testimonial without permission and advise getting written permission before showing identifiable members of the public, and under UK GDPR an identifiable person's image is their personal data. So before a real customer's face, voice or words go into a client's ad, get a signed release that says where, how and for how long the footage will be used, and keep it with the project files.

This page is general information about UK rules, not legal advice. For a release template, ask the client's lawyer or a production lawyer.

Key takeaways

  • ASA guidance on testimonials: "Marketing communications must not feature a testimonial without permission" (CAP rule 3.50), and marketers must hold evidence it's genuine plus the person's contact details.
  • The ASA's guidance on implied endorsements urges written permission before portraying identifiable members of the public, and says a customer's identifiable home is covered too.
  • The Intellectual Property Office notes that "under UK GDPR a photograph is classed as someone's personal data" when the person is identifiable.
  • If you rely on consent, it must be freely given, clearly worded, recorded, and it "can be withdrawn at any time".
  • Testimonials must not be edited out of context, so a release doesn't let you rearrange someone's words into a new claim.

Why real customers are worth the paperwork

Real people talking about a real result often hold attention better than polished brand footage: viewers recognise themselves, and a specific story creates a natural open loop ("what happened next?"). That's why clients hand agencies customer clips, reviews and UGC. The paperwork is what makes that footage safe to run.

Example: a gym client's UGC ad

Example: a gym client sends an agency eight member videos from Instagram for a January ad.

  1. The agency asks the client which members have signed releases. Three have; five only posted publicly.
  2. The editor builds the ad from the three released clips and asks the client to request releases from the other five.
  3. One member's clip says "I lost two stone in eight weeks". The client can't prove that's typical, so the editor uses a different line from the same member about enjoying the classes.
  4. Release forms, contact details and the original uncut clips go into the project folder.
  5. Two more members sign during the week; they go into a second version.

The ad keeps its strongest hook (a real member's first-day nerves) and nothing runs without permission. Details are illustrative.

Editing rules that go with the release

  • Don't change meaning. Cutting for length is fine; stitching words into a new sentence isn't.
  • Keep claims provable. A real customer's statement can still be an objective claim the client must back up.
  • Keep the originals. If the ASA asks, the uncut footage shows you didn't distort it.
  • Watch for minors. Under-18s need parent or guardian consent, and child performers may need a council licence.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches editing customer stories for retention?

Yes. The Retention Editing Playbook covers story structure and pacing for £1,720 one-time. The ASA's guidance on testimonials is free.

Can I use a customer's public review video in an ad?

Not without permission. ASA rules say testimonials need permission, and posting publicly isn't the same as agreeing to an ad.

Does a release need to be written?

ASA guidance urges written permission, and written records make consent easy to prove.

What if the customer withdraws consent?

Consent can be withdrawn under UK GDPR. Plan how you'll pull or re-edit ads if that happens.

Do I need releases for people in the background?

It depends how identifiable and prominent they are. Blurring or reframing is the safe option for passers-by.
