Can I use a shocking or curiosity hook in an ad without breaking the rules?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Yes, as long as the hook is true and the ad pays it off. A curiosity hook ("The one setting that halved my editing time") or a surprising statement is fine; a hook that creates a false impression, exaggerates what the product does, or is contradicted by small print is not. In the UK, the CAP Code says ads "must not materially mislead", must not exaggerate a product's capability or performance, and need evidence for objective claims. Honest hooks also work better: viewers who feel tricked leave and don't buy.

General information about UK advertising rules, not legal advice.

Key takeaways

  • CAP Code rule 3.1: "Marketing communications must not materially mislead or be likely to do so."
  • Rule 3.7: marketers must hold documentary evidence for claims consumers are likely to see as objective.
  • Rule 3.11: ads must not mislead "by exaggerating the capability or performance of a product".
  • The ASA judges the overall impression, and qualifications "may clarify but must not mislead by contradicting the claims that they qualify."
  • Curiosity works by opening a gap in knowledge; the ad must close it.

Why curiosity hooks work

Curiosity, in the psychologist George Loewenstein's account summarised by PBS NOVA, arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge". A good hook opens a gap that the viewer wants closed: what the setting is, how the result happened, why the common advice is wrong. That's why "Here's the mistake..." keeps people watching. The obligation is simple: close the gap with something real.

What the rules say

From the CAP Code's section on misleading advertising and the ASA's misleading advertising overview:

RuleWhat it means for hooks
3.1 Must not materially misleadThe impression the hook creates must be true
3.3 Must not omit material informationKey conditions can't be hidden, unclear or too late
3.7 Hold evidence for objective claims"Halved my editing time" needs proof before the ad runs
3.9 and 3.10 QualificationsMust be clear, and can't contradict the headline claim
3.11 No exaggerationDon't overstate what the product does
Unqualified superlatives"The best" is treated as a claim against all competitors and needs evidence

The ASA says it considers "the impression created by marketing communications as well as specific claims", so a hook can mislead even if each word is technically true.

Shock hooks

Shock grabs attention, but it can bring the wrong viewers and, if it's unrelated to the product, little else. Platform policies also restrict shocking content in ads, so check the rules for the platform you use. A useful test: would the hook still make sense if the viewer watched the whole ad? If the payoff disappoints, the hook was bait.

Worked example

An illustrative ad for a video-editing template pack opens with: "Delete this from every video." The ad then shows a 4-second logo intro being removed and explains that starting on the most interesting moment keeps viewers watching.

CheckResult

Does the ad pay it off?Yes, within 5 seconds
Any objective claims?"Keeps viewers watching" is softened to "can help keep viewers watching", with no invented numbers
Any hidden conditions?The price and what's included are shown before the call to action

A pre-launch hook review

Before any ad with a curiosity or surprising hook goes live, run it through these questions. It takes five minutes and prevents most problems.

QuestionIf the answer is no
Is every factual statement in the hook true?Rewrite or remove it
Do we hold evidence for any numbers or results?Remove the number or get the evidence
Does the ad deliver what the hook promises, and soon?Move the payoff earlier or change the hook
Would a typical viewer get the result shown?Make typical results clear, or choose a different example
Is anything important only in small print?Bring it into the main message
Would the hook make sense to someone who watched the whole ad?It's bait; rewrite
Does the hook relate to the product?Viewers attracted by it won't buy

Superlatives need care. The ASA treats unqualified superlatives such as "the best" or "the fastest" as comparisons with all competitors, which need evidence. "My favourite" or "the one I use" is a personal view and safer, as long as it's genuine.

Personal stories still count. "I went from 0 to 10,000 followers in a month" in a hook is a claim viewers may take as typical. If it was exceptional, say so, or pick a more representative example.

Fear-based hooks. Hooks that suggest viewers face a penalty, a ban or a danger unless they watch need to be accurate. False alarms mislead and can breach platform rules as well as the CAP Code.

Keep evidence on file. CAP Code rule 3.7 expects you to hold documentary evidence for objective claims before the ad runs. A simple folder per campaign, with the source for each number and screenshots of results, is enough for most small advertisers.

Honest hooks test better long term. A misleading hook may win the first few seconds and then lose the viewer, the click and the customer's trust. Test honest variations against each other; see how to A/B test video ad hooks. For hook types that work without stretching the truth, see video ad structure.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches honest hooks and open loops?

The Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks and open loops at £1,000 one-time. The CAP Code and Google's ABCDs are free.

Is clickbait illegal in UK ads?

Not as such, but ads that materially mislead or exaggerate breach the CAP Code.

Can small print fix a misleading hook?

No. The ASA says qualifications can clarify but must not contradict the claim.

Do I need evidence for a claim in my hook?

Yes, for anything a viewer would take as objective, such as numbers or results.
