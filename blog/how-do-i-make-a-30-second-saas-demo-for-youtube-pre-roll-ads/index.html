How do I make a 30-second SaaS demo for YouTube pre-roll ads?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Win the first five seconds, because that is when viewers can skip. Open on the problem or the result in the first frame, name the product early, show one feature doing one job on a readable screen, and end on a single call to action. Do not try to demo the whole product in 30 seconds; show one moment of "this solves it" and send interested viewers to a page that shows more. Make it understandable with the sound off, since many viewers will not hear it.

Key takeaways

  • Google Ads Help says of skippable in-stream ads "viewers can skip the ad after 5 seconds", lists length as "no max length (<3 min recommended)", and says under cost-per-view bidding you pay "when a viewer watches 30 seconds of your video" (or the full video if shorter) or interacts, "whichever comes first" (Google Ads Help). Billing depends on bidding type, so check yours.
  • Google's ABCD-style guidance says to "jump in", "brand early, often, and richly", "feature people" and "include a call to action (CTA)", with the on-screen CTA paired to voiceover (Google Ads Help). Google's page uses the terms Attention, Branding, Connection and Direction.
  • A LinkedIn marketer writes in IAB UK that "79% of videos are watched on mute" on LinkedIn, per LinkedIn's data, and that overlays should "stick to a maximum of 14 words per scene" (IAB UK). It is a LinkedIn figure; check your own placement data.
  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded, rather than included" (Dartmouth); 30 seconds leaves no room for extras.

What 30 seconds can and cannot do

A demo in a pre-roll slot has a hard constraint: five seconds to earn attention, then 25 more to make one point. It cannot teach the product. It can show the pain, the one thing the product does about it and where to go next. Viewers who skip are not your audience for this creative; the viewers who stay are the ones you want to give a clear next step.

That is also why a demo-style ad should differ from a product tour. A tour shows features; an ad shows a before and after.

A 30-second structure

TimeBeatWhat the viewer sees and hears
0 to 5 secHookThe problem or the result, product name or logo visible
5 to 10 secSetupWho it is for, one line
10 to 22 secDemoOne feature, one task, readable screen, one cursor movement
22 to 27 secResultThe outcome on screen: the report ready, the invoice sent
27 to 30 secCTAOne action, voiceover plus on-screen text

This is a planning template from editing practice, not a research finding. Adjust to your product.

Step-by-step

  1. Pick one job. Choose the single task that best shows value to your target viewer. If you need "and" to describe it, choose one half.
  2. Write the hook as the problem. "Still copying data between three tools?" in the first two seconds, with the product visible.
  3. Brand early. Show the logo or name in the first five seconds and say it aloud, per the guidance to "brand early, often, and richly".
  4. Record the demo at high resolution. Then zoom to the active area and highlight the control; phone viewers need larger text.
  5. Keep the screen uncluttered. Hide extra tabs and notifications; use a clean account with realistic, non-personal data.
  6. Use a person where it fits. A hand on a mouse, a face reacting, or a voice you can hear can make it feel human.
  7. Show the result, not the process. Cut the steps to the minimum, show the outcome clearly.
  8. Add short captions and overlays. Keep each overlay to a few words; check the captions for names.
  9. End with one CTA. "Try it free for 14 days" only if true; "See how it works" if you are directing to a demo page. Say it and show it.
  10. Export a version for sound off. Watch it muted; if the story still makes sense, it is ready.
  11. Test variants. Run two hooks against the same demo and compare view rate and click-through. Keep other settings equal.
  12. Check claims and compliance. Any claim about performance, savings or time must be true, current and supportable; follow the platform's ad policies.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with a logo animation. The skip button appears at five seconds.
  • Showing the whole product. One job, not the full tour.
  • Tiny interface text. Zoom in.
  • Voiceover that reads the screen. Say the benefit and let the screen show the action.
  • No CTA or three CTAs. One.
  • Relying on sound. Add captions and visible cues.
  • Overclaiming. Claims need support.

Example: a project management tool

Example: a SaaS company makes a 30-second pre-roll. The first cut opens with a five-second logo sting, then a feature tour of six screens. The second cut opens with a cluttered inbox and the line "Still chasing project updates by email?" with the logo in the corner. It then shows one feature, a weekly status that writes itself, zoomed to the status panel with a highlight, and ends on a single CTA "See it in action" with a link. The team runs both against the same audience and compares view rate and clicks. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Measuring

Google's help page explains how you are billed under different bidding strategies; read it for your setup. Use your ad platform's reporting for view rate and click-through, and YouTube's retention graph if the ad also exists as a video. Compare creatives against each other under the same targeting, and allow enough impressions before drawing a conclusion.
