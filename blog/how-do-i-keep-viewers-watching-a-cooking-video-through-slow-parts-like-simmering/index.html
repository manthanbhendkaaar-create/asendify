How do I keep viewers watching a cooking video through slow parts like simmering?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Do not show the whole wait. The simmer, the proof or the reduction is slow in the kitchen but it should be short on screen: cut to the moment the food visibly changes, put the real time on screen as a small text cue, and use the gap for one useful tip or a sound cue. Give the slow step its own chapter so viewers who only want the method can jump past it. Your retention graph then tells you whether the cut was too long or too short.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's retention report says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video", and "gradual declines mean that viewers are losing interest over time" (YouTube Help). A long simmer typically shows as one or the other.
  • Chapters need a first timestamp at 00:00, at least three timestamps and a minimum of 10 seconds per chapter (YouTube Help). A named "Simmer (12 min)" chapter lets viewers choose.
  • Mayer's segmenting principle says "people learn better when the information is presented in user-paced segments, rather than in one continuous unit" (Dartmouth). A recipe is a natural set of segments.
  • Wistia's data says "engagement naturally declines after the five-minute mark" (Wistia). A recipe that crosses five minutes needs more pattern changes, not fewer.

Why slow cooking steps lose viewers

A simmer, a dough rise, a roast or a slow reduction share one problem: the action stops while the clock runs. The viewer has already seen you add the ingredients, so a static pot has nothing new to offer. The edit has to replace waiting time with something the viewer can use: the visible change, the sensory cue or the next decision.

The mistake is to treat the step as dead time to hide. Cooks watch recipe videos to learn how to judge doneness, and the slow parts are where judgement matters most. Done well, the slow part is the most useful part of the video. Done badly, it is the dip on the graph.

Step-by-step: editing a 25-minute simmer into 40 seconds

  1. Mark the three moments that matter. For a ragù, for example: when it first bubbles, when the liquid has dropped by about a third, and when it coats the spoon. These are the shots you keep.
  2. Place the time cue before the cut, not after. Show "Simmer on low, 25 minutes" while you are still on the stove, so the viewer understands the jump.
  3. Fill the gap with one decision. While time passes, tell the viewer what to check: heat level, lid position, whether to stir. One short instruction beats a tour of everything.
  4. Use sound. The gentle bubble of a simmer is part of the content. Keep a small amount of real kitchen sound under the voiceover rather than silence or constant music.
  5. Re-hook before the wait. A line such as "wait for this part, it is where most people stop too early" gives a reason to stay through the cut.
  6. Chapter it. Add "Simmer" as a named chapter. YouTube chapters each have "an individual preview", and viewers use that to jump to the method step they need.
  7. Read the graph. If the dip lands at the start of the simmer, the cut came too late or the tip was not interesting. If it lands at the end, the payoff after the wait is too slow.

Make the wait useful: ideas that work in a kitchen

  • Teach the doneness test. "The sauce should leave a clear line when you drag a spoon through the base." It is a real instruction and makes the wait feel like progress.
  • Show two stages side by side. A split screen of "10 minutes" and "25 minutes" lets the viewer see the change without watching it happen.
  • Introduce the next step's ingredient. While the sauce simmers, prep the garnish on screen. The waiting time becomes the next section's lead-in.
  • Answer the common mistake. "If it catches on the bottom, here is what to do." This is a re-hook and useful content at once.
  • Show the clock honestly. If it needs 25 minutes, say so. Viewers trust a creator who does not pretend the recipe is faster than it is.

What not to do

  • Do not leave a locked-off shot of a pot for 20 seconds with nothing changing. Viewers will check the time or leave.
  • Do not time-lapse without a reason. Fast motion is a pattern interrupt, but if you use it every time it stops working.
  • Do not hide the real time. A video that says "ready in 10 minutes" over a 45-minute braise earns negative comments and weakens trust.
  • Do not stack text and voiceover that say the same thing. Mayer's redundancy idea suggests the combined load can overwhelm learners (Dartmouth); let the voice carry the tip and the text carry the time.

Example: one braise, two edits

Example: a creator films a beef braise with a two-hour oven stage. Edit A shows 90 seconds of the pot in the oven with music. Edit B shows the door closing with a "2 hours at 150°C" text cue, cuts to a 10-second sequence of the rest-and-skim steps, then shows the finished pot with a sound cue as the lid lifts, followed by a doneness test. The creator publishes both as separate uploads on different weeks or compares the same section across two videos, and checks the graph around the oven step and the average view duration. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result; recipes and audiences differ.

How to know if you cut too much

Cutting a slow step can fail the other way: the viewer misses a cue and ruins the dish. Watch the comments for "how do I know when it is done" and the retention graph for spikes. YouTube notes spikes appear when viewers are "watching, rewatching, or sharing those parts", and can mean "your content isn't clear and your audience had to rewatch a section" (YouTube Help). A spike right after a jump cut is a sign to add a clearer cue next time.

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, pacing and pattern interrupts with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. It does not include recipes.

Should I show the whole simmer in real time?

Usually not. Show the start, the visible change and the finish, with a real time cue on screen.

Does a time-lapse help?

It can as a pattern interrupt, but pair it with a cue explaining what to watch for.

Should slow steps have chapters?

Yes. A named chapter lets viewers skip the wait or jump to it. You need at least three timestamps, starting at 00:00, each at least 10 seconds.

How do I check if I cut the wait correctly?

Read the retention graph around the step and compare with similar videos.
