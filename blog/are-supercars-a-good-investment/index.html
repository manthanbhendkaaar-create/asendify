Are supercars a good investment?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 7 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

For most buyers, a supercar is not a good investment: Hagerty's analysis found modern supercars typically lose about 6% of their value in the first year and 20% after three years. A small number of rare, limited or collectible models do rise in value, but picking them is hard, and insurance, servicing and storage costs eat into any gain. Buy one to enjoy, not to make money.

This page is for education only, not financial advice. Past price movements do not predict future ones. For advice on your own situation, speak to a regulated financial adviser.

Key takeaways

  • Most modern supercars depreciate. Hagerty found typical losses of about 6% after one year and 20% after three years.
  • Even cars that rise after launch, such as the Ferrari 488 Pista in Hagerty's study, had fallen in value by year three.
  • A few rare models and older collectible cars have risen strongly, but they are the exception.
  • Running costs continue whether the car's value goes up or down.
  • In the UK, you do not usually pay Capital Gains Tax on selling your car, unless you used it for business.

What the data says about supercar values

The best evidence comes from people who track car values for a living. Hagerty, a specialist insurer that monitors the collector car market, studied the depreciation of modern supercars using UK and US data. It looked at cars from Aston Martin, Audi, Bentley, Ferrari, Lamborghini, McLaren, Mercedes-Benz and Porsche, typically costing at least £70,000 new.

Its main finding was blunt: "all these cars depreciate". The typical loss was about 6% after one year and 20% after three years. There were differences between brands and models:

Hagerty also noted that buyers who paid over list price to jump a queue often lost more, because the car's value settled back once demand calmed down. It added that early depreciation does not mean a car will never become collectable, which is the hope many owners hold on to.

When supercars and classic cars have gone up

There are real cases of cars rising in value, mostly older or rare models. Knight Frank's Luxury Investment Index for 2026 tracks collectibles including classic cars, art, watches and wine. It reports that:

  • The overall index closed 2025 down 0.4%, after falls in 2023 and 2024.
  • Over the past decade, the index rose 38.6%.
  • A 1995 Ferrari F50 sold for US$9.2 million at Monterey Car Week.
  • Cars from the late 1980s to the early 2000s are now entering collectible territory, often outperforming traditional classics.

Two points matter here. First, the index covers a mix of luxury assets, so its decade figure is not a return on supercars alone. Second, cars like the F50 were rare, limited models bought decades ago. A typical new or nearly new supercar bought today is in a very different position.

The costs that work against you

An investment that costs money every year has to rise by at least that much just to break even. Supercars carry heavy holding costs:

  • Insurance, which depends on you, the car and where you keep it.
  • Servicing and repairs, which stay expensive however old the car is. As Auto Express notes in its cheap supercars guide, "A £100k car picked up for £30k still has the maintenance costs of a £100k vehicle."
  • Storage, especially if you want to keep a collectible car in top condition.
  • Vehicle tax. UK cars registered from April 2017 with a list price over £40,000 pay an extra £440 a year for five years, according to the GOV.UK vehicle tax rate tables.

To keep value, many collectible cars also need low mileage, which means you drive them less. That defeats much of the point of owning one.

The one tax advantage

There is one thing in a car's favour in the UK. GOV.UK's guide to what you pay Capital Gains Tax on lists most personal possessions worth £6,000 or more as chargeable, "apart from your car". So if a car you own privately does rise in value, you would not usually pay Capital Gains Tax on the gain. The exemption does not apply if you have used the car for business. Tax rules change, so check the current position or speak to a tax adviser.

For the car to break even, its value would need to rise by more than £24,420 over three years, rather than fall by £30,000. That would mean a swing of over £54,000 against the typical trend. It can happen with the right rare car, but it is not something to plan on. Daniel decides to treat the car as a cost he can afford, with a set budget, rather than an investment.

How to buy a supercar without losing more than you need to

If you want a supercar mainly to enjoy it, you can still limit the loss:

  1. Buy used. The first owner usually takes the steepest depreciation.
  2. Avoid paying over list price for a hyped new model; Hagerty found those buyers often lost the most.
  3. Keep a full service history, which supports resale value.
  4. Know your full yearly cost before you buy, including insurance and a repair fund.
  5. Set a budget for the loss, as you would for any hobby.

FAQ

Do supercars go up in value?

Most do not. Hagerty found modern supercars typically lose about 6% in the first year and 20% after three years. Some rare and limited models have risen, but they are exceptions.

Which supercars hold their value best?

In Hagerty's study, Ferrari and Lamborghini depreciated less over three years than German brands, while Aston Martin and McLaren lost a fifth to a quarter. Rarity and condition matter more than brand alone.

Is a classic car a better investment than a new supercar?

Some classic cars have risen strongly, and Knight Frank notes that cars from the late 1980s to early 2000s are entering collectible territory. But the overall collectibles index fell slightly in 2025, and classics have high storage and care costs.

Do I pay tax if I sell my car for a profit?

In the UK, GOV.UK says your car is not usually subject to Capital Gains Tax, unless you used it for business. Check current rules or ask a tax adviser.

Should I buy a supercar as an investment?

For most people, no. Buy one if you will enjoy it and can afford the full cost, including the value it is likely to lose.
