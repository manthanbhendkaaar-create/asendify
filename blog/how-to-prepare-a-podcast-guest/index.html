How do you prepare a podcast guest so the episode holds attention?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Prepare a podcast guest by deciding the one central question the episode answers, researching the guest, and running a short pre-interview to find their two or three best stories. Then build your questions in an order that leads to those stories, and tell the guest what to expect so they relax. Most flat episodes were lost before recording, not in the edit.

Key takeaways

  • Start with the central question: what will listeners know at the end that they didn't at the start?
  • Research the guest so you don't spend recording time on facts you could have read.
  • A 15-minute pre-interview finds the stories worth building the episode around.
  • Order your questions so each answer sets up the next, and save a strong story for the second half.
  • Brief the guest on length, format and tech. A relaxed guest gives better, shorter answers.

Why preparation decides retention

Listeners leave when nothing is happening: long background answers, repeated points, or a conversation without direction. Spotify notes that "listeners have a tendency to drop off early" and that "getting into the heart of your episode as quickly as you can is a great way to keep up retention" (Spotify for Creators). You can only get to the heart quickly if you know where it is before you press record.

NPR's show editor checklist starts in the same place: "What's the focus, the central question you are trying to answer?" It also asks whether the questions "build in a clear sequence" and whether the finished conversation answers that central question (NPR Training).

1. Decide the central question

Write one sentence: "By the end, listeners will know how she got her first 100 customers without ads." Every question you plan should move towards that answer or explain why it matters. The University of Glasgow's interviewing guide makes the same point: be clear about your objectives so you can keep questions relevant and steer back if the conversation drifts (University of Glasgow).

2. Research the guest

Read their website, recent posts and any earlier interviews. Note what they've already said many times. If every podcast has asked about their origin story, listeners who follow them have heard it. Ask about something newer.

3. Run a short pre-interview

A quick call or voice note exchange of around 15 minutes. You're listening for stories with a clear turn: a problem, a decision, a result. NPR's tip is useful here: vet the guest beforehand, but "don't ask the questions the same way the host will". If the guest tells their best story in full on the pre-call, it often comes out flatter on the recording.

4. Build the running order

Put a strong, specific moment early. Plant a question you'll answer later. Save one of the best stories for after the halfway point, where attention usually sags. Keep the number of questions suited to the length: a 40-minute episode rarely needs more than eight to ten main questions with follow-ups.

5. Brief the guest

Send a short email: the episode's angle, the rough length, whether it's video, that you'll edit for time, and two or three topics you'll cover. Don't send the full question list unless they need it; scripted answers sound scripted.

6. Check the tech

Test levels and connection a few minutes before. NPR's list includes exploring all connection options for the best audio quality. Poor audio makes even a great story hard to stay with.

Example: prepping a 40-minute founder interview

Example (a hypothetical guest, to show the method): a café owner who opened a second site that failed.

  • Central question: "What did she get wrong about the second café, and what would she do differently?"
  • Research: her local newspaper interview covers the opening; nobody has asked about the closure.
  • Pre-interview finds: the landlord dispute, the week she ran both sites alone, and the day she decided to close.
  • Running order: open with a 20-second clip of her saying "I knew on day three it was wrong"; ask about the first café briefly; then the second-site decision; the landlord dispute at about minute 15; the week alone at about minute 25; the closure decision near the end, paying off the "day three" line from the open.
  • Brief: "About 40 minutes, video, we'll edit for time, we'll cover the second site honestly."

Mistakes to avoid

  • No central question. The conversation wanders and the edit can't save it.
  • Over-briefing. Sending every question invites rehearsed answers.
  • Spending the first ten minutes on background. Background can be one line in your intro.
  • Not listening. Prepared questions are a map, not a script. Follow the better story when it appears.
  • Forgetting the guest's nerves. A short chat before recording helps people relax and talk naturally.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches podcast interview structure?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers open loops, re-hooks and payoffs for podcasts, clips and YouTube.

Should I send my guest the questions in advance?

Send the topics and the angle, not the full list. Full lists tend to produce rehearsed answers.

How long should a pre-interview be?

Around 15 minutes is usually enough to find the stories worth building around.

What if the guest is nervous?

Chat for a few minutes before recording, explain that you'll edit, and start with an easy, specific question.

How many questions should I prepare?

For a 40-minute episode, eight to ten main questions plus follow-ups is a sensible range.

What if the guest goes off topic?

Let a good story run. If it isn't going anywhere, bring them back with a question tied to your central question.
