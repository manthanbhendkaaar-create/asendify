What happens if you get a copyright strike on YouTube?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 19 November 2026 · Last updated 19 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

When you get a copyright strike, the video is removed because the owner sent a valid legal removal request. One or two strikes do not end your channel, but three can terminate your account and any linked channels. A strike expires 90 days after it was applied if you complete YouTube's Copyright School and have fewer than three; you can also ask for a retraction or file a counter notification.

Key takeaways

  • A strike comes from a copyright removal request, a formal legal process; a Content ID claim usually does not cause one.
  • Three strikes can terminate your account and linked channels, and you cannot create new channels.
  • A strike on an active live stream restricts live streaming for 7 days; a second extends it to 14 days.
  • Copyright School has four questions and only needs completing once; strikes then expire after 90 days if you have fewer than three.
  • Deleting the video does not remove a strike, except during a scheduled removal's 7-day window.

What a strike is

YouTube's copyright strike basics say a strike means a legal copyright removal request led to your content being removed. YouTube reviews valid requests and must remove the content to comply with copyright law.

The copyright removal requests page explains that the owner, or an authorised representative such as a lawyer, can submit a request. Owners can schedule a removal to take effect in 7 days, which gives the uploader time to remove the content and avoid a strike. Removed videos display the claimant's name in place of the video.

Three ways to resolve a strike

  1. Wait it out. Complete Copyright School (four questions, once only) and the strike expires after 90 days, provided you have fewer than three.
  2. Ask for a retraction. Contact the person who filed the request and ask them to withdraw it, for example if you have since agreed a licence.
  3. File a counter notification. Only if you believe the removal was a mistake or your use is covered by an exception such as fair use. This is a legal process, so take it seriously.

The myths that lead to strikes

YouTube's page on common copyright myths lists the beliefs that most often get creators into trouble:

MythYouTube's answer
"I gave credit, so it's fine"Crediting the owner doesn't give you the rights
"It's non-profit or educational"Not making money doesn't prevent copyright issues; it's only one fair-use factor
"No copyright infringement intended"Disclaimers don't give you the right to use content
"Other creators do it"That doesn't mean you have the rights
"I bought the song or DVD"Buying content doesn't give you upload rights
"I recorded it from TV or radio"Recording doesn't make you the rights owner
"It's only a few seconds"Any amount used without permission may cause issues
"I changed it into something new"Parodies, covers and translations usually still need permission

Worked example: a channel with one strike

Example: a film-review channel with 30,000 subscribers uploads a review containing a 3-minute uncut trailer with its original music. The studio files a removal request; the video is removed and the channel gets its first strike.

  • Day 1: the creator completes Copyright School.
  • Day 2: they re-edit the review with short, purposeful clips under their own commentary, music from the Audio Library, and re-upload it.
  • Day 3: they audit the 10 most-viewed older videos for long, uncut clips and trim two of them.
  • Day 91: the strike expires, as the channel has fewer than three.

The review itself was never the problem; the long, uncommented clip was. Whether a shorter clip counts as fair use is still a legal judgement, so the creator keeps clips brief and clearly part of the commentary. The scenario is illustrative.

How to protect your channel

  • Use music from YouTube's Audio Library or with a licence you can show.
  • Keep a record of every licence and its terms.
  • Do not rely on credit lines or disclaimers; YouTube says neither gives you rights.
  • Check older videos if your channel has grown; a small video can become a target once it gets views.
  • If you receive a scheduled removal, act inside the 7 days.
  • Ask editors and collaborators to tell you where every clip and track came from, so you can answer a claim or request quickly.

FAQ

Is there a free tool that reviews my channel and tells me what to fix this week?

Yes. Asendify's free YouTube Channel Roast scores your channel's packaging out of 100 and gives three fixes, with no sign-up. It does not check copyright.

How long does a copyright strike last?

It expires 90 days after it was applied if you complete Copyright School and have fewer than three strikes.

Does deleting the video remove the strike?

No, except for scheduled removals, where deleting the content within the 7-day window avoids the strike.

Can I still upload with one strike?

YouTube's strike page describes removal of the content and, for strikes on live streams, a 7-day then 14-day live streaming limit; three strikes can terminate the channel. Check YouTube Studio for any restrictions shown on your channel.

Is a Content ID claim the same as a strike?

No. A claim usually doesn't cause a strike, but disputing one without a valid reason can lead to a removal request and a strike.
