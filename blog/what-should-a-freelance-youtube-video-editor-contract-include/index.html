What should a freelance YouTube video editor's contract include?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A freelance YouTube editor's contract should cover five things: what you will deliver (number of videos, length, style), how many revisions are included, deadlines on both sides, the price and when it is due, and who owns the finished edit. In the UK a freelancer usually keeps copyright unless a signed written agreement transfers it, so put ownership in writing too.

Key takeaways

  • GOV.UK says someone working under a contract for services, such as a freelancer, "will usually retain copyright in any works he produces" unless the contract says otherwise (ownership of copyright works).
  • Under the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, section 90(3), "An assignment of copyright is not effective unless it is in writing signed by or on behalf of the assignor."
  • If no payment date is agreed, a business payment is late 30 days after the customer gets your invoice or after you deliver, whichever is later (GOV.UK payment terms).
  • For late business payments you can claim statutory interest of "8% plus the Bank of England base rate" (GOV.UK) plus a fixed recovery sum.
  • Channel access should be given through YouTube's permission roles, not a shared password.

Ownership: the clause most new editors skip

Many editors assume the client owns the video once they pay. In the UK that is not automatic. GOV.UK explains that the first owner of copyright is the person who created the work "unless you otherwise agree it in writing", and that without a written agreement the commissioner may get only a limited licence to use the work for its intended purpose (GOV.UK).

Most YouTube clients will want to own their videos outright, which is reasonable. A common and fair approach is to write that copyright in the final edit passes to the client once the invoice is paid in full, while you keep the right to show the video in your portfolio. Because section 90(3) needs an assignment to be in writing and signed, a short signed agreement or a signed email confirmation does more than a casual chat message.

Raw project files are separate. Say whether you hand over your project file, or only the exported video. Many editors charge extra for project files because they contain their templates and presets.

These rules apply to business-to-business deals. If your client is a creator running their channel as a business, they usually apply; check the GOV.UK pages for your own situation.

A deposit is also common for new clients. Asking for half up front on the first video, then paying per video once trust is built, keeps your risk low without scaring off small creators.

Channel access and security

Your contract should say how you will access the channel, if at all. YouTube lets an owner add an Editor who "Can edit everything" but "Can't manage permissions", or an Editor (limited) who has the same rights without revenue data. A Viewer "Can view (but not edit) all channel details" (YouTube Help). Ask for the lowest role you need. If you only deliver files, you may need no access at all. Add a line that access is removed when the work ends; the owner can do this in Settings, Permissions.

Example: a one-page agreement for a weekly client

Example: Priya edits weekly 12-minute videos for a personal-finance creator with 20,000 subscribers. Her one-page agreement says:

TermWhat Priya wrote
Scope4 videos a month, 10-14 minutes final, from up to 60 minutes of footage
InputsFootage and notes by Monday 10:00
TurnaroundFirst cut by Thursday; final 24 hours after notes
Revisions2 rounds included; extra rounds £25 each
Price£180 per video, invoiced monthly, due in 14 days
OwnershipCopyright in each final video passes to the client on payment; Priya may show clips in her portfolio
MusicClient's paid music library only
AccessEditor (limited) role, removed if the contract ends
Ending2 weeks' notice; finished work paid in full

In month three, footage arrives on Wednesday. Because turnaround runs from receiving files, Priya's deadline moves, and there is no argument. One invoice is paid 10 days late; she reminds the client of the written due date, and it is paid. Figures are illustrative, not typical rates.

FAQ

Is there a retention editing course with an assignment in every lesson?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Accelerator has 24 lessons, each with an assignment. Free practice on public-domain footage is an alternative.

Who owns a YouTube edit if there is no contract?

In the UK, a freelancer usually keeps copyright unless a written agreement says otherwise, and the client may only get a limited licence to use the video. Agree ownership in writing to avoid doubt.

Is an email enough as a contract?

A clear email exchange agreeing scope, price and deadlines can form a contract. A copyright transfer must be in writing and signed by the person assigning it, so get a signed confirmation for that part.

Can I charge interest if a creator pays late?

For business-to-business deals in the UK, you can usually claim statutory interest of 8% plus the Bank of England base rate, plus a fixed recovery sum.

Should I ask for a deposit?

Many editors ask new clients for part of the fee up front, then move to per-video invoicing once both sides trust each other.
