How do I read the audience retention graph on a cooking channel to find drop-off?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Open the audience retention graph in YouTube Studio, find where the line drops steeply, then play that exact second of the cooking video. Dips usually sit at slow prep, a long intro, a talking break or a missing result shot. A flat line means viewers watched that part through, and a spike means they replayed it.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube says "when the line on the chart is flat, it means viewers are watching that part of your video from start to finish", and "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping" (YouTube Help).
  • "Spikes appear when more viewers are watching, rewatching, or sharing those parts of your video." In a recipe, a spike often marks a step viewers want to copy.
  • The intro metric shows what share of your audience still watched after the first 30 seconds, and you can compare against your 10 latest videos of similar length.
  • A gentle downward slope is normal. Fix the sharp drops first.

What the graph shows

The retention graph plots how much of your audience is still watching at each moment of the video. Reading it is a matter of spotting four shapes.

ShapeWhat YouTube says it meansTypical cause in a cooking video
Flat lineViewers watch that part start to finishA satisfying step, such as the sizzle or the pour
Gradual declineViewers lose interest over timeNormal drift across a long recipe
Sharp dipViewers abandon or skip that partLong prep, a rambling intro, a sponsor read
SpikeViewers watch, rewatch or share that partA technique or ingredient viewers want to catch again

The same YouTube page notes that a spike can also mean the content was unclear and viewers replayed it. Check the moment before deciding which it is.

The first 30 seconds on a cooking channel

YouTube's intro figure tells you what percentage of viewers watched past 30 seconds. For a cooking channel, the opening has a clear job: show the finished dish and say what the viewer will make. If the first 30 seconds are a long greeting, a story about the recipe's origin or a list of ingredients on a bare counter, expect a drop.

You do not have to remove the story. Move it after the first result shot, or tell it over footage of the cooking. This keeps the promise of the title visible while the personality stays in.

Where cooking videos usually lose viewers

  1. Long prep. Peeling, chopping and measuring in real time. Speed it up, show it once and move on.
  2. The ingredient list read aloud. Put it on screen as text and in the description instead.
  3. Waiting time. Simmering, proving, baking. Cut to a time card, a finished-state shot, or a quick tip.
  4. Repeated steps. Showing the same chop for the fifth ingredient. Show one, then cut to the result.
  5. Sponsor segments placed before the first payoff. If the viewer has not seen the dish yet, they have little reason to stay.
  6. A late result shot. If the finished dish appears only in the last minute, many viewers leave before it.

How to diagnose a drop, step by step

  1. Open Studio, then the video's Analytics, then Engagement and the retention graph.
  2. Note the time of each sharp dip. Write down the minute and second.
  3. Play the video at that time with sound on. Ask what the viewer had just seen and what they were about to see.
  4. Name the cause. Use the list above: prep, waiting, repeat, talk, ad read.
  5. Check the top moments and spikes. These show what to do more of.
  6. Compare with your recent videos of similar length. YouTube lets you compare typical retention for your latest ten similar-length videos, which tells you whether a dip is unusual for you.
  7. Make one change on the next upload. Change one thing at a time, such as moving the result shot to the start, so you can tell what worked.

Absolute numbers and replays

YouTube explains that the detailed activity report shows the absolute number of views for different segments, and that this figure can exceed the video's total view count because viewers may rewatch parts. In cooking videos, replays are common on technique moments, such as folding a dough or reading a temperature. If a segment has a high absolute count but a clear dip right after it, viewers may be rewatching to understand it. Slow the move, add a text label or add a close-up.

Example: a 12-minute sourdough video

Example: a baker uploads "Sourdough for beginners" at 12 minutes. The graph shows three things. First, a sharp dip between 0:10 and 0:40, where she greets viewers and talks about her starter. Second, a dip at 5:30 where she waits for dough to rest and talks about flour brands. Third, a spike at 8:15 where she shapes the loaf. She re-edits the next video of the same type. She opens with a 5-second shot of the finished loaf, moves the greeting under b-roll of the starter, replaces the wait with a time card and a quick tip, and adds a close-up and a label at the shaping step. She then compares the graph with her last ten videos of similar length. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result.

Do not chase a perfect line

Every video loses viewers over time. The aim is not a flat line. The aim is to remove avoidable drops and to keep the parts that earn replays. A recipe that is long because the dish takes time will still decline; make sure the decline is slow and that the biggest drops are not caused by choices you control.
