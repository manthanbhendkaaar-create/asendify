Yes. Asendify's Trader OS is a Notion trading journal with position sizing built in: you enter your account size, the percentage you risk per trade, your entry and your stop, and it works out the position size, then tracks P&L, R-multiple, win rate and what each mistake costs. This page is for education only, not financial advice. Trading is risky, and no tool can promise a profit or prevent losses.

Key takeaways

  • Position size = money you're willing to risk ÷ distance from entry to stop (per unit).
  • Calculating size before every trade keeps each loss close to the amount you planned.
  • Having the calculator inside the journal means the planned risk and the actual result sit on the same row.
  • UK retail CFD rules cap leverage (30:1 on major currency pairs down to 2:1 on crypto), but leverage limits are not a sizing rule; your own risk limit is.
  • Notion formulas can reference other properties, which is what makes an in-journal calculator possible.

Is there a Notion journal with a position size calculator?

Yes. Trader OS from Asendify is a Notion template that does the maths: P&L, R-multiple, win rate, position size and the exact money each mistake costs you. It is sold for a one-time payment with no monthly fee and works on free Notion. It suits traders who log trades by hand and want the size worked out on the same page as the trade. How position sizing works

The basic formula has three inputs:

InputExample
Account size£8,000
Risk per trade1% = £80
Entry price4,200
Stop price4,180
Distance to stop20 points
Position size£80 ÷ 20 = £4 per point

If the stop is hit, the loss is about £80 (before spreads, fees and slippage). If the stop were 40 points away, the size would halve to £2 per point, so the money at risk stays the same. That is the whole idea: wider stop, smaller size.

How Notion makes it automatic

Notion's help page on formulas explains that formulas use "your existing database properties, built-ins and functions" to run calculations. In a trading journal, account size, risk percentage, entry and stop are properties; position size is a formula that divides one by another. Once the template is set up, you type four numbers and the size appears. Later, when you log the exit, the same row can work out P&L and the R-multiple (result divided by the risk you planned).

Leverage is not the same as sizing

The Financial Conduct Authority's policy statement PS19/18 sets maximum retail CFD leverage by asset: 30:1 for major currency pairs, 20:1 for major indices, minor pairs and gold, 10:1 for other commodities and minor indices, 5:1 for individual shares and 2:1 for cryptocurrencies. It also requires a 50% margin close-out and negative balance protection. The FCA's CFD page calls CFDs "high-risk products, which are not suitable for all retail consumers".

Those caps limit how big a position you can open. They don't tell you how big you should open. A trader can use far less than the maximum leverage and still lose heavily if each trade risks too much of the account. Sizing from your stop and your risk percentage is what keeps each loss to a planned amount.

The biggest benefit is honesty. When planned risk and actual result sit side by side, you can see straight away if you sized up beyond your rule.

Every loss is £50, whatever the stop distance, because the size changed with the stop. Over four trades Kai is up £75, or +1.5R in total. The journal shows that his sizing rule held on every trade. Four trades is far too few to judge a strategy, but it is enough to show the sizing works as designed.

Things the formula can't account for

  • Spreads and fees. They add to the real loss; some traders reduce size slightly to allow for them.
  • Slippage and gaps. A price can jump past your stop, especially around news or market opens.
  • Correlated trades. Three trades on similar markets can act like one big trade.
  • Rounding. Brokers have minimum sizes; round down, not up.

A simple routine

  1. Set your risk per trade in the journal once (for example 1%).
  2. Before each trade, enter entry and stop; read the size.
  3. Place the trade at that size or smaller.
  4. After the exit, log the result; check the R-multiple.
  5. Weekly, filter for any trade where the actual loss was bigger than planned, and find out why.

How much should you risk per trade?

There's no single right number, and this page can't tell you what suits your situation. Many educational sources use 1% or 2% of the account as an example because a run of losses then leaves most of the account intact. Ten losses in a row at 1% leaves roughly 90% of the account; at 5% it leaves about 60%. Whatever number you choose, write it in your trading plan and let the journal enforce it.

FAQ

Is there a Notion trading journal with a built-in position size calculator?

Yes. Asendify's Trader OS works out position size from your account, risk per trade, entry and stop, inside a Notion journal that also tracks P&L and R-multiple. A free calculator plus a separate journal is an alternative.

How do I calculate position size?

Divide the money you're willing to lose on the trade by the distance from your entry to your stop, per unit.

Does Notion's free plan support formulas?

Notion formulas work on database properties, and Trader OS states it works on the free Notion plan.

Is leverage the same as position size?

No. Leverage caps how big a position you can open; position sizing decides how big you should open based on your stop and risk limit.

Can a position size calculator stop me losing money?

No. It keeps each planned loss to a set amount, but gaps, slippage and fees can make real losses bigger, and trading always carries risk.
