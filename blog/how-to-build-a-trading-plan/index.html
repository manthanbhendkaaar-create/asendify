How do I build a trading plan?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 7 min read · Published 13 November 2026 · Last updated 13 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

To build a trading plan, write down six things before you trade: your goal and available time, your markets and timeframes, the exact setups you will enter, your risk per trade, your exit rules, and how you will review results. Keep it to one or two pages, test it on paper first, and change it only at a weekly review.

This page is for education only, not financial advice. Trading is risky and you can lose some or all of the money you put in.

Key takeaways

  • A trading plan is a written set of rules that decides your trades before the market opens, so you are not deciding in the moment.
  • The core parts are: goal, markets, setups, risk per trade, exits, and a review routine.
  • Risk rules come first. Decide the most you will lose on one trade and one week before you think about profit.
  • Write every rule so a stranger could check whether you followed it. "Enter on a strong move" is not a rule; "enter on a close above the prior day's high" is.
  • Review the plan weekly against a journal, and only change one rule at a time.

What a trading plan is, and why you need one

The Corporate Finance Institute defines a trading plan as a complete set of rules based on research that covers your objectives, time and risk tolerance. It lists the underlying elements as the conditions for buying and selling, the size of each position, how you manage positions and the types of securities you trade, and notes that traders should not risk capital until a plan has been developed.

The reason is simple. Fast trading invites fast decisions. FINRA's guide to frequent intraday trading warns that it "comes with risks—particularly if you're trading on margin—including losing some or all of your investment." A written plan does not remove that risk, but it stops you adding to it with impulsive trades.

The six parts of a trading plan

PartThe question it answersExample rule
1. Goal and timeWhy am I trading, and how many hours a week can I give it?"Swing trade part-time; 30 minutes each evening, 1 hour at the weekend."
2. Markets and timeframesWhat will I trade, and on which charts?"Large-cap UK and US shares only; daily chart for direction, 4-hour for entries."
3. SetupsWhat exactly must I see before I enter?"Pullback to the 20-day moving average in an uptrend, then a close above the prior candle's high."
4. Risk per tradeHow much can I lose if I am wrong?"Risk 1% of the account per trade; stop goes below the pullback low."
5. ExitsWhen do I take profit or cut a loss?"Take half at 2R, trail the rest under the prior swing low; exit at the stop with no exceptions."
6. ReviewHow will I check the plan is working?"Log every trade the same day; review every Sunday; change at most one rule a month."

1. Goal and time

Start with honesty about time. A plan built for someone watching screens all day will fail for someone with a full-time job. Decide whether you are day trading, swing trading (holding for days or weeks) or position trading, and match it to the hours you really have.

2. Markets and timeframes

Pick a small number of markets you can follow properly. Each market behaves differently, trades at different hours and carries different costs. If you trade leveraged products such as contracts for difference, read the FCA's page on CFD rules for retail clients: firms must limit leverage to between 30:1 and 2:1 depending on the asset, and show a risk warning with the percentage of their retail accounts that lose money. Those warnings are worth reading before you choose a market.

3. Setups

A setup is the exact picture that must appear before you enter. Write it as a checklist with yes/no items, such as trend direction, the level price must reach, and the trigger candle. Two or three well-defined setups are easier to test and follow than ten loose ones.

4. Risk per trade

This is the part that keeps you in the game. Choose a fixed percentage of your account to risk on each trade, then work out the position size from the distance between your entry and your stop. Many educators suggest 1–2% as a starting range; our page on how much to risk per trade explains the trade-offs. Add a daily or weekly loss limit too, such as "stop for the week after losing 3%".

5. Exits

Every trade needs a planned exit before you enter. That means a stop-loss level and a profit plan. Know how your orders work: Investor.gov explains that a stop order becomes a market order once the stop price is reached, and that the price you get may differ from the stop price, especially in a fast-moving market. Build that slippage into your expectations.

6. Review

A plan without a review is a wish list. Log each trade with the setup, entry, stop, exit, result in R (multiples of the risk you took) and whether you followed your rules. Once a week, look at which setups made money and which rules you broke. Our guide to calculating expectancy with R-multiples shows how to tell whether a setup has an edge over time.

Example: a one-page plan for a part-time swing trader

Example: Priya has a £5,000 account and about five hours a week. Her first written plan looks like this.

RulePriya's version
GoalLearn to swing trade without losing more than 10% of the account in the first six months
Markets20 large-cap UK shares she knows well
SetupDaily uptrend (price above the 50-day moving average); pullback to the 20-day; enter on a close above the prior day's high
Risk per trade1% of the account = £50
Position sizeIf entry is 400p and stop is 380p, risk per share is 20p, so £50 ÷ £0.20 = 250 shares
ExitsStop at 380p; take half at 440p (2R); trail the rest under each new higher low
Loss limitsStop for the week after three losing trades or £150 lost
ReviewLog every trade that evening; review each Sunday for 30 minutes

After 20 trades she can see that 12 were losses of about 1R each and 8 were winners averaging 2.2R. That is +17.6R from winners and −12R from losers, so +5.6R in total, or about £280 on £50 risk per trade. The numbers are illustrative, but the method is the point: the plan tells her what to do, and the review tells her whether it works.

You can check the share count yourself with the free position size calculator, which takes account size, risk %, entry and stop.

How to test a new plan before using real money

  1. Backtest it. Go back through charts and mark every time your setup appeared. Record what would have happened. Our guide on how to backtest a trading strategy walks through the steps.
  2. Paper trade it. Follow the plan in real time with no money at risk for at least a few weeks.
  3. Start small. When you go live, risk less than your plan's normal amount until you have 20–30 trades logged.
  4. Review weekly. Compare what you did with what the plan said.

Common mistakes when writing a trading plan

  • Vague rules. If you cannot say yes or no to a rule, it will bend under pressure.
  • Too many setups. You will never collect enough trades on each to know which works.
  • No loss limits. One bad week without a limit can undo months of careful work.
  • Changing rules after every loss. A losing trade is not proof the plan is broken. Wait for a sample of trades.
  • No record. Without a journal, you are reviewing your memory, not your trades.

Keeping the plan and the record together

Many traders keep the plan in one document and the journal in another, then never compare them. It helps to keep both in one place, with the setup rules next to the trades that used them. FAQ

How long should a trading plan be?

One or two pages is enough for most people. If it is too long to read before each session, you will stop reading it.

What should be in a trading plan for beginners?

Your goal and available time, the markets you will trade, one or two setups, a fixed risk per trade, exit rules and a weekly review. Beginners should also add a daily or weekly loss limit.

How often should I change my trading plan?

Review it weekly but change it rarely, and one rule at a time. Wait until you have enough trades, often 20 or more on a setup, before deciding it does not work.

Do I need a trading plan if I only trade occasionally?

Yes. Occasional traders often make bigger mistakes because each trade feels more important. A short plan with risk and exit rules helps.

Is a trading plan the same as a trading strategy?

No. A strategy is the method for finding trades. A plan includes the strategy plus your risk rules, exits, schedule and review routine.

Will a trading plan make me profitable?

No plan can promise profit. It makes your decisions consistent and measurable, so you can see what works and stop what does not.
