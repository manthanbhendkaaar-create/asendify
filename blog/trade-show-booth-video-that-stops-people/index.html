How do you make a trade show booth video that stops people walking past?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A booth video that stops people is designed for how it is seen: silently, from several metres away, for a few seconds, by people walking past. Use big text and strong motion from the first frame, one message per scene, and a short loop (often 30 to 90 seconds) so anyone glancing at any moment sees something clear. Finish the loop with a prompt that gives people a reason to step in, such as a live demo time.

Key takeaways

  • On a stand, there is no start: people join the loop at random. Every few seconds must work on its own.
  • Most halls are loud, so design for no sound. Text and visuals must carry the message.
  • Google's ABCDs advise keeping visuals "bright and high-contrast" and messaging "focused and simple"; that matters even more on a screen across an aisle.
  • Cvent lists trade shows among the places event promos are used, alongside event pages, emails and sales materials.
  • Measure by conversations started, not views. Ask staff to note what people mention.

Why booth video is different

Online, a viewer starts at 0:00 and you can build a cold open. On a stand, people catch your video halfway through, from an angle, while walking. That changes the rules:

Online videoBooth video
Starts at 0:00Joined at a random point
Sound usually availableHall noise; assume silent
Watched at arm's lengthSeen from 2-10 metres
Viewer chose to clickViewer is walking past
One cold openEvery scene needs its own hook

The Retention Editing Playbook's cold open rule, start on the most interesting thing, becomes "every scene starts on something interesting". Think of the loop as a chain of five-second cold opens.

Design rules that work from across an aisle

  • Huge text, few words. Three to six words per screen. If you can't read it from the back of your own office, it's too small.
  • High contrast. Google's ABCDs of effective video ads say "Keep visuals bright and high-contrast"; hall lighting washes screens out.
  • Motion in the first frame of each scene. A product moving, a hand doing something, a number counting up. Static slides look like a screensaver.
  • One message per scene. The ABCDs also say "Avoid doing too much in your ad. Keep messaging and language, focused and simple."
  • People and products in action. "Feature people to help you tell a better product story," Google says. Show the product being used, not a logo spinning.
  • Brand on screen throughout. Because people arrive at random, a small, steady logo or product name in a corner helps.

A 60-second loop structure

  1. 0-5 s: the problem, in five words, over a striking visual ("Your warehouse loses 1 in 20 orders"; only use a number you can support).
  2. 5-15 s: the product solving it, shown, not described.
  3. 15-25 s: a single proof point: a real customer logo (with permission) or a measured result.
  4. 25-35 s: a second use case.
  5. 35-45 s: a short, real customer clip with captions.
  6. 45-55 s: "Live demo every hour on the hour" or "Ask us to scan your label".
  7. 55-60 s: brand card, then loop to the start.

Each block makes sense alone, so a passer-by who catches 10 seconds still gets one clear idea.

Sound, if you use it

If your stand has a quiet area, a short sound loop can draw people in, but many shows have rules on volume, and constant audio exhausts your staff. Captions are essential either way. The W3C's captions guidance notes that open captions are always displayed, which suits a screen nobody can control.

Example: a booth loop for a SaaS company

Example: a software company selling scheduling tools for clinics has a 3 x 3 metre stand with a 55-inch landscape screen.

  • They cut a 45-second loop from their existing product demo and customer interview footage.
  • Every scene starts with motion: a calendar filling up, a phone buzzing with a reminder, a receptionist smiling.
  • Text is set at a size readable from about 5 metres, with no more than five words per screen.
  • At 30 seconds, a captioned clip of a real clinic manager saying what changed for them (consent in writing).
  • The loop ends with "Live demo at :00 and :30" every 45 seconds.

Staff note that visitors often mention the "calendar filling up" shot when they walk in, so the team moves it to the start of the next show's loop.

Make it once, use it elsewhere

Cvent's event video guide notes that event promos appear at trade shows, in emails and in sales materials. Your booth loop can become a silent social clip with small changes, and footage from the stand (with visitors' permission) can feed your post-show recap.

Quick checklist before the show

  • Watch the loop from five metres away on the actual screen, with the sound off. Can you read every line?
  • Start the loop at three random points. Does each one show a clear idea within five seconds?
  • Check the brand name is visible on screen most of the time, not only at the end.
  • Make sure every number, logo and customer quote is accurate and approved.
  • Export a version with burned-in captions for any speech.
  • Bring the file on two USB sticks and test playback on the stand screen before doors open.
  • Brief staff on what the loop shows, so they can start conversations from it.
  • After the show, note which scenes visitors mentioned and move those to the start next time.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches pattern interrupts and cold opens you can apply to a loop?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches cold opens, pattern interrupts and pacing. It is written for YouTube; Google's free ABCDs are a fair alternative.

How long should a booth video loop be?

Short enough that someone waiting nearby sees the whole thing: often 30 to 90 seconds.

Should my booth video have sound?

Assume not. Most halls are loud, and many shows limit volume. Use captions and text.

Can I use my website promo video on the stand?

Usually only after re-editing: bigger text, no reliance on voiceover, and every scene readable on its own.

Should I show prices on the booth screen?

Only if they are current and simple. Out-of-date prices cause awkward conversations.

How do I know if the video works?

Ask staff to track how many visitors mention something from the screen, and test changes between shows.
