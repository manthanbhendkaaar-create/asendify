Animated or live-action: which holds attention better in a SaaS explainer?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Neither format wins by default. Animation tends to suit software with abstract ideas (data flows, integrations, permissions) because it can show what a camera cannot, while live action tends to suit products where trust, people or a physical context matter. What holds attention is the same in both: a clear first problem, one idea per scene, and a show-it-working moment early. If you cannot decide, test both short cuts against your own retention graph rather than trusting a rule.

Key takeaways

  • Google's guidance on effective video ads says to "jump in" and "get to the heart of the story faster", and to "feature people to help you tell a better product story" (Google Ads Help). Both are choices about format as much as script.
  • Wistia's data says videos meant to "educate or explain" suit "1 to 5 minutes", and that "engagement naturally declines after the five-minute mark" (Wistia). Format cannot rescue an explainer that runs long.
  • Mayer's modality idea says "people learn better when there is voiceover with images or animation, instead of on-screen text with images or animation", and coherence says to exclude extraneous content (Dartmouth). Both formats should follow it.
  • YouTube's retention report is the judge: "flat line" means viewers watch that part "from start to finish" (YouTube Help).

What actually holds attention in either format

  1. A first scene that names the problem. "Your team has five tools and no single view" works whether it is a cartoon or a person at a desk.
  2. One idea per scene. Mayer's coherence principle applies: strip scenes of anything that does not support the point.
  3. A working-product moment early. Show the actual interface in the first third. Animation that never shows the product leaves viewers unsure what they are buying; live action that shows only smiling people has the same problem.
  4. Pacing by information, not by seconds. Change the visual when the idea changes. If a scene runs 12 seconds because the idea needs it, fine.
  5. Voiceover that does not repeat the text. Mayer's redundancy idea suggests duplicating narration as on-screen text adds load.
  6. A call to action that matches the length. Google's guidance says to "include a call to action (CTA)" and pair the on-screen CTA with voiceover so the next step is clear.

A decision guide

Choose animation when:

  • The product is infrastructure or data work with nothing to film.
  • You expect interface changes and have source files to re-render.
  • The audience watches in feeds with sound off.

Choose live action when:

  • The buyer needs to trust a person (consulting-heavy software, health, finance).
  • A customer or team member can speak honestly about the problem.
  • The product has a physical or on-site component.

Choose a hybrid (a presenter intro, then screen recording or motion graphics) when:

  • You need both trust and clarity. It is common because the first ten seconds benefit from a human face while the middle benefits from visuals.

A fair test you can run

  1. Write one script with a single problem, three steps and one CTA.
  2. Produce the first 30 to 45 seconds in both formats (storyboard plus rough cut is enough for animation).
  3. Publish both as separate unlisted or small-audience placements to similar viewers.
  4. Compare the intro figure (YouTube defines it as "what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds"), the first dip, and the click-through to the demo page.
  5. Pick the version that holds the middle better, not just the opening.

Keep other variables identical (length, title, thumbnail, audience) or the test tells you nothing. If you have very little traffic, the numbers will be noisy; treat a small difference as inconclusive.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the format before the script. The idea should decide the format.
  • A logo animation first. It spends part of the 30-second intro window before the viewer learns anything.
  • Animation that is only decoration. If a scene could be removed without losing meaning, remove it.
  • Live action with no on-screen product. Viewers still want to see the software.
  • Ignoring sound-off viewing. A LinkedIn marketer writing for IAB UK says "79% of videos are watched on mute" on LinkedIn, and that figure comes from LinkedIn's own analysis (IAB UK). If your explainer will be seen in feeds, captions and visual clarity matter in either format.

Is animation always cheaper?

Which works better with sound off?

Either, if text, motion and captions carry the meaning. Plan for it.

How long should a SaaS explainer be?

Wistia suggests 1 to 5 minutes for explainers; shorter for ads. Let the idea decide.

Can I mix both?

Yes. A presenter opening with animated or screen sections is common.
