How often should a faceless channel re-hook viewers in a ten-minute video?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 25 November 2026 · Last updated 25 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

There is no fixed number. A sensible starting plan for a ten-minute faceless video is a small re-hook every one to two minutes and a bigger reset at each section change, then move them to where your retention graph dips. Treat the interval as a test, not a rule.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's retention report says "dips mean that viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part of your video", and "spikes appear when more viewers are watching, rewatching, or sharing those parts" (YouTube Help). Those are the places to add or move a re-hook.
  • YouTube's intro metric is the share of viewers still watching after the first 30 seconds, and the help page suggests experimenting with the first 30 seconds if it is low (same source).
  • AIR Media-Tech's 2025 data on 21,755 long-form videos found a median average percentage viewed of 30% for videos of 5 to 15 minutes, with the top quarter reaching 41% (AIR). This is one vendor's data set, so treat it as a rough guide.
  • YouTube's own blog explains that absolute retention is a share of total views, while relative retention compares a video with others of similar length (YouTube Blog).

What a re-hook is, in a voice-led video

A re-hook is a short moment that gives the viewer a new reason to keep watching: a question that the next section answers, a surprising fact, a preview of what is coming, or a change of picture or sound. In a faceless video there is no host to change expression or move, so the re-hook has to come from the script, the visuals and the sound.

It should not feel like an interruption. The best ones are part of the story: "Before we get to the fix, there is one mistake that cost this channel two months." That line raises a question and promises an answer.

How often, as a starting plan

The table is a planning aid based on common practice, not a published rule. YouTube does not state an interval.

Moment in a 10 minute videoRe-hook typeSuggested spacing
0:00 to 0:30Opening hook that confirms the title and thumbnail promiseOnce, in the first 15 to 30 seconds
Every 60 to 120 secondsSmall re-hook: a question, a teaser or a visual changeAbout 4 to 7 in ten minutes
At each section changeBigger reset: recap and next promise2 to 3 times
Around the middleA stronger reveal or exampleOnce, near 4:30 to 5:30
Final minutePayoff and one next stepOnce

Use these as a first draft, then change them using your own graph.

Step-by-step: plan and test re-hooks

  1. Check the graph of your last five videos. Find where retention drops fastest.
  2. Note the intro percentage. If fewer viewers than usual pass 30 seconds, work on the opening first.
  3. Mark the sections of the new video. Aim for three or four clear parts.
  4. Write the opening hook. Confirm the title and thumbnail promise in the first 15 to 30 seconds.
  5. Add a small re-hook every minute or two. A question, a "but there is a catch", a number to come.
  6. Add a bigger reset at each section change. Recap in one line, then promise the next part.
  7. Vary the picture. Change the visual every few seconds, such as a new image, a zoom or a graphic, so the eye keeps moving.
  8. Vary the sound. A change of music, a sound effect or a short pause can mark a new beat.
  9. Place a stronger payoff in the middle. Give a reason to stay past the halfway point.
  10. Publish, then wait one to two days. YouTube says retention data takes that long to process.
  11. Read the dips. Move or rewrite the re-hook before each dip, and compare with relative retention.
  12. Keep notes. Record the spacing you used and the result, so each video teaches the next.

Example: a ten-minute video about saving for a deposit

Example: a faceless finance channel has a ten-minute video with one hook at the start and none after. The graph shows a steep drop at 2:10 and a slow fall afterwards. In the next video, the writer plans small re-hooks at about 1:30, 3:00, 4:15, 6:00 and 7:30, such as "the third mistake is the one most people miss", and section resets at 2:30 and 6:30. Why the number is not fixed

Videos differ. A tutorial with a clear step-by-step order may need fewer re-hooks, because each step is a reason to keep going. A story with a long set-up may need more. The AIR article makes a related point: read the shape of the graph first and match it to a pattern, such as an intro cliff, a sudden cliff or a slow decline, because each has a different fix.

It also warns that benchmarks vary. Its 2025 data shows the median share of a video watched falls as videos get longer, so a ten-minute video will not hold the percentage a three-minute one does. Compare yours with videos of similar length, which is what relative retention does.

Do not over-hook

Too many teasers can annoy viewers if the answers keep slipping away. Every re-hook should be paid off within a reasonable time. If you promise "the biggest mistake in a moment", deliver it within the next minute or two. A re-hook that is never answered teaches viewers to ignore the next one.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on re-hooks and open loops?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, open loops and pacing with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. It is not faceless-specific and does not promise results.

Is there an official re-hook interval?

No. YouTube does not give one. Start with a small re-hook every one to two minutes and adjust from your graph.

What should I do first if retention is poor?

Check the intro percentage and the shape of the graph. If many viewers leave in the first 30 seconds, fix the opening before adding re-hooks.

How soon can I read retention data?

YouTube says it can take one to two days to process.

Can I re-hook with only a voice?

Yes. Use a question, a teaser, a change of picture, music or a short pause to mark a new beat.
