How do I choose licensed music for a wedding film that will go on YouTube?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Choose music from a source whose licence explicitly covers publishing the film online and client work, keep the licence with the project, and avoid chart songs unless a rights holder has agreed in writing. YouTube's Audio Library is a free, copyright-safe option; paid libraries give more variety. A song the couple loves is not a licence.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube says "Copyright holders decide how they want their music to be used on YouTube". Outcomes include ads on your video, a video that "may be muted or may be entirely unavailable", or blocking in some countries, and "your video's status could change in the future" (YouTube Help).
  • The Audio Library offers "royalty-free production music and sound effects", described as "copyright-safe". Creative Commons tracks need credit in the description; other tracks do not (YouTube Help).
  • That page also says only Audio Library music is "known to YouTube to be copyright-safe", YouTube is not responsible for "royalty-free" music from other libraries and it "can't give legal guidance" (same page).
  • YouTube's retention report treats dips as moments where "viewers are abandoning or skipping" (YouTube Help); a song that fits the film helps pacing.
  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded" (Dartmouth); music that fights the audio of vows or speeches is extraneous.

Why this matters more for wedding films

A wedding film is paid client work. If it is muted or blocked after you deliver it, the client loses a film, and you lose a reference. If it gets a claim, the revenue may go to the rights holder, and the claim can change later. A client will not distinguish between a platform problem and a licensing problem; they will see a film that does not play as promised.

There are also two layers of rights for most songs: the composition (the song) and the recording (this performance). A commercial recording of a famous song involves both, and a licence for one is not a licence for the other. That is why picking a chart song is harder than it looks.

Step 2: Read what the licence covers

Before you download, look for these points in the licence:

  1. Online publication, including YouTube, Vimeo and your website.
  2. Client work. Some licences are for personal use only.
  3. Paid promotion, if you may boost the film or use it in ads.
  4. Number of projects. Per-project or unlimited.
  5. Territory and term. Worldwide, and forever or for a period.
  6. Credit requirements.
  7. Whether the track can be registered in Content ID. Some libraries may do this; ask what happens if a claim appears and how to dispute it.

Keep a copy of the licence and the receipt with the project files. If a claim arrives, you will need them.

Step 3: Understand Audio Library limits

YouTube describes Audio Library tracks as copyright-safe and not claimed through Content ID. That is useful for YouTube. But it is not a licence for every use. If you plan to use the film elsewhere, such as in a paid ad or on a different platform, check the terms. Creative Commons tracks need attribution in the description; YouTube lets you copy the credit text from YouTube Studio. And YouTube says it cannot give legal guidance, so ask a qualified lawyer if you are unsure.

Step 4: Handle the couple's favourite song

Couples often want a specific song for the film. Be honest early. Options:

  • Use the song only in a private version (for example, a private download) if that is allowed, and keep the public YouTube version on licensed music. Check that your arrangement is legal; private use is not always permitted either, so do not assume.
  • Ask for a licence from the rights holders, which can be slow and costly.
  • Find a similar licensed track in tone and tempo.
  • Use live audio from the ceremony, such as vows, laughter and ambient sound, as the emotional centre and a licensed track as support.

This page cannot give legal advice. If the song matters, take professional advice.

Step 5: Choose music that suits the film

Once the rights are clear, choose for the film.

  • Match tempo to the cut. Faster for the party, slower for vows.
  • Leave room for speech. Lower or drop music under vows and toasts so voices stay clear.
  • Pick a clear arc. A track that builds can match a story from preparation to dance floor.
  • Avoid abrupt music changes that jolt the viewer.
  • Check length. The track should fit the film or edit cleanly.

Step 6: Test before you deliver

Upload the film as unlisted or private to YouTube and check whether any claim or restriction appears in YouTube Studio. If a restriction shows, change the music before you deliver. Wait for processing to finish; claims can take time to appear.

Step 7: Keep records and credit

Add music credits to the description where required. Save the licence, the track name and the source. If you dispute a mistaken claim, your records are your evidence.

Example: one film, two outcomes

Example: a videographer's first film uses a popular song the couple loved, uploaded as a public film. A rights holder blocks it in some countries and the couple's overseas relatives cannot watch. The videographer's next film uses a licensed library track whose licence covers client work and online publication, saves the licence, tests an unlisted upload first and uses ceremony audio for the emotional peak. Common mistakes

  • Assuming "royalty-free" means free for any use.
  • Using chart songs without a rights-holder licence.
  • Not checking client-work terms.
  • Skipping the test upload.
  • Losing the paperwork.
  • Music over vows so they cannot be heard.

FAQ

Is YouTube Audio Library music safe for wedding films?

YouTube describes it as copyright-safe on YouTube and not claimed through Content ID. Check credit rules and any use beyond YouTube.

Can I use the couple's favourite chart song?

Not safely without a licence from the rights holders. A standard library licence usually does not cover it.

What happens if a song is claimed?

The rights holder may monetise the video, mute it or block it, and the status can change later.

Do I need to keep the licence?

Yes. Keep it with the project and use it if you need to dispute a claim.
