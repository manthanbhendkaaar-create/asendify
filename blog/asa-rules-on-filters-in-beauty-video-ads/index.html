What does the ASA say about filters in beauty and skincare video ads?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 23 November 2026 · Last updated 23 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

The UK's Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) says filters in ads are not a problem in themselves, but they are likely to mislead if they exaggerate what the advertised product does. So a skin-smoothing or tanning filter on a foundation, skincare or fake-tan video ad is likely to break the rules, while a colour filter on an ad for headphones probably is not. A disclaimer is unlikely to fix an exaggerated impression.

Key takeaways

  • CAP's guidance says "The use of filters in ads is not inherently problematic" but "is likely to become an issue if a filter exaggerates the efficacy of the product" (ASA/CAP, February 2021).
  • In the Skinny Tan and Tanologist rulings, filters that darkened influencers' skin were misleading because they were "directly relevant to the performance of the products being advertised".
  • In the BPerfect ruling (July 2021), a smoothing filter on Instagram stories for a make-up palette "gave a misleading impression about the performance capabilities of the product", breaching CAP Code rules 3.1 and 3.11 (ASA ruling).
  • The ASA's 2025 beauty guidance adds that "any disclaimer is likely to counteract the overall impression of the ad", and before-and-after images "need to be genuine" with signed and dated proof (ASA, August 2025).
  • The advertiser (the brand) is responsible, even when an influencer or agency made the video.

Is the filter a problem? A quick test

The question to ask: would a viewer reasonably think the look they see is what the product will do for them? If yes, and a filter changed that look, it is risky.

What this means for editors and agencies

Most UK beauty video ads are now UGC-style: a creator applying a product to camera. That raises three editing risks:

  1. Filters applied at capture. Creators often film with phone beauty modes or app filters switched on. The ASA notes that because filters are usually applied at creation, "before" material proving the filter didn't mislead is unlikely to exist. So the problem has to be stopped at the brief, not fixed in the edit.
  2. Retouching in post. Smoothing skin, removing blemishes or sharpening lashes on a skincare or make-up ad raises the same issue as a filter.
  3. Before-and-after sequences. These are popular hooks, but they must be genuine, consistent in lighting and angle, and backed by proof.

A compliant workflow for beauty video ads

StageWhat to do
BriefWrite "no beauty filters, beauty mode or face retouching" into the creator brief and contract
CaptureAsk creators to film with the phone's beauty mode off, in plain light; ask for the raw file
EditColour-correct the whole scene for exposure and white balance only; no skin smoothing on the face
Before-and-afterSame light, angle and camera; keep dated originals and the creator's signed confirmation
Captions and textMake claims only you can substantiate; no "flawless in seconds" unless proven

Does this apply outside the UK?

The ASA covers ads aimed at UK consumers. Other countries have their own rules, and platforms have their own policies. If a client runs the same beauty ad in several countries, the UK position is a useful baseline: avoid filters that change what the product appears to do.

This is general information, not legal advice. For borderline cases, CAP's free Copy Advice service or a lawyer can review the ad before it runs.

FAQ

Is there a playbook on hooks for UGC beauty video ads?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers hooks, pacing and pattern interrupts with templates and a 30-day plan, for £1,720 one-time. CAP's free guidance covers the rules.

Can I use a filter if I say "filter used" on screen?

Probably not, if it exaggerates the product. The ASA says a disclaimer is likely to contradict the overall impression rather than fix it.

Is colour grading the same as a filter?

Not necessarily. Correcting exposure and white balance across a scene is normal. Smoothing skin or changing skin tone in a beauty ad is the risk.

Who gets in trouble, the brand or the influencer?

The ASA holds the advertiser responsible, and rulings have named both brand and influencer.

Does this apply to organic influencer posts?

If the post is paid for or the brand controls it, the ASA treats it as an ad, so yes.
