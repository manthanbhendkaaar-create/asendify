How loud should the music bed be under a voiceover?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 24 November 2026 · Last updated 24 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Set the voice as the loudest element and the music well below it, then duck the music a few decibels further while the narrator speaks. A common starting point from mixing practice is music roughly 15 to 20 dB below the voice during speech, rising a little in gaps, but no source I opened fixes one correct ratio. Check the result on phone speakers and headphones, and use a loudness meter for the overall level.

Key takeaways

  • The EBU's supplement R 128 s4 says dialogue loudness is "the average loudness of the speech component of a programme" and that, for broadcast cinematic content, programme loudness should by default be normalised to "−23.0 LUFS" (EBU R 128 s4). That is a broadcast standard for films and drama, not a rule for online video, but it shows that speech level and overall loudness are measured separately.
  • The same document says dialogue loudness near the target can give "a better balance to the level of speech", especially around advertisement breaks (EBU R 128 s4).
  • Mayer's coherence principle says "learning is more effective when extraneous information and content is excluded" (Dartmouth). Loud music competing with narration is extraneous load.
  • Mayer's modality principle favours "voiceover with images or animation" (Dartmouth), which makes the voice the carrying channel in many explainers. It must stay clear.

What "how loud" means

There are three separate levels to keep apart:

  1. Voice level. How loud the narration is relative to your final output.
  2. Music level during speech. How far below the voice the bed sits when someone is talking.
  3. Overall loudness. The whole mix, usually measured in LUFS, which platforms may adjust.

Fix them in that order. Get the voice right first, set the music relative to it, then check the overall loudness.

Starting points by situation

SituationMusic relative to voice during speech (practice guide)Notes
Calm narration, explainerAbout 18 to 22 dB belowMusic is felt more than heard
Energetic ad, voice and music both presentAbout 12 to 18 dB belowDuck during key phrases
Documentary with sparse narrationAbout 15 to 20 dB below, rising in gapsLet music carry pauses
Meditation or soft contentAbout 10 to 15 dB belowMusic is part of the experience
Gap between sentencesRaise 3 to 6 dBSmooth, not abrupt

These are working values from mixing practice. Different music (a dense track versus a sparse piano) needs different offsets, so listen, do not just trust the numbers.

A mixing process

  1. Clean the voice first. Remove noise, apply light compression and a gentle EQ so speech is steady. Then set the voice at a consistent level.
  2. Pick music with space. Tracks with busy midrange, strong vocals or loud hi-hats compete with speech. Instrumental, sparse tracks sit better.
  3. Place the music at a low starting level. Begin well below the voice and raise it until you can just feel the mood under the words.
  4. Add ducking. Use automation or a sidechain compressor so music dips a few decibels when the voice is present and rises in pauses. Keep the movement smooth, with attack and release times that do not "pump".
  5. EQ a pocket for the voice. Reduce the music slightly in the speech midrange so the voice sits clearly.
  6. Check on bad speakers. Phone speakers, laptop speakers, earbuds, and a car if possible. Speech should be clear on all.
  7. Check the whole mix with a meter. Use a loudness meter to see the overall level and compare with your platform's guidance.
  8. Listen at a low volume. If you can follow every word at low volume, the balance is likely good.

Why a fixed number can mislead

Dense or bright music masks speech more than quiet piano does at the same level. A deep male voice and a thin voice differ in how easily they cut through. And listeners differ: many watch on phones in noisy places. The EBU document itself distinguishes dialogue loudness from programme loudness, which implies speech balance cannot be inferred from the overall figure alone. So treat any decibel range as a starting point.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing on studio monitors only. Phone listeners lose the voice.
  • Music at the same level for the whole video. Without ducking, speech competes.
  • Pumping sidechain. Obvious level jumps distract.
  • Loud intro music right before narration. Bring it down before the first word.
  • Using a track with vocals under narration. Two voices compete.
  • Normalising the music and the voice separately. Decide the balance, then check the total.

Example: a 4-minute explainer voiceover

Example: an editor mixes a calm explainer with a sparse piano track. The first mix has the music at the same level through the whole video, and on a phone the narrator's softer words are lost. The editor lowers the music by a few decibels, adds a light ducking of about 4 dB during speech, raises the bed slightly in the pauses between sections, and cuts a small dip in the music's midrange. The voice is then clear on laptop and phone speakers. This is an illustrative scenario, not a measured result. The editor also checks the final loudness on a meter and compares with other videos on the channel for consistency.

How to check retention effects

If viewers drop at a particular point, check whether audio quality changes there, such as music swelling over speech. YouTube's retention report says dips mean "viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part" (YouTube Help). It will not tell you why, so listen back at that time with phone speakers.

Checklist

  • Voice clean and steady first.
  • Music chosen for space, no competing vocals.
  • Music starts well below the voice.
  • Ducking is smooth.
  • Checked on phone and headphones.
  • Overall loudness checked with a meter.

Is there one correct dB difference?

Do I need ducking?

Usually yes, for narration over music.
