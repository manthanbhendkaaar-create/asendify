What is a podcast cold open, and how do you make one?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 7 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

A podcast cold open is a short clip of the episode's most gripping moment, played at 0:00 before the theme music, host welcome or sponsor read. It gives a new listener a reason to stay before any housekeeping starts. To make one, find a 15 to 60 second moment that raises a question the episode answers, cut it cleanly, then go straight into the episode.

Key takeaways

  • A cold open borrows a strong moment from later in the episode and plays it first, before any intro.
  • Spotify tells creators that "listeners have a tendency to drop off early", so the start has to earn attention fast.
  • The best cold opens raise a question rather than answer it, which uses the curiosity "gap" psychologists describe.
  • Keep it short: most work at 15 to 60 seconds, and the episode should start within a few seconds of it ending.
  • Check the result in Spotify, Apple and YouTube analytics, not by feel.

What a cold open is (and is not)

The term comes from television: the scene before the opening titles. In a podcast, it is the same idea. Instead of starting with "Welcome back to the show, I'm..." you start with the guest's best line, a tense moment in the story, or a surprising claim that comes up at minute 34.

It is not a trailer for the whole show, and it is not a summary. A summary tells the listener what they will hear. A cold open makes them want to hear it. Apple's guide to making a podcast trailer recommends building "tension or curiosity", for example with "an unanswered question by a guest", and the same advice works at the top of an episode (Apple Podcasts for Creators).

Why the first minute matters so much

New listeners leave early. Spotify's own guide to episode performance says "listeners have a tendency to drop off early, so it's crucial to capture people's attention as quickly as possible", and that "getting into the heart of your episode as quickly as you can is a great way to keep up retention" (Spotify for Creators).

Video podcasts make this sharper. On YouTube, the "intro" key moment in Studio shows the percentage of viewers still watching after the first 30 seconds, and YouTube suggests you "modify the first 30 seconds" and try different styles if it is low (YouTube Help). A long welcome and a sponsor read in those 30 seconds is the most common reason a good episode loses people before it starts.

Why a question beats a highlight

The strongest cold opens end on something unresolved. Carnegie Mellon economist George Loewenstein's 1994 review of curiosity argued that curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge", and that such gaps "produce the feeling of deprivation labeled curiosity" (quoted by PBS NOVA). A cold open that stops just before the answer creates that gap. A cold open that plays the punchline gives the listener the payoff and no reason to stay.

How to make a podcast cold open, step by step

  1. Mark candidates while you edit. Note any moment where you leaned in: a strong opinion, a reveal, laughter, a sharp disagreement, a number nobody expected.
  2. Choose the one that raises a question. Ask: "If I heard only this, what would I want to know next?" If the answer is "nothing", pick another.
  3. Cut tight. Start on the first word that matters, not on "So, yeah...". End just before the answer, or on the line that makes the listener ask why.
  4. Keep it 15 to 60 seconds. Long enough to make sense, short enough that the episode starts while curiosity is high.
  5. Bridge into the episode fast. A short sting or a single line ("That's later. First, here's how it started.") then straight into content. Move the long intro, housekeeping and calls to action later.
  6. Pay it off. The moment must appear in full later in the episode. A cold open that is never resolved teaches listeners not to trust your openings.
  7. Match the title and thumbnail. YouTube notes that a high intro score can mean the first 30 seconds "matched the viewer's expectation of the video's thumbnail and title". The cold open should deliver on what the packaging promised.

Example: re-cutting an interview episode

Example: a 62-minute business interview starts with 40 seconds of theme music and welcome, 90 seconds of sponsor read, then two minutes of small talk. Real content begins at about 4:10. In the re-cut:

  • 0:00 to 0:35: the guest at minute 41 saying they lost their biggest client in one email, stopping before what the email said.
  • 0:35 to 0:45: a short sting and one line from the host: "We'll get to that email. First, how did a two-person agency land that client at all?"
  • 0:45 onwards: the first real question.
  • The sponsor read moves to a natural break around minute 20, with a lead-in line.

The listener now hears a reason to stay in the first 35 seconds instead of waiting four minutes for one. Compare the YouTube intro percentage and the Spotify retention curve for this episode with your last five before deciding it worked.

Mistakes that ruin a cold open

  • Giving away the answer. The clip should end before the payoff.
  • Using a moment that needs context. If the listener can't follow it without knowing who "he" is, it confuses instead of hooks.
  • Following it with a long intro. The curiosity fades if two minutes of music, welcome and adverts follow.
  • Using the same type every week. Rotate between questions, claims and story moments so it doesn't feel like a formula.
  • Misleading. If the cold open suggests drama the episode doesn't have, people leave and stop trusting the show.

How to check if it worked

  • YouTube: in Studio, look at the intro key moment (viewers left after 30 seconds) and the audience retention graph for dips at 0:30 to 2:00.
  • Spotify: Spotify says "steep and sudden changes in retention" are the evidence to act on; compare the first five minutes of the curve before and after you start using cold opens.
  • Apple Podcasts: Apple's analytics show average consumption, "a percent average indicating how much of the episode users played per device" (Apple Podcasts for Creators).

Change one thing at a time, and judge over several episodes, because guests and topics vary.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches cold opens for podcasts?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook covers the cold open rule and open loops and applies them to podcasts and clips. Spotify's and Apple's free creator guides are good starting points too.

How long should a podcast cold open be?

Usually 15 to 60 seconds. It needs to make sense on its own and end before curiosity fades.

Should the cold open come before the theme music?

Yes. That is what makes it "cold": the strongest moment plays first, then a short sting or intro, then the episode.

Can I use a cold open on every episode?

Yes, but rotate the type (a question, a claim, a story moment) so it doesn't feel like a formula.

Does a cold open work for audio-only listeners?

Yes. Spotify's guidance about early drop-off applies to audio shows too; the cold open just needs clean audio that makes sense without pictures.

Where should the sponsor read go instead?

Later, at a natural break, with a lead-in line. Spotify notes ad slots often show as dips, and a sudden steep drop means the lead-in needs work.
