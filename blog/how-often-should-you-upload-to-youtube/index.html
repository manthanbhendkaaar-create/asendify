How often should you upload to YouTube?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Upload as often as you can keep up for months without the videos getting worse. For most solo creators that means one good long video a week, or every two weeks, plus Shorts if they fit. Large studies link more frequent uploading with faster channel growth, but that is correlation, and YouTube's own advice stresses a consistent, sustainable schedule over a punishing one.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube's own guidance: "A consistent, sustainable release schedule is critical."
  • A vidIQ study of 10.2 million channels found median monthly view growth rose from 0.53% (under 1 upload a month) to 2.18% (12+ a month), but it "shows correlation, not causation."
  • The same study warns that "frequency cannot compensate for videos viewers do not want to watch."
  • Back in 2011, YouTube's Creator Playbook said a typical frequency among successful creators was "a video a week".
  • Choose the highest frequency you can sustain at your current quality, then improve one thing per video.

What YouTube says

YouTube's help page on upload schedules asks creators: "How often do you upload per week/month? Is this frequency sustainable over the long term?" It adds that "a consistent, sustainable release schedule is critical", and asks whether to focus on "low-frequency, high-production-value content" (YouTube Help).

Its older Creator Playbook post put it this way: "Releasing videos on a recurring schedule helps build a structure to your show that an audience can rely on," noting that "a typical frequency we've seen among successful YouTubers is a video a week" (YouTube Official Blog, 2011). Platforms change, but the principle of a schedule viewers can rely on still holds.

In other words, channels that are already growing can afford to upload more. Frequency helps when each video is still worth watching.

Long videos vs Shorts

Long videos and Shorts have different costs. A 15-minute researched video might take 10 to 30 hours to make; a Short cut from it might take one. Many creators run two schedules:

  • Long-form: weekly or fortnightly, at the quality your audience expects.
  • Shorts: several a week, often cut from long videos, each pointing back to the full video.

Don't let Shorts eat the time that makes your long videos good.

How to choose your schedule

  1. Time one full video. Track every hour from idea to upload: research, scripting, filming, editing, thumbnail, title.
  2. Check your real weekly hours. Be honest about work, family and rest.
  3. Divide. If a video takes 12 hours and you have 8 spare hours a week, weekly isn't sustainable. Every two weeks is.
  4. Build a buffer. Have one or two videos finished before you announce a schedule.
  5. Tell viewers. Put the schedule in your channel banner or description so expectations are clear.
  6. Review after eight weeks. If quality slipped, slow down. If you have spare time, add Shorts first.

Example: picking a realistic cadence

Example (a hypothetical creator, to show the maths): a part-time creator has about 10 free hours a week. A finished long video takes 14 hours. Shorts take about 45 minutes each.

She chooses a long video every second Thursday and three Shorts a week, with a two-video buffer. After two months she reviews retention and click-through rate before deciding whether to speed up.

Common mistakes

  • Daily uploads that get worse. Viewers notice when titles, thumbnails and edits slip.
  • Long silences. Disappearing for months without warning makes returning harder.
  • Uploading to "feed the algorithm". The study above says frequency can't save videos viewers don't want.
  • Ignoring the packaging. A great video uploaded on time still fails with a weak title or thumbnail.
  • No buffer. One busy week and the schedule breaks.

What to do when you need a break

Breaks are normal, and burnout makes every video worse. Plan them like part of the schedule:

  • Film ahead. Finish two or three videos before a holiday so the schedule keeps running.
  • Tell viewers. A community post or a line at the end of a video ("back on the 14th") keeps expectations clear.
  • Use the gap. Re-edit an old video with weak retention, refresh titles and thumbnails, or cut Shorts from your back catalogue.
  • Come back steadily. Return to your normal rhythm rather than a burst of uploads that you can't keep up.

How consistency shows up on your channel

New visitors judge your channel from your videos page: how recent the last upload is, how regular the gaps are and whether titles and thumbnails look like a series. Gaps of several months, or a burst of uploads followed by silence, make it harder to convince someone to subscribe.

FAQ

Is there a free tool that checks how consistently I upload?

Yes. The free Asendify YouTube Channel Roast checks upload consistency along with titles and thumbnails and gives three fixes.

Does uploading more often help on YouTube?

Channels that upload more often tend to grow faster, but studies show correlation, not causation. Quality still decides whether viewers watch.

Is one video a week enough?

For most solo creators, yes. It's a common, sustainable rhythm.

Does YouTube punish you for not uploading?

YouTube's guidance focuses on a consistent, sustainable schedule. Long gaps make it harder to keep viewers' habits, but quality matters more than frequency.

Should Shorts count towards my schedule?

Treat them as a separate schedule so they don't take time from long videos.

What day should I upload?

When your audience is online. Check the "When your viewers are on YouTube" report in YouTube Studio.
