How do you make a charity appeal video for a fundraising gala that holds the room?

By Manthan Bhendkar · 5 min read · Published 22 November 2026 · Last updated 22 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Build the video around one real person's story, open on a moment that makes the room go quiet, and keep it to roughly two to four minutes so it ends while attention is high. Get clear, informed consent from anyone identifiable, keep every claim accurate, and end on one specific ask that leads straight into the live pledge moment.

Key takeaways

  • In a gala room, the video competes with conversation, wine and phones. A strong first moment matters as much as it does online.
  • One person's story usually holds attention better than a list of programmes and figures.
  • The UK Code of Fundraising Practice says you must get "clear and informed permission from any people who could be identified from a case study."
  • Case studies must be representative of real situations, and you must not use images people may find shocking without warnings.
  • End with a single, concrete ask that the host repeats live.

Structure that works in a room

SectionTimePurpose
Cold open10-20 sOne striking line or image from the person at the centre of the story
The problem30-45 sWhat life was like, in their words
The turning point30-60 sWhat the charity did, shown not listed
Today30-45 sWhere they are now, honestly
Scale15-20 sHow many others are in the same position (only figures you can source)
The ask15-20 sOne amount, one thing it pays for, then hand to the host

Open on a person, not a logo

The most common mistake in gala films is opening with the charity logo, a drone shot of the building and a narrator listing services. By the time the story starts, the room has gone back to talking. Open instead on a short line from the person at the centre: something specific they said, or a moment from their day.

Google's guidance on the ABCDs of effective video ads is written for online ads, but its advice carries over: "Hook and sustain attention with an immersive story" and "Help people think or feel something." Its "Direction" point, "Say what you'd like viewers to do", is the pledge ask at the end.

The rules you must follow

Fundraising communications in the UK fall under the Code of Fundraising Practice (section 8). Key standards for appeal videos:

  • Accuracy. "You must make sure information you include in your fundraising communications... is accurate."
  • Representative case studies. "You must be able to show that all case studies are representative of real situations", and any changed details must be made clear.
  • Consent. Get clear and informed permission from anyone who could be identified. If the person has died, take reasonable steps to get permission from their estate.
  • No shock without warning. Don't use claims or images people may find shocking; if you do, include warnings.
  • No guilt pressure. The Code says materials must not encourage donations based on guilt or embarrassment.

Many fundraising communications are also advertising and fall under the ASA's CAP and BCAP codes, as the Fundraising Regulator notes.

Make it work in a noisy room

  • Captions. Gala audio is often poor and rooms are noisy. Burn in captions. The W3C's captions guidance notes captions help people who are Deaf or hard of hearing and people who process written information better than audio.
  • Check the venue screen and sound in a technical rehearsal, at the volume the room will be at.
  • Dim the lights and pause service. Agree with the venue that waiting staff hold back while the film plays.
  • Short is kind. A four-minute film feels long in a ballroom. If the story needs more, cut a longer version for the website.

Example: a 3-minute appeal for a youth mentoring charity

Example: a charity raising money for mentoring places at its annual dinner.

  • Cold open (12 s): a 19-year-old says, "The first person who asked what I wanted to do was my mentor." Black screen, title.
  • Problem (40 s): she describes leaving school with no plan, filmed at home, with her written consent and approval of the final cut.
  • Turning point (50 s): footage of a mentoring session; her mentor talks briefly.
  • Today (40 s): she has started an apprenticeship.
  • Scale (15 s): one sourced figure on young people not in education, employment or training, with the source shown on screen.
  • Ask (20 s): "£600 funds a year of mentoring for one young person. Tonight we're aiming for 50 places." The film ends; the host repeats the ask and pledge cards go round.

Total: just under three minutes, one story, one number, one ask.

After the gala

Cut a 30-60 second vertical version for social, with captions, and a full version for the website donate page. Check the consent you collected covers online use too, not just the event screening. Track donations from the night against the ask so next year's film can be judged on results, not applause.

Common mistakes

  • Opening on the logo and a narrator.
  • Several stories competing for attention.
  • Unsourced statistics.
  • Consent that covers the gala but not later online use.
  • No clear ask, or an ask that doesn't match the pledge mechanism in the room.

FAQ

Is there a book that teaches cold opens and story pacing for short films?

Yes. Asendify's Retention Editing Playbook teaches cold opens, open loops and pacing for YouTube, which carries over to appeal films. For fundraising rules, use the Fundraising Regulator's free Code.

How long should a gala appeal video be?

Roughly two to four minutes works in most rooms. Make a longer version for the website if the story needs it.

Do I need consent from people in a charity appeal video?

Yes, if they could be identified. The Code of Fundraising Practice requires clear and informed permission.

Can I change names or details to protect someone?

Yes, but the Code says changed details must be made clear and the case study must still be representative of real situations.

Should the video include statistics?

One or two sourced figures can show scale. Keep them accurate and show the source.

Should we use a celebrity narrator?

Only if it helps the story. The person whose life changed is usually more convincing than a famous voice.
