How should an animated explainer end: end screen, call to action and what to ask viewers to do?

By the Asendify editorial team, led by Manthan Bhendkar · 6 min read · Published 25 November 2026 · Last updated 25 November 2026

Disclosure: Asendify publishes this page and makes some of the options mentioned here; ours are named as ours. Every option is described from its own official page, and prices and features can change, so check before you buy.

Ask for one next step, say it in your own words before the final seconds, and leave the last 5 to 20 seconds free for YouTube's end screen elements. More than one ask splits attention, and a call to action that appears only on the end screen is missed by everyone who left earlier.

Key takeaways

  • YouTube says end screens can be added to "the last 5-20 seconds of a video" and that the video "has to be at least 25 seconds long to have an end screen" (YouTube Help).
  • You can add "up to four elements to your end screen for videos with standard 16:9 aspect ratio" (YouTube Help). Choices include video, playlist, subscribe and channel; the link option needs YouTube Partner Programme membership.
  • Instagram's retention chart always slopes down, and "the flatter the line, the more engaged your audience is" (Social Media Today). The same logic holds on YouTube: many viewers will be gone before your closing seconds.
  • Descript advises that clips should be "short, self-contained moments" (Descript), a useful test for the closing beat of an explainer too.

What an explainer ending has to do

An animated explainer usually solves one problem for the viewer. The ending should turn that into one action: watch the next video, subscribe, read a guide or try the product. The ending is also where animation timing goes wrong. Teams often finish the story, add a logo sting, a voiceover call to action and a long music tail, and then place end screen boxes over the sting.

YouTube's rules help you plan. The end screen lives in the last 5 to 20 seconds, holds up to four elements and needs a video of at least 25 seconds. So build a closing section that is deliberately empty enough for those elements: a calm background, no important text under the boxes and a voiceover that has already said the ask.

Step-by-step: build the closing 20 seconds

  1. Choose one action. Write it in a single sentence before you animate anything.
  2. Say the ask earlier than the end. Put a spoken call to action about 10 to 25 seconds before the end, while attention is still there.
  3. Close the story first. Give the answer or summary, then ask. Do not ask in the middle of an explanation.
  4. Reserve the end screen area. Plan 10 to 20 seconds with a quiet background and no key words in the zones where boxes will go.
  5. Check the minimum length. Under 25 seconds, YouTube will not allow an end screen.
  6. Use up to four elements, not always four. One video and a subscribe button is often enough.
  7. Pick the video element on purpose. Choose the next logical explainer, and mention it in the voiceover.
  8. Match the animation to the layout. Hold the final frame or loop a simple background so nothing important moves under a box.
  9. Keep the sting short. A two-second logo is plenty; long stings push viewers out.
  10. Add the ask in the description too. First line of the description repeats the next step with a link.
  11. Test on a phone. End screen elements are small; check they do not cover text or characters.
  12. Review the graph. Look at what share of viewers reach the last 20 seconds before you judge the end screen.

The call to action should sound like the explainer

If the explainer has been warm and plain, a loud "smash the like button" ending feels off. Match the voice. A good ending for an educational explainer might be: "That is how X works. If you want the next step, the video on screen shows how Y fits in." It is one ask, in the same tone, and it points to the element the viewer can click.

Avoid asking for everything: like, comment, subscribe, share and visit the website. A viewer who hears five requests tends to do none of them. If you want comments, ask a specific question instead of a generic request.

Example: a 3-minute animated explainer

Example: a studio's explainer on how a heat pump works ends with 30 seconds of recap, logo sting, a voiceover asking viewers to subscribe, like and visit the site, and music playing out. In the revised version, the recap ends at 2:35 with a spoken line, "If you want to know what it costs to run, the next video covers that." The final 15 seconds show a plain background with a video element and a subscribe element on screen; the logo sting is two seconds. The studio compares what share of viewers reach the final 20 seconds, and clicks on the end screen, across the old and new endings. This scenario uses invented numbers and is not a measured result.

If your explainer is under 25 seconds

Very short explainers cannot have an end screen, so rely on a spoken call to action, a pinned comment and the description. For Shorts and vertical video, use the on-screen interface instead of planning for boxes.

When to skip the call to action

If the video is a pure brand piece with no next step, a clean ending can be right. If the explainer will be embedded on a site, where YouTube end screens do not apply, build the ask into the page around it. Keep the ask honest and do not promise things the next video does not deliver.

FAQ

How long is a YouTube end screen?

It can run in the last 5 to 20 seconds, and the video must be at least 25 seconds long, per YouTube Help.

How many elements can I add?

Up to four on a standard 16:9 video, per YouTube Help.

Can I add a link?

The link element needs YouTube Partner Programme membership.

Should I ask for likes and subscribes?

Ask for one thing. A subscribe element is fine alongside a video, with a reason to subscribe.
